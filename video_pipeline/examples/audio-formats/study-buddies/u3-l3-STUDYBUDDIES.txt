# U3-L3 STUDY BUDDIES SCRIPT (Fish Audio) — Maya + Jay cramming the night before the AP exam
# Same verified facts. Retrieval practice disguised as hanging out. Jay gets one thing wrong; Maya catches it.

Maya: (energetic) Okay, APUSH exam tomorrow. Unit 3, lesson 3.3 — taxation without representation. Quiz me.
Jay: (casual) Alright. Why does Britain start taxing the colonies after 1763?
Maya: Because they won the war and went broke doing it. National debt doubled, empire's huge, treasury's empty. So Parliament looks at the colonies like... "you guys barely paid for your own defense, chip in."
Jay: (impressed) Nailed it. Okay, what was the deal before? Like, why were the colonists so mad about the change?
Maya: Salutary neglect! For decades Britain passed trade laws and then just... didn't enforce them. Colonies ran their own stuff, taxed themselves. Then 1763 — Britain draws the Proclamation Line down the Appalachians, no settling past it. Party's over.
Jay: Right, right. Okay, the big vocab term: virtual versus actual representation. Define both.
Maya: Virtual representation is Parliament's argument — most people in Britain can't vote either, but Parliament speaks for the whole empire, so it can tax the whole empire. Actual representation is the colonist comeback — only our own elected assemblies can tax us, because only they answer to us.
Jay: (playful) So it's not about the money, it's about...
Maya: Who rules! That's the thesis statement. Write that down.
Jay: Okay, acts in order. Go.
Maya: Sugar Act, 1764 — first law actually meant to raise revenue, not just regulate trade. Stamp Act, 1765 — direct tax on paper. Legal docs, newspapers, pamphlets...
Jay: Playing cards and dice!
Maya: (laughs) Yes! Playing cards and dice. Couldn't shuffle without paying the king.
Jay: And the colonists freak out. Sons of Liberty form. How many colonies sent delegates to the Stamp Act Congress?
Maya: Nine. And Parliament repeals the Stamp Act in 1766 — but same day, Declaratory Act, claiming power "in all cases whatsoever."
Jay: Wait, so they repealed it AND asserted total power? On the same day?
Maya: Same day. Gave up the tax, kept the principle. Classic Britain.
Jay: (confident) Townshend Acts were 1765, right? Taxed glass, lead, paint, paper, tea—
Maya: (catching) Nope — 1767! 1765 was the Stamp Act. Townshend is '67. Glass, lead, paint, paper, tea — plus the new customs boards and juryless courts.
Jay: (sheepish) Right, '67. My bad. And the boycotts come back even bigger — Daughters of Liberty spinning homespun cloth so nobody buys British textiles.
Maya: Then Parliament repeals most of the Townshend duties in 1770 but keeps the tea tax just to prove the point. Same year — March — Boston Massacre. Soldiers fire into a crowd, five dead.
Jay: And Paul Revere's engraving turns it into—
Maya: Propaganda overnight. In a war of ideas, that's a weapon.
Jay: Okay, best part. Tea Act, 1773. The twist?
Maya: The tea got cheaper! East India Company monopoly, lower prices. And the colonists destroyed it anyway.
Jay: (incredulous) 342 chests! December 16, 1773! Some of them dressed as Mohawks! Over CHEAPER tea!
Maya: (laughs) Because buying it meant accepting Parliament's right to tax them. Principle beat price. That's the line.
Jay: Then the Intolerable Acts — harbor closed till the tea's paid for, Massachusetts loses self-government.
Maya: Coercive Acts, officially. Parliament's plan: isolate the radicals.
Jay: Backfires completely. Twelve colonies send delegates to the First Continental Congress — Philadelphia, September 1774.
Maya: So the whole chain: war debt, taxes, resistance, harsher measures, wider resistance.
Jay: Every time. That spiral goes straight to Lexington and Concord.
Maya: (warm) We are so passing this exam.
Jay: (laughs) One lesson down. Now do 3.4.
