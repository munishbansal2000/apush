# U3-L3 STORY MODE SCRIPT (Fish Audio) — single cinematic narrator (Marcus, Deep)
# Same verified facts. Told as a thriller: open at the harbor, flash back, return.

Narrator: (dramatic, low) December 16th, 1773. Boston Harbor. Night. Three ships sit at anchor, heavy with tea. On the docks, men wrap blankets around their shoulders — some have darkened their faces, dressed as Mohawks. They've been waiting for this signal for weeks. (pause) What happens in the next three hours will be called vandalism by some... and the birth of a nation by others. (beat) To understand why men would destroy a fortune in tea, you have to go back ten years.
Narrator: (building) 1763. Britain has just won the biggest war of the century. The maps are redrawn, the empire is the largest on earth — and the treasury is empty. The national debt has doubled. Across the Atlantic sit thirteen colonies that barely paid a farthing toward their own defense. Parliament does the math. (dry) The colonies can help pay.
Narrator: (conversational) But here's the thing — for decades, Britain had barely governed the colonies at all. Salutary neglect, historians call it. London passed trade laws and looked the other way. The colonies ran their own affairs, taxed themselves, lived free in all the ways that mattered. Everybody was happy. (pause) Until 1763, when Britain drew a line down the Appalachian Mountains — no settlement past this point — and the old arrangement died overnight.
Narrator: (intense) Then came the question that would split an empire. Parliament said: you are virtually represented. Most people in Britain can't vote either — yet Parliament speaks for the whole empire, so Parliament can tax the whole empire. The colonists answered: only our own elected assemblies can tax us, because only they answer to us. Actual representation — or nothing. (pause) It was no longer about money. It was about who gets to rule.
Narrator: (quickening) 1764: the Sugar Act — the first law designed to raise colonial revenue, not just regulate trade. 1765: the Stamp Act — a direct tax on nearly every piece of paper. Legal documents. Newspapers. (darkly amused) Playing cards and dice. The colonies erupted. The Sons of Liberty were born. Nine colonies sent delegates to the Stamp Act Congress. Parliament blinked — repealed the Stamp Act in 1766. (beat) And on the very same day, passed the Declaratory Act, claiming power over the colonies in all cases whatsoever. They surrendered the tax. They kept the principle.
Narrator: (driving) 1767: the Townshend Acts. Duties on glass, lead, paint, paper, and tea — plus customs boards and juryless courts to squeeze out every penny. The boycotts roared back, bigger than before. The Daughters of Liberty spun homespun cloth so their families would never need British textiles. In 1770, Parliament repealed most of the duties... but kept the tax on tea. Just to prove the point. (ominous) And in March of that same year, British soldiers fired into a Boston crowd. Five colonists fell dead in the snow. Paul Revere's engraving carried the scene across the colonies overnight — and in a war of ideas, propaganda is a weapon.
Narrator: (slow, deliberate) 1773. Parliament passed the Tea Act, handing the British East India Company a monopoly on colonial tea. And here is the twist the history books love: the tea got cheaper. Cheaper! Any sensible merchant would have celebrated. (pause) Instead, on the night of December 16th, the Sons of Liberty rowed out to those three ships and dumped 342 chests of tea into Boston Harbor. Because buying it — even cheap — meant accepting Parliament's right to tax them. (emphatic) Principle had beaten price.
Narrator: (cold) Parliament was furious. The Coercive Acts slammed down — the colonists called them the Intolerable Acts. Boston's harbor was closed until every leaf was paid for. Massachusetts was stripped of its self-government. London meant to isolate the radicals. (beat) Instead, twelve colonies sent delegates to the First Continental Congress in Philadelphia, September 1774. They stood with Massachusetts. Every crackdown had made the resistance wider.
Narrator: (quiet, final) War debt. Then taxes. Then resistance. Then harsher measures. Then wider resistance. (pause) That spiral runs straight to Lexington and Concord — to the shot heard round the world. And it started here: on a cold December night, with tea in the water and an empire about to lose a continent.
