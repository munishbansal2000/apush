Why did the English colonies split into three regions that looked, worked, and worshiped so differently — and who got a voice in each one? This lesson answers that question. The answer is that the land, the crop, and the founding reason shaped each region, and each region decided for itself who counted.

## Tobacco pays the bills

Tobacco made Virginia pay, and it shaped everything about the Chesapeake. The first shipments left in 1617, and by 1700 the colonies were sending more than 35 million pounds a year to England. By 1750 tobacco was about three-quarters of the Chesapeake's exports and about a third of all British North American exports. **Tobacco** was the region's **staple crop** — one crop carried a whole region.

Tobacco also exhausted the soil. A field that grew tobacco for a few years could grow little else, so planters kept clearing new land and spreading out along the rivers. The Chesapeake became a scatter of plantations, not towns — and the hunger for fresh land kept pushing settlers toward Native peoples' ground.

Jamestown and Massachusetts Bay show how much motive mattered. Jamestown began with single men chasing riches, who died in droves and built little. Massachusetts Bay began with families building towns, who stayed and grew. The same ocean, the same century — but one colony was a camp and the other a home.

The work was done by bound labor. Indentured servants signed away years of their lives for passage, and nearly half died before their terms ended. The crop that paid for Virginia was grown by people who had little say in their own lives — a fact the next lesson takes up in full.

## Who gets a voice?

New England governed itself in **town meetings**, where the town's voters gathered to decide local business. But the **franchise** was narrow: only male church members could vote, and most men in Massachusetts Bay never joined the church. The town meeting was democratic for the few, not for the many.

Virginia's answer was different. The House of Burgesses, elected from 1619, spoke for the colony's settlements — but over time it grew more exclusive too, as the vote narrowed to property holders. Both regions began with a broader promise than they kept, and both left most people outside the room where decisions were made.

**Dissenters** who were pushed out did not always leave quietly. Roger Williams said civil power had no warrant in spiritual matters, and was banished in 1636; he founded Providence, where conscience was free. Anne Hutchinson was tried in 1637 and banished in 1638. Thomas Hooker left in 1636 for Hartford, where the **Fundamental Orders** of 1639 set out a government by written agreement.

Williams put his case in print. In The Bloudy Tenent of Persecution (1644), he argued that forcing conscience was itself a persecution — that the magistrate's sword had no business in matters of the soul. It was the boldest defense of religious freedom yet written in English America, and it came from a man Massachusetts Bay had thrown out.

By 1662 the Puritans faced a new problem: the children of church members had not joined the church, so the **Halfway Covenant** let their children be baptized anyway — a loosening of the rules that kept families inside the church.

## Maryland's narrow tolerance

Maryland began in 1632 as a refuge for Catholics, founded by the **proprietor** Lord Baltimore under a proprietary charter. But Protestants soon outnumbered Catholics in the colony, and the Catholic minority needed protection from the Protestant majority it had invited in.

The **Act of Toleration** of 1649 promised that no person professing to believe in Jesus Christ would be troubled for their religion. Read it closely: the protection covered Trinitarian Christians only. Anyone who denied the Trinity could be put to death under the same act, and Jews and people of other faiths had no protection at all.

The act did not last. It was later repealed, and Maryland's Catholics lost the protection it had promised. Tolerance in the colonies was often a bargain struck for the moment, not a right that held — and Maryland shows both the promise and the limit in a single law.

Maryland's story is the mirror of Pennsylvania's. Both were proprietary colonies founded as refuges, but Maryland's tolerance protected one faith's survival, while Penn's welcomed anyone who would buy land. One was a shield; the other was an open door.

## Sugar islands and the mainland

The richest colonies in the English world were not on the mainland at all. Around 1700, Barbados shipped about 50 million pounds of sugar a year — more than Virginia's tobacco — and enslaved people made up about three-quarters of the island's population, against less than a quarter in Virginia. The average sugar grower held 115 enslaved workers, and Barbados planters were about four times as wealthy as Virginia planters.

Sugar was a West Indies crop, not a mainland one. The lower South grew rice and indigo instead, and South Carolina's planters learned rice-growing from enslaved Africans who knew the crop. The mainland's staples were many; the islands' staple was one, and it was the most profitable crop in the Atlantic world.

New England lived off the sugar islands in a different way. Its rocky soil grew little, so New Englanders fished, built ships, and carried goods — grain and livestock to the sugar islands, and sugar and molasses back. The **carrying trade** made Boston a port city, and it tied every region's economy to the islands' enslaved labor.

The numbers tell the shape of each region. Tobacco in the Chesapeake, fish and ships in New England, rice and indigo in the lower South, sugar in the islands — and wheat in the middle colonies, which is where this lesson turns next.

## The middle colonies and a crowded harbor

Look at the East Prospect of Philadelphia. It was drawn by George Heap in 1754, under the direction of Nicholas Scull, and engraved by Thomas Jefferys in 1768. (The name "Thomas Vandergucht" does not exist — it mixes the artist Gerard Vandergucht with the engraver.) The view shows a grid city across the Delaware, with sailing ships crowded at the wharves.

![An East Prospect of the City of Philadelphia, drawn 1754, engraved 1768](u2/philadelphia-east-prospect-1768.jpg)
Figure 1: An East Prospect of Philadelphia, drawn 1754, engraved 1768. Ships crowd the Delaware below a grid city. Public domain.

Philadelphia grew because the middle colonies welcomed everyone. Germans, Scots-Irish, Dutch, and Swedes farmed wheat in the valleys behind the port, and the city shipped their grain abroad. Benjamin Franklin worried in 1751 that the Germans would "Germanize us instead of our Anglifying them" — a complaint that shows how fast the mixture was changing the colony.

Georgia was the last of the thirteen colonies, founded in 1732 as a **buffer colony** between South Carolina and Spanish Florida and a home for the "deserving poor." Its settlers were expected to serve as soldiers, and it had no assembly at first. The trustees' rules failed, and Georgia became a royal colony in 1752.

By 1750 the three regions were distinct, and a traveler could tell them apart by the work, the worship, and the wealth. The Chesapeake grew tobacco on plantations, New England fished and traded, and the middle colonies farmed wheat and welcomed strangers. The lesson's last section asks how much of that past you can trust a picture to tell.

## A burning town, two centuries late

Look at the painting. Howard Pyle's The Burning of Jamestown shows armed rebels with torches and muskets marching through a burning town at night, flames lighting the streets and the faces of the men. It is a dramatic picture — and it was painted in 1905, more than two centuries after the event it shows.

![The Burning of Jamestown, painted 1905](u2/pr25e-test3-q01.jpg)
Figure 2: Howard Pyle's The Burning of Jamestown, painted 1905. Rebels with torches march through a burning town, 229 years after the event. Public domain.

Pyle never saw Jamestown burn. No one alive in 1905 had. The painting is an imagining, useful for what it tells us about how Americans in 1905 remembered Bacon's Rebellion, not for what happened in 1676. A source's date is part of its evidence: a painting made 229 years after the fact cannot prove what the burning looked like.

The rebellion itself belongs to the next lesson, where it changed Virginia's labor system for good. Here the point is the skill: when a question shows you a picture, ask when it was made, who made it, and what it could possibly have seen. The answer decides how far the picture can carry you.

## Worked items

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0042 (Topic 2.3)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage: by 1662, the children of the first Puritans were having children of their own — but fewer of them had joined the church, and only church members could vote or have their children baptized. The Halfway Covenant of 1662 let the children of church members be baptized even if their parents had not joined the church. Thirty years later, in Salem, more than 100 people were accused of witchcraft and 20 were executed.

a. Identify one change in New England religion between 1662 and 1692.

Model answer: The Halfway Covenant of 1662 loosened the rules of church membership, letting the children of members be baptized without their parents joining. By 1692, Salem showed the other side of Puritan religion — fear of the devil led to accusations and executions.

b. Describe one reason the Halfway Covenant was adopted.

Model answer: Fewer second-generation Puritans had joined the church, so the colony faced a generation of children who could not be baptized and parents who could not vote. The covenant kept those families inside the church by baptizing their children.

c. Explain one way the Salem trials show the limits of Puritan religious authority.

Model answer: Salem shows that Puritan authority could turn fear into deadly punishment — twenty people were executed on accusations of witchcraft. The trials also show its limits — the panic ended, and the colony later apologized for the executions.

How to think about it: part a asks for a change, so name the loosening and the fear together. Part b asks for a reason, so start with the shrinking membership. Part c asks for a limit, so name what the authority did and where it stopped.

### Worked item 2 — apush-saq-0044 (Topic 2.3)

The stimulus is the painting you have already seen as Figure 2: Howard Pyle's The Burning of Jamestown, painted in 1905 — more than two centuries after the event it shows. Armed rebels with torches and muskets march through a burning town at night. Flames light the streets and the faces of the men, while smoke rises over the houses.

a. Identify the event shown in the painting.

Model answer: The painting shows Bacon's Rebellion of 1676, when Nathaniel Bacon's followers burned Jamestown. The rebels torched the Virginia capital after Governor Berkeley refused to let them fight Native peoples on the frontier.

b. Describe one cause of the rebellion.

Model answer: Frontier farmers wanted protection from Native raids, but Governor Berkeley refused to let them raise a militia. Berkeley's refusal pushed the farmers to follow Bacon and take the fight themselves.

c. Explain one consequence of the rebellion for Virginia.

Model answer: The rebellion frightened Virginia's leaders because former servants and free Black people had fought alongside Bacon. Afterward the colony moved toward laws that divided poor whites from enslaved Black people and tied slavery to race.

How to think about it: part a asks what the painting shows, so name the rebellion and the year. Part b asks for a cause, so name Berkeley's refusal. Part c asks for a consequence, so follow the fright to the laws that divided the poor by race.

### Worked item 3 — apush-saq-0045 (Topic 2.3)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage: Georgia was founded in 1732 as a colony for the "deserving poor" — debtors and others who had fallen on hard times in England. Its founder, James Oglethorpe, meant it as a buffer between South Carolina and Spanish Florida, and its settlers were expected to serve as soldiers. Georgia had no assembly at first, and in 1752 it was ceded to the Crown.

a. Identify one purpose for which Georgia was founded.

Model answer: Georgia was founded as a colony for the 'deserving poor' — debtors and others who had fallen on hard times. It was also meant as a military buffer between South Carolina and Spanish Florida.

b. Describe one way Georgia differed from other English colonies at its founding.

Model answer: Georgia had no assembly at first, while other colonies had elected assemblies. Its settlers were also expected to serve as soldiers, a military duty other colonies did not impose.

c. Explain one reason Georgia became a royal colony in 1752.

Model answer: Georgia struggled under the trustees' rules, and its military buffer role needed the Crown's support. The trustees ceded Georgia to the Crown in 1752, and it became a royal colony.

How to think about it: part a asks for a purpose, so take it from the passage — the poor, or the buffer. Part b asks for a difference, so name what Georgia lacked or demanded. Part c asks for a reason, so link the trustees' struggles to the Crown's support.

## Key terms

- **tobacco** — the Chesapeake's staple crop, shipped to England in tens of millions of pounds a year by 1700.

- **staple crop** — the one crop a region grows mainly for sale, such as tobacco in the Chesapeake or sugar in Barbados.

- **town meeting** — the New England gathering where a town's voters decided local business.

- **franchise** — the right to vote, limited in Massachusetts Bay to male church members.

- **dissenter** — a person who disagreed with the established church, such as Roger Williams or Anne Hutchinson.

- **Fundamental Orders** — the 1639 written agreement governing Connecticut, drawn up by the settlers at Hartford.

- **Halfway Covenant** — the 1662 rule letting the children of church members be baptized even if their parents had not joined the church.

- **Act of Toleration** — Maryland's 1649 law protecting Trinitarian Christians from punishment for their religion.

- **proprietor** — a person who held a colony under a charter as a personal grant, such as Lord Baltimore in Maryland.

- **carrying trade** — hauling other people's goods by ship for pay, the business that made Boston a port city.

- **buffer colony** — a colony founded to shield a richer colony from an enemy, such as Georgia against Spanish Florida.

## Closing check

After this lesson, you should be able to explain how the land and the crop shaped each region's economy, and why tobacco, fish, and wheat made three different societies.

You should be able to explain who could vote in New England and in Virginia, and how dissenters such as Williams and Hutchinson answered exclusion.

You should be able to explain what Maryland's Act of Toleration protected, and what it left unprotected.

You should be able to judge a picture as a source, asking when it was made and what it could possibly have seen.
