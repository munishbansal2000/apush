The United States won a war with Mexico in less than two years and took a third more territory. The victory raised a question the nation could not answer: would slavery spread into the new land? This lesson follows that question from the battlefield to the Compromise of 1850.

## A war over a border

President James K. Polk wanted California and the Southwest, and he tried to buy them. Mexico refused to sell, and the two nations also quarreled over the southern border of Texas. Mexico said the border was the Nueces River. The United States said it was the Rio Grande, 150 miles farther south. Polk sent General Zachary Taylor's army to the Rio Grande, and in April 1846 Mexican troops attacked an American patrol there.

Polk told Congress that American blood had been shed on American soil, and asked for war. Congress declared war on May 13, 1846. Not everyone agreed it was a just war. Whigs said Polk had forced it, and Abraham Lincoln, a young Illinois congressman, introduced **spot resolutions** demanding that Polk name the exact spot where American blood was shed. Northerners suspected the war was a scheme to win land for slavery. Slavery was illegal in the Mexican lands, and the fight was whether the war would introduce it.

Henry David Thoreau refused to pay a tax that supported the war and spent a night in jail for it. His protest was small, but it showed that the war divided the North as well as the nation. The war was popular with expansionists and opposed by many who saw it as a war for slavery's spread.

## The Mexican Cession

The fighting went the Americans' way. Taylor won battles in northern Mexico, including Buena Vista in February 1847. In California, American settlers declared the Bear Flag Republic on July 4, 1846, and American troops took New Mexico. General Winfield Scott landed at Vera Cruz in March 1847, marched inland, and captured Mexico City on September 13, 1847. Mexico's government could no longer field an army against the Americans.

The **Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo**, signed on February 2, 1848, ended the war. The United States paid Mexico $15 million for California and New Mexico, the land called the **Mexican Cession**. It covers present-day California, Nevada, Utah, and parts of Arizona, New Mexico, Colorado, and Wyoming. The spoils of the war increased the territory of the United States by one-third.

Five years later the United States bought one more strip of land from Mexico, the **Gadsden Purchase** of 1853, paying $10 million for a strip along the southern border where a railroad could run. It was the last piece of the continental United States, bought for a railroad route rather than taken in war.

The treaty said little about the people already living in the new lands. Mexicans who stayed were promised citizenship and the protection of their property, but many lost their land in American courts in the years that followed. Native peoples faced new pressure as settlers poured in, and the government began pushing them onto reservations.

## The question that would not wait

In August 1846, while the war was still being fought, David Wilmot, a Democratic congressman from Pennsylvania, proposed banning slavery in any land taken from Mexico. The **Wilmot Proviso** passed the House twice, in August 1846 and February 1847, and failed in the Senate each time. The vote split Congress by section rather than by party: Northerners of both parties voted for it, and Southerners of both parties voted against it.

John C. Calhoun answered for the South. Slavery was legal in many states, he argued, so Congress could not ban it in territories held in common by all the states. Polk proposed extending the Missouri Compromise line to the Pacific, so that slavery would be banned north of the line and allowed south of it. Neither side accepted the plan, and the question stayed open.

In the election of 1848 both major parties dodged the question. The Whigs ran Taylor, a war hero. The Democrats ran Lewis Cass, who proposed **popular sovereignty**, letting the people of a territory decide whether to allow slavery. Antislavery men in both parties formed the **Free-Soil Party** and ran former president Martin Van Buren. He won about 10 percent of the popular vote, and Taylor won the election.

The Free-Soilers were not mostly abolitionists. They opposed the expansion of slavery because they did not want white settlers to compete with slave labor in the new territories. Their vote showed that the argument over the Mexican Cession had created a new political force, one organized around keeping slavery out of the West.

## Gold rush California

Gold was found at Sutter's Mill in January 1848, a week before the treaty was signed. Within a year more than 80,000 forty-niners had arrived, and by late 1849 California had enough settlers to seek statehood. Californians wrote a constitution that banned slavery, and asked Congress to admit them as a free state.

President Taylor encouraged California to apply, and Southerners objected. Much of California lay south of the Missouri Compromise line, and a free California would tip the balance of the Senate against the South. Talk of secession began in the South, and Calhoun warned that the Union would break unless the South's rights were restored.

New Mexico also sought statehood with a constitution that banned slavery, and Texas claimed part of New Mexico's land. The crisis piled up: California, New Mexico, the Texas boundary, the slave trade in Washington, and runaway slaves. Congress had to settle all of it at once, and the sections could not agree on any of it.

California's numbers tell the story. The census counted 92,597 people in California in 1850 and 379,994 in 1860. A territory that had been Mexican a few years earlier was now a state-sized population demanding admission, and its admission would change the balance of power in the Senate.

## The Compromise of 1850

Henry Clay, broker of the Missouri Compromise thirty years earlier, offered a package. California would enter as a free state; Utah and New Mexico would decide slavery by popular sovereignty. The Texas boundary would be settled. The slave trade, though not slavery, would be banned in Washington, D.C. A stronger Fugitive Slave Act would please the South. The package became the **Compromise of 1850**.

Clay could not pass his package as one bill: no one would vote for all of it. Stephen Douglas of Illinois broke it into separate bills, passing each with a different majority. Taylor died in July 1850, and Millard Fillmore signed the bills into law. The compromise held the Union together but pleased no one fully.

The **Fugitive Slave Act** was the bitterest part. Federal commissioners decided cases without a jury, and the accused could not testify. Commissioners were paid more for ruling a person a runaway than for ruling otherwise. Northern states answered with **personal liberty laws**, giving accused runaways a jury trial and a lawyer. Northerners formed vigilance committees to block slave catchers. Crowds in Boston and Syracuse freed accused runaways from federal officers in 1851 — Shadrach Minkins in Boston, William ‘Jerry’ Henry in Syracuse.

Look at the map. Elliott's 1856 map calls itself "the area of freedom and slavery, and the territories whose destiny is yet to be decided." Frémont and Dayton look down. The Missouri Compromise line runs across the middle; the tables below compare the free and slave states from the census of 1850. Six years on, the question was still open.

## The book that changed the North

The Fugitive Slave Act brought slavery into Northern streets, and a novel gave it a human face. Harriet Beecher Stowe's Uncle Tom's Cabin appeared in 1852. It showed enslaved families torn apart and a system that broke them. It sold almost 275,000 copies within a year, and readers who had never seen a plantation now felt they had.

Look at the title page. It reads "Uncle Tom's Cabin; or, Life Among the Lowly," with an engraving of a woman at a cabin door with children nearby. The words "Tenth Thousand" mark a book already selling fast in its first edition. The picture put a family, not an argument, in front of the reader.

![Title page of Uncle Tom's Cabin, vol. 1, Boston, 1852](u5/5s24-ch14-mcq-07.jpg)

The novel turned many indifferent Northerners against slavery. Southerners answered with proslavery novels like Aunt Phillis's Cabin, picturing kind masters and contented enslaved people, and tried to ban Stowe's book. The two sides were no longer arguing only about territories. They were arguing about what slavery was.

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0139 (Topic 5.3)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage. It says the war ended in 1848 with the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, that the United States paid $15 million for California and the Southwest, and that the Wilmot Proviso made slavery in the new lands the central question of American politics.

a. Identify one territorial gain the United States made in the Mexican-American War, as described in the passage.

Model answer: The United States took California from Mexico in the war, along with the Southwest, the region that became New Mexico, Nevada, Utah, and parts of other present-day states. Any one of these gains answers the question.

b. Explain how the Wilmot Proviso showed that the war had made slavery a national political question.

Model answer: The Proviso tried to ban slavery in all land taken from Mexico, and it split Congress by section rather than by party. Northerners in both parties voted for it and Southerners in both parties voted against it, so a war over land had become a national fight over slavery.

c. Explain one political development that resulted from the argument over slavery in the territories taken from Mexico.

Model answer: The Free-Soil Party formed in 1848 around opposition to slavery's expansion, with Martin Van Buren as its candidate for president. It won about 10 percent of the popular vote, showing that the argument over the new lands had created a new political force.

How to think about it: part a asks for one gain from the passage, so name a place the United States took. Part b asks what the Proviso showed, so look at how the vote split. Part c asks for a political result, so name the party that formed around the argument.

### Worked item 2 — apush-saq-0142 (Topic 5.4)

The stimulus is a paraphrase of historian David Potter’s account of the 1850s: the slavery question stopped being a subject for debate and became a test of loyalty, with each section demanding the other prove its good faith.

a. Identify one issue over slavery in the territories that deepened sectional hostility in the period 1848–1850.

Model answer: One issue was whether slavery could spread into the lands taken from Mexico. The Wilmot Proviso tried to ban slavery there in 1846, and the fight over it split Congress by section rather than by party.

b. Explain how one measure of the Compromise of 1850 tried to settle that issue.

Model answer: The Compromise of 1850 admitted California as a free state, which pleased the North, and it passed a stronger Fugitive Slave Act, which pleased the South. The package tried to settle the territorial question by giving each section something it wanted, instead of deciding the slavery question by a single rule.

c. Explain one way the Fugitive Slave Act showed the Compromise had not settled the sectional conflict.

Model answer: The Fugitive Slave Act showed the settlement had failed because it forced Northerners to help return accused runaways, and many Northerners who had ignored slavery now resisted the law. Rescues in Boston and Syracuse, and personal liberty laws in Northern states, proved the compromise had bought quiet in Congress but not peace in the country.

How to think about it: part a asks for one issue, so name the fight over slavery in the Mexican Cession. Part b asks how one measure tried to settle it, so name the measure and what each side got. Part c asks how the Fugitive Slave Act showed the failure, so name what it forced Northerners to do and how they answered.

## Key terms

- **spot resolutions** — Lincoln's 1847 resolutions demanding Polk name the exact spot where American blood was shed.

- **Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo** — the 1848 treaty ending the Mexican-American War, by which Mexico sold California and New Mexico to the United States for $15 million.

- **Mexican Cession** — the land Mexico sold to the United States in 1848: present-day California, Nevada, Utah, and parts of Arizona, New Mexico, Colorado, and Wyoming.

- **Gadsden Purchase** — the 1853 purchase of a strip of land along the Mexican border for a southern railroad route.

- **Wilmot Proviso** — the 1846 proposal to ban slavery in any land taken from Mexico.

- **popular sovereignty** — letting the people of a territory decide whether to allow slavery.

- **Free-Soil Party** — the 1848 party opposed to the expansion of slavery into the western territories.

- **Compromise of 1850** — the package of bills admitting California as a free state and strengthening the Fugitive Slave Act.

- **Fugitive Slave Act** — the 1850 law that made it easier to return accused runaways, decided by federal commissioners without a jury.

- **personal liberty laws** — Northern state laws giving accused runaways a jury trial and a lawyer.
