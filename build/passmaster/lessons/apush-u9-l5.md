How did September 11, the Great Recession, and the changes of the 2010s and 2020s reshape what Americans expect from their government? This lesson answers that question. Terror abroad, crisis at home, and a divided politics tested the country's institutions, and the tests are still running.

## September 11

On the morning of September 11, 2001, nineteen hijackers from the terrorist group al-Qaeda seized four passenger planes. Two flew into the twin towers of the World Trade Center in New York, and both towers collapsed within two hours. A third struck the Pentagon, outside Washington. Passengers on the fourth plane fought the hijackers, and the plane crashed in a field near Shanksville, Pennsylvania.

Nearly 3,000 people died that day, the worst attack on American soil since Pearl Harbor. Look at the photograph: the Pentagon after the attack, its western wedge collapsed and blackened by fire, with cranes and fire trucks at work and a large American flag hung on the intact facade.

![The Pentagon after the September 11 attacks](u9/pr25e-ch13-q11.jpg)
Figure 1: The Pentagon after the September 11 attacks, its western wedge collapsed and blackened by fire. Public domain (U.S. federal government work).

The attacks launched the **War on Terror**, a campaign against terrorist networks abroad and new security measures at home. Security became a central job of the federal government, and the country began a long argument over how much liberty to trade for safety.

## The War on Terror

The first target was Afghanistan, where the Taliban government sheltered al-Qaeda. American forces invaded in October 2001 and overthrew the Taliban within weeks. But killing one leader did not end the insurgency, and building a stable government proved harder than winning battles. The war lasted twenty years, until American troops left in 2021.

Three days after the attacks, President George W. Bush stood at Ground Zero in New York, speaking through a bullhorn to rescue workers. Look at the photograph: Bush with the bullhorn, standing beside a firefighter, with rescue workers and heavy equipment behind them.

![President Bush speaks through a bullhorn at Ground Zero, September 14, 2001](u9/original-ctx-u9-05.jpg)
Figure 2: President Bush speaks through a bullhorn at Ground Zero on September 14, 2001. Public domain (U.S. federal government work).

In January 2002, Bush named Iraq, Iran, and North Korea an **axis of evil**, warning that they might arm terrorists with weapons of mass destruction. The phrase set the case for a new policy, the **Bush Doctrine**: rather than deter threats, the United States would strike first against nations seen as dangers.

Congress authorized the use of force against Iraq in October 2002, and American forces invaded in March 2003. The overthrow of Saddam Hussein was quick, but stability proved elusive, and insurgent attacks dragged on for years. No weapons of mass destruction were found, and no link was found between Iraq and the attacks of September 11.

The wars cost thousands of American lives and trillions of dollars, and they divided the country. Supporters said the wars kept the next attack from happening. Critics said the wars created new enemies and distracted America from problems at home. The argument over the wars became an argument over what American power was for.

## Liberty and security at home

At home, Congress passed the **USA Patriot Act** six weeks after the attacks. The law greatly expanded the government's power to investigate suspected terrorists. National security letters let the FBI obtain phone, email, and financial records without a court order. In 2003, Congress created the **Department of Homeland Security**, a new department to protect the country from terrorist attacks.

The government also held suspected terrorists at Guantanamo Bay, Cuba, calling them enemy combatants rather than prisoners of war. In **Hamdan v. Rumsfeld** in 2006, the Supreme Court ruled that the government could not hold detainees indefinitely without due process. The ruling set a limit: even in wartime, the government must answer to the law.

The measures outlasted the Bush presidency. President Obama kept the Patriot Act measures and Guantanamo, expanded the use of drones against terrorist leaders, and renewed a program of mass data collection. That program was exposed in 2013, when a government contractor named Edward Snowden leaked its existence, and the country argued again over where security ends and privacy begins.

## Boom, bust, and the government's answer

The 1990s had brought the longest economic expansion the country had seen to that point. Personal computers and the internet created new industries, trade agreements linked American firms to the world, and the federal budget moved into surplus from 1998 to 2001. The surplus reached $236 billion in 2000, the largest in American history to that point.

The boom did not lift everyone. Manufacturing jobs kept shrinking, and the gap between rich and poor kept widening. When the dot-com bubble burst in 2001, the expansion ended. Tax cuts cut revenue and the wars raised spending, and the surpluses were gone.

The next crisis came from housing. Banks made risky loans to buyers with weak credit, and Wall Street bundled those loans into investments sold around the world. When house prices fell in 2007, the system froze. Unemployment rose from 4.6 percent in 2007 to 9.6 percent in 2010, in the worst downturn since the Depression of the 1930s.

The government stepped in. In 2008 Congress approved $700 billion to buy troubled assets from banks, and in 2009 it passed a stimulus of almost $800 billion. The rescue steadied the banks, but the recovery was slow. Bank rescues fed populist fury on both sides: Occupy Wall Street on the left, and the **Tea Party** on the right.

## A changing society, a divided politics

The 2010s changed daily life as well as politics. The **Affordable Care Act**, signed in March 2010, expanded Medicaid and set up exchanges where people could buy health insurance. The share of non-elderly Americans without insurance fell from 18 percent in 2010 to 10 percent in 2016, and by 2021 more than 30 million people had gained coverage. The law stayed controversial: Republicans opposed it, and premiums rose for some who bought insurance without subsidies.

Opposition to the health-care law helped fuel the Tea Party, a movement that rose sharply after 2009 among voters who saw government as too big. Society itself was changing: women entered the workforce in greater numbers, married later, and built careers outside the home. Households headed by unmarried adults rose from 30 percent in 1970 to 48 percent in 2000.

In 2015, the Supreme Court ruled in **Obergefell v. Hodges** that same-sex couples had a constitutional right to marry nationwide. In 2022, the Court ruled in Dobbs v. Jackson that the Constitution did not protect abortion, overturning Roe v. Wade and sending the question back to the states. The two rulings showed how much of American life the Court still decided.

Politics grew more divided. **Polarization**, the sorting of Americans into two camps that rarely agree, deepened as media fragmented and the parties sorted by region and outlook. In 2000 and again in 2016, the winner of the Electoral College lost the popular vote. After the election of 2020, the sitting president refused to accept the results, and on January 6, 2021, a mob attacked the Capitol as Congress counted the electoral votes.

## America in a changing world

American foreign policy swung with each president. The United States signed the Kyoto climate agreement but the Senate never ratified it. The Paris Agreement of 2015 set climate goals, and American participation swung with the White House: one president withdrew, the next rejoined, and the next withdrew again.

In the Middle East, the **Abraham Accords** of September 2020 normalized relations between Israel and the United Arab Emirates and Bahrain, with the United States mediating. Look at the photograph: the signing at the White House, with President Trump, Prime Minister Netanyahu, and the representatives of the Emirates and Bahrain seated at an outdoor table.

![The Abraham Accords are signed at the White House, September 2020](u9/5s24-ch31-mcq-08.jpg)
Figure 3: The Abraham Accords are signed at the White House in September 2020. Public domain (U.S. federal government work).

When Russia invaded Ukraine in February 2022, the United States backed Ukraine with aid and sanctions, but sent no troops. The contrast with Iraq in 2003 was plain: America would arm an ally, not invade. The old question of when to use force had a new answer, and a new president could change it.

Then came the **COVID-19 pandemic**, which spread through the world in 2020. Businesses and schools closed, the economy contracted sharply, and unemployment neared 15 percent at its worst. More than a million Americans died. The pandemic reopened the old argument over safety and liberty, over masks, closures, and vaccines, and it left the country arguing over what the government owed its people.

The twenty-first century has not settled the twentieth century's questions. How much power should the government have, at home and abroad? Who belongs, and who decides? The answers keep changing with each election, each crisis, and each generation — and that is what the period has taught.

## Key terms

- **War on Terror** — the campaign against terrorist networks abroad and new security measures at home after September 11.

- **USA Patriot Act** — the 2001 law that greatly expanded the government's power to investigate suspected terrorists.

- **Department of Homeland Security** — the department created in 2003 to protect the country from terrorist attacks.

- **Bush Doctrine** — the policy of striking first against nations seen as threats, rather than deterring them.

- **axis of evil** — Bush's term for Iraq, Iran, and North Korea in his January 2002 State of the Union address.

- **Hamdan v. Rumsfeld** — the 2006 Supreme Court ruling that detainees could not be held indefinitely without due process.

- **Affordable Care Act** — the 2010 law that expanded Medicaid and set up exchanges for buying health insurance.

- **Tea Party** — the movement that rose sharply after 2009 among voters who saw government as too big.

- **Obergefell v. Hodges** — the 2015 Supreme Court ruling that same-sex couples had a constitutional right to marry nationwide.

- **polarization** — the sorting of Americans into two camps that rarely agree.

- **COVID-19 pandemic** — the pandemic that spread through the world in 2020, killing more than a million Americans.

- **Abraham Accords** — the 2020 agreements normalizing relations between Israel and the United Arab Emirates and Bahrain.
