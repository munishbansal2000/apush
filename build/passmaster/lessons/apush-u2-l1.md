Why did England, France, and the Netherlands all cross the Atlantic after 1607 — and why did they stay when Spain had already taken the richest lands? This lesson answers that question. The answer is that each power wanted something different, and each built a different kind of colony to get it.

## A century of crossings

In the century before 1607, Spain built an empire of conquest in the Americas. It took silver from mines and labor from Indigenous peoples, and by 1650 about 350,000 Spaniards had crossed to the New World. Spain's empire was the model every rival studied — and the rival every newcomer had to work around.

After 1607, three new rivals crossed to stay. England planted settler colonies along the coast, France built a fur-trading empire along the St. Lawrence, and the Netherlands built a trading post on the Hudson. None of them controlled the continent alone, and all four powers competed for land, trade, and Native allies.

The crossings were deadly. Of the 144 settlers who landed at Jamestown in 1607, only 38 were still alive a year later. In the winter of 1609–1610, the "starving time," nearly 90 percent of about 500 colonists died. Massachusetts Bay did better: more than 20,000 settlers arrived between 1630 and 1642. France sent few settlers, mostly single men — the books give no exact count.

The four powers treated Native peoples differently. Spain conquered and put them to work in mines and on estates. France allied with them for the fur trade and depended on their knowledge. England displaced them, clearing land for farms. The differences mattered — and the next lessons show how each pattern broke down.

## Why England came

England pushed people across the ocean before any colony paid. In the 1580s, Richard Hakluyt argued that England had idle poor with no work, timber for ships, and a Protestant faith to spread. **Enclosure** had fenced common land, so families who once farmed it had nowhere to go. Younger sons with no inheritance looked across the ocean for land of their own.

Virginia began as a business venture. The Virginia Company, a **joint-stock company** owned by investors who hoped for a return, sent settlers to find gold and a route to Asia, and found neither. Tobacco saved the colony: John Rolfe's crop sold in London, and planters grew it on large tracts worked by bound labor. Virginia's motive was economic gain, and its shape — plantations, servants, and then enslaved workers — followed from that motive.

A 1670 painting shows enslaved laborers curing tobacco under open sheds. Its place is unknown, and the palm trees suggest the Caribbean, so it cannot prove what Virginia's sheds looked like.

Other colonies began for other reasons. Plymouth was a refuge for Separatists in 1620, Maryland a refuge for Catholics in 1634, Rhode Island a refuge for dissenters in 1636, Pennsylvania a refuge for Quakers in 1682, and Georgia a buffer against Spanish Florida in 1732. The same crowded, quarreling England produced all of them — and each colony's founding reason shaped the place it became.

Georgia's trustees banned slavery and limited land grants, hoping for a colony of small soldier-farmers. Both rules were gone by 1751, and Georgia became a royal colony in 1752.

## A map is a claim

John Smith's map of Virginia, published in 1612, was practical knowledge for settlers, not a picture of conquest or treasure. It names Native towns and peoples across the Chesapeake, marks rivers and bays, and shows where food and friends could be found. A settler who could read this map could survive; one who could not was lost.

Look at the map. Powhatan sits in the upper left, in the pose of a ruler receiving a prisoner, with a note explaining that Smith was delivered to him in 1607. A Native man with a bow stands at the right. The royal arms of England sit at the center. The map records what Smith learned from Native peoples — and it also claims the land for the king whose arms it carries.

The French built differently. Champlain's Habitation at Quebec, drawn in 1613, shows a small fortified trading post: walls, cannons, storehouses, and dwellings on a riverbank, with gardens outside the walls. It was a place to trade furs with Native partners, not a town of farmers. The drawing's size tells the story — France's empire in North America was a trading post, not a settlement.

## Covenants and charters

The Pilgrims landed outside their patent, so they had no legal right to settle where they stood. Before leaving the ship in 1620, they signed the **Mayflower Compact**, a **covenant** in which they combined into a civil body politic and promised to make just and equal laws. Self-government in New England began as an agreement among settlers, not a gift from the king.

Ten years later, John Winthrop told the settlers aboard the Arbella that they would be a **"city upon a hill"**, a community the world would watch. Massachusetts Bay was a refuge for Puritans, but it was also a project: a godly society built by families who came to stay. That is why more than 20,000 crossed in twelve years, while Jamestown's single men died in droves.

The godly society had sharp edges: Massachusetts banished Roger Williams in 1635 and Anne Hutchinson in 1638 for dissenting from its church. Williams founded Rhode Island; Hutchinson followed him there.

Virginia governed itself sooner than any other colony. In 1619 the **House of Burgesses** met, the first elected assembly in English America. When Virginia became a royal colony in 1624, the Burgesses survived — the king took the company, not the assembly. Self-government, once planted, proved hard to uproot.

Not every colony began with an assembly. Maryland was a **proprietary colony**, held by Lord Baltimore under a **charter** as a personal grant. Pennsylvania began the same way, under William Penn. The charter mattered: who held it decided who made the laws, and the next section shows what Penn did with his.

## Penn's bargain

William Penn held Pennsylvania as a personal grant, and he sold the land himself. That is the link behind his colony's religious tolerance: tolerance was a business plan as well as a belief. Penn welcomed Quakers, Germans, and other persecuted people, and each family that came bought land from him. His **Frame of Government** of 1682 promised an assembly and religious freedom, and settlers came because the promise was real.

Look at Thomas Holme's map of Pennsylvania, drawn in 1687. It shows surveyed parcels with owners' names along the Delaware River, and in the upper right, the grid plan of Philadelphia. This is a colony drawn for sale: every parcel has a buyer, and the city's straight streets were laid out before anyone lived in them. Penn's **Holy Experiment** was a town planned on paper and a colony sold lot by lot.

![Holme's map of Pennsylvania, 1687](u2/5s24-exam2-mcq-45.jpg)
Figure 2: Thomas Holme's map of Pennsylvania, 1687. Surveyed parcels with owners' names line the Delaware, and Philadelphia's grid sits at the upper right. Public domain.

Penn went further in 1701. His Charter of Privileges gave Pennsylvania toleration and a representative government that could not easily be taken back. Pennsylvania grew faster than any colony in the region, because persecuted people came for the freedom he promised — and stayed because the land they bought was theirs.

## The empire loosens its grip

The Dutch built for trade, not settlement. The Castello Plan shows New Amsterdam in 1660 as a fortified trading port: a fort at the tip of Manhattan, a wall across the town, canals, gardens, and ships in the harbor. England took the town in 1664 and renamed it New York, but the Dutch pattern — a commercial port full of traders — stayed.

![The Castello Plan of New Amsterdam, 1660, redrawn 1916](u2/original-ctx-u2-07.jpg)
Figure 3: New Amsterdam in 1660, redrawn in 1916. The fort guards Manhattan's tip, and a wall crosses the town. Public domain.

England tried to tighten its grip in 1686, when James II joined the northern colonies into the **Dominion of New England** and revoked their charters. The Dominion collapsed three years later, when the Glorious Revolution drove James from the throne in 1689. Colonists who had governed themselves for sixty years were not about to stop.

After 1689, England mostly left the colonies alone. From the 1720s, under Robert Walpole, the trade laws stayed on the books but were barely enforced — a policy Edmund Burke later called **salutary neglect**. London had its reasons: an ocean's width, wars in Europe, and a prime minister who valued quiet trade over colonial quarrels. The colonies used the quiet decades to grow, govern themselves, and forget how to obey. The empire's loosest years were also its most prosperous.

## Worked items

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0034 (Topic 2.1)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage: in the century before 1607, Spain built an empire of conquest in the Americas, taking silver from mines and labor from Indigenous peoples. After 1607, three new rivals crossed the Atlantic to stay: England planted settler colonies along the coast, France built a fur-trading empire along the St. Lawrence, and the Netherlands built a trading post on the Hudson.

a. Identify one way European activity in the Americas in Period 2 (1607-1754) differed from European activity in Period 1 (1491-1607).

Model answer: One way Period 2 differed from Period 1 is that Europeans came to stay: in the 1500s Spain conquered and mined, while in the 1600s England, France, and the Netherlands planted permanent settlements with families, farms, and trading posts. The goal shifted from taking wealth from the land to building colonies on it.

b. Explain one reason Europeans came to stay in North America during Period 2.

Model answer: One reason Europeans came to stay is that the land offered a living: tobacco made Virginia pay, beaver fur made New France pay, and the hope of a godly community drew Puritan families to Massachusetts Bay. Each colony promised something worth settling for, whether wealth, trade, or worship.

c. Explain one way Native peoples shaped the outcome of European colonization in Period 2.

Model answer: One way Native peoples shaped the outcome is that their alliances and resistance decided which colonies survived: the French depended on Wendat allies for the fur trade, while Powhatan's peoples fought the English and nearly destroyed Jamestown. No colony succeeded without Native knowledge, trade, or opposition shaping its path.

How to think about it: part a asks for a difference between the two periods, so name what changed — conquest became settlement. Part b asks for a reason, so link each colony to what it promised. Part c asks for an effect, so name an alliance or a resistance and the colony it decided.

### Worked item 2 — apush-saq-0037 (Topic 2.2)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage: English colonies in North America were founded for many reasons at once. Virginia began as a business venture in tobacco, Plymouth as a refuge for Separatists, Maryland as a refuge for Catholics, and Pennsylvania as a refuge for Quakers. Behind them all stood England itself: a crowded country where enclosure had fenced common land, where religious quarrels divided neighbors, and where younger sons with no inheritance looked across the ocean for land of their own.

a. Identify one motive for English colonization named in the passage.

Model answer: One motive was economic gain. Virginia began as a business venture in tobacco, founded to make money for its investors.

b. Describe one way that motive shaped the colony it founded.

Model answer: The profit motive shaped Virginia into a plantation colony. Planters grew tobacco on large tracts worked by bound labor, first indentured servants and then enslaved Africans, because tobacco made money only at scale.

c. Explain one similarity or difference between English colonization and Spanish or French colonization in this period.

Model answer: One difference is who did the founding. English colonies were founded by companies, proprietors, and religious groups, while Spanish colonies were ruled directly by the crown through viceroys. French colonization differed too, since France sent few settlers and built its empire on the fur trade and Native alliances.

How to think about it: part a asks for one motive from the passage, so take it straight from the text. Part b asks how the motive shaped its colony, so follow the money to the plantation. Part c asks for a comparison, so name who founded each empire's colonies.

### Worked item 3 — apush-saq-0041 (Topic 2.2)

The stimulus is William Penn's own words, from the preface to Pennsylvania's Frame of Government (1682):

> "Governments, like clocks, go from the motion men give them; and as governments are made and moved by men, so by them they are ruined too."

a. Identify Penn's claim about government.

Model answer: Penn claims that governments run on the motion men give them, like clocks. A government is made and moved by people, and the same people can ruin it.

b. Describe one way Pennsylvania's government reflected Penn's claim.

Model answer: Pennsylvania's government gave settlers a real assembly and protected religious freedom. Penn's Frame of Government let the people he invited take part in making the laws that moved their colony.

c. Explain one way Pennsylvania differed from other English colonies in this period.

Model answer: Pennsylvania differed in its religious tolerance. While most colonies had an established church, Penn welcomed Quakers, Germans, and others, and his colony grew quickly because persecuted people came for the freedom he promised.

How to think about it: part a asks for Penn's claim, so restate the clock image in your own words. Part b asks for a reflection of the claim, so name what Penn's government gave the people. Part c asks for a difference, so name the tolerance and the growth it brought.

## Key terms

- **charter** — a written grant from the king giving a person or company the right to found and govern a colony.

- **joint-stock company** — a business owned by investors who share the cost and the profit, such as the Virginia Company.

- **enclosure** — the fencing of common land for private use, which left many English families without land to farm.

- **covenant** — a binding agreement, such as the Mayflower Compact, in which settlers promised to govern themselves by agreed laws.

- **Mayflower Compact** — the 1620 agreement signed aboard ship at Cape Cod, joining the Pilgrims into a civil body politic.

- **city upon a hill** — John Winthrop's image for Massachusetts Bay in 1630: a godly community the world would watch.

- **House of Burgesses** — Virginia's elected assembly, first met in 1619, the first in English America.

- **proprietary colony** — a colony held by a proprietor under a charter, such as Maryland under Lord Baltimore and Pennsylvania under William Penn.

- **Holy Experiment** — Penn's name for Pennsylvania: a colony built on religious tolerance and representative government.

- **Frame of Government** — Penn's 1682 plan for Pennsylvania, promising an assembly and religious freedom.

- **Dominion of New England** — James II's 1686 union of the northern colonies under one royal governor, which collapsed in 1689.

- **salutary neglect** — England's policy from the 1720s of leaving the trade laws on the books but barely enforcing them.
