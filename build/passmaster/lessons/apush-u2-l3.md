What tied a sugar field in Barbados to a fur trader in Canada, a silver mine in Peru, and a tobacco farm in Virginia? This lesson answers that question. The answer is the Atlantic economy: a system of trade, labor, and war that made each colony's wealth depend on the others — and on the people it used up.

## The empire's money theory

England's rulers believed wealth was fixed — that one country could only grow richer by taking wealth from another. The policy that followed was **mercantilism**: sell more than you buy — a **favorable balance of trade** — keep gold at home, and make the colonies serve the mother country. Thomas Mun put it plainly in 1664: a nation's treasure grows by foreign trade, when exports exceed imports.

The numbers show the idea at work. In Mun's own example, England exported goods worth £2.2 million in one year and imported £2.0 million, keeping a net gain of £200,000. The colonies existed to widen that gap: they supplied raw materials England could not grow, and bought finished goods England made.

The **Navigation Acts** turned the theory into law. The act of 1651 said colonial goods must travel on English ships; the act of 1660 said what colonies could sell and on whose ships; the act of 1663 said European goods bound for the colonies must pass through an English port. Tobacco, rice, and naval stores were **enumerated goods** — listed goods that could only be sold to England.

Other laws tightened the net: the Wool Act of 1699 limited colonial wool, and the Molasses Act of 1733 taxed foreign molasses. But the laws were strict on paper and porous in practice. Smuggling was common, customs officers were few, and from the 1720s England barely enforced the acts at all — a policy Edmund Burke later named **salutary neglect**, in 1775.

Adam Smith attacked the whole system in 1776, arguing that wealth grows when people trade freely, not when governments fence it in.

## Winds, ships, and the triangle

Look at Emanuel Bowen's chart of the Atlantic Ocean, drawn in 1740. Its title calls the ocean "the seat of war, both in Europe and America," and it shows the trade winds and the courses of sailing from one continent to the other. The winds are the reason the triangle worked: ships rode the trade winds west to the Americas and the westerlies home.

![Emanuel Bowen, chart of the Atlantic Ocean, 1740](u2/original-u1-trade-02.jpg)
Figure 1: Emanuel Bowen's chart of the Atlantic Ocean, 1740. The trade winds and sailing courses cross an ocean titled "the seat of war." Public domain.

The **triangular trade** had three legs. Manufactured goods sailed from England to Africa, where traders exchanged them for captive Africans. The captives crossed the Atlantic on the **Middle Passage** to the Americas, where they were sold to plantation owners. Plantation crops — sugar, tobacco, and rice — sailed back to Europe, and the profits paid for the next voyage.

The trade ran through African hands as well as European ones. At Elmina, on the Gold Coast, African middlemen in canoes carried captives and goods between the shore and the European ships, as a 1732 engraving by Kip shows. The Atlantic economy was not a European machine with African victims only — Africans shaped it as traders, rulers, and resisters, even as the trade destroyed their societies.

Spain's silver ran on a different route. From Havana harbor, treasure fleets carried silver mined in the Americas to Spain, guarded by warships. The silver paid Spain's wars and bought goods from across Europe — and the English, French, and Dutch all wanted a share of it, by trade or by theft.

## The Middle Passage

The Brookes diagram shows what the Middle Passage meant in practice. Published by abolitionists in 1788, it draws the lower deck of a slave ship with enslaved people packed in rows — 454 allowed under the Regulated Slave Trade Act of 1788 — with only 2 feet 7 inches of height between the beams. The drawing was made to shock, and it worked: it is the most famous image of the trade ever printed.

![Stowage of the British slave ship Brookes under the Regulated Slave Trade Act of 1788](u2/original-u1-trade-03.jpg)
Figure 2: Stowage of the British slave ship Brookes, 1788. Enslaved people packed in rows below deck, with 2 feet 7 inches between the beams. Public domain.

Olaudah Equiano lived the crossing and wrote about it in 1789. Brought to the coast as a boy, he saw the sea and a slave ship waiting for its cargo, and was handled and tossed up by the crew to see if he was sound — inspected the way a buyer inspects goods. He was persuaded he had gotten into a world of bad spirits, and that they were going to kill him.

Equiano's account is evidence of a system, not only of one man's suffering. The ship waited for its cargo, the crew checked the merchandise, and the destination was the Americas, where plantation owners bought people by the hundred. The trade linked Africa and the Americas in a single system — and the link was made of human bodies.

The Brookes was allowed 454 people under the 1788 act, and even that regulated number left each person less than three feet of height below deck. Before the act, ships carried more. The law did not end the crowding; it only set a number on it.

## The work behind the wealth

Look at the sugar plantation engraving. It shows a Caribbean estate in the 1700s: the master's house, the slave quarters, the sugar works, and the cane fields spread below green hills. It is an idealised view — and the idealisation is the point. No one is shown working the cane; one person fishes in the foreground. The forced labor that made the sugar is deliberately not shown.

That observation — that the labor is deliberately left out — comes from the file's modern published description, not from any note on the engraving itself. The engraving says nothing about its own omissions. The lesson is about reading images: what a picture leaves out can be evidence too, but you must say where the observation comes from.

![A Caribbean sugar plantation, engraved by Paolo Fumagalli in 1830](u2/original-u1-trade-11.png)
Figure 3: A Caribbean sugar plantation, engraved by Paolo Fumagalli in 1830. An idealised view: no one is shown working the cane. Public domain.

Spain's mines and estates ran on forced labor too. Under the **repartimiento**, towns in New Spain supplied four percent of their tributaries each week to work for Spaniards. In the Andes, the **mita** took one man in seven for the silver mines of Potosí. On Spanish estates, the **encomienda** gave a Spaniard the right to demand labor from a Native community. The systems differed by place, but all took Native labor by law.

The trade began with a license, not a raid. The first **asiento** — the contract to supply enslaved people to Spanish America — was granted in August 1518 to Laurent de Gouvenot, for eight years and at most 4,000 people. Gouvenot sold the license to Genoese merchants for 25,000 ducats. From the start, the trade was a business in paper as in people.

## Trade, disease, and war in the North

John Smith met Powhatan as a prisoner, and wrote about it as if he had met an emperor. His account calls Powhatan "emperor" and his followers "courtiers" — European categories imposed on a Native world. The famous "rescue" by Pocahontas may have been a ritual whose meaning Smith misunderstood. Read Smith for what he saw, and for what he wanted his readers to believe.

Trade turned to war quickly. The English and Powhatan's peoples traded corn, then raided each other, and in 1622 the Powhatan attacked, killing 347 settlers — about a quarter of the colony. The next year the English lured Native leaders to a peace meeting and served poisoned wine. The colony survived on tobacco money and more settlers.

Disease traveled the fur trade's routes. After 1634, measles and smallpox killed between half and two-thirds of the Huron people, who had allied with the French. As beaver grew scarce, the Iroquois — armed with Dutch guns — pushed into Huron territory, burning their villages in 1649. Trade, disease, and war arrived together, and no Native people in the fur country escaped all three.

In New England, the Pequot War of 1636–1638 ended with the burning of the main Pequot village in 1637, where about 400 people were killed, many of them women and children. Survivors were scattered or sold into slavery. The English also built fourteen **praying towns** by the 1670s, where Native converts lived under English rule — but converts remained second-class, and the towns did not protect them when war came.

## Two wars that broke the balance

King Philip's War began with land. English settlers kept pushing onto Wampanoag ground, and the trial and execution of three Wampanoag men lit the spark. The war opened with attacks on Swansea in June 1675 and burned for fourteen months. Metacom — called King Philip by the English — was killed in August 1676, and many survivors were sold into slavery in the West Indies, and Native power in southern New England was broken.

Look at the engraving. F. O. C. Darley's The King Philip War — A Raid on the Settlers was published in 1886, more than two centuries after the war. It shows warriors with tomahawks attacking settlers in a field, one settler firing a musket, a frightened child in the center. It is an imagining of the war, not evidence of it.

![The King Philip War — A Raid on the Settlers, engraving by F. O. C. Darley, 1886](u2/king-philip-war-darley.jpg)
Figure 4: Darley's The King Philip War — A Raid on the Settlers, 1886. An imagining of the war, not evidence of it. Public domain.

In the Southwest, the Pueblo Revolt of 1680 drove the Spanish out for twelve years. Popé, a Tewa leader from San Juan, coordinated the rising. One witness, Josephe, told interrogators in 1681 of beatings, seized property, and forced labor without pay — and of churches burned, because "the Spaniards' God was dead." When the Spanish returned in 1692, they ruled with more accommodation than before.

Mary Rowlandson was taken captive at Lancaster in February 1676. Her 1682 narrative calls the attackers "hell-hounds" — a grieving captive writing for a Puritan audience.

The Catawba adapted instead of fighting: they traded deerskins and welcomed peddlers, but alcohol and smallpox followed the trade, and they ceded land and status.

## Worked items

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0046 (Topic 2.4)

The stimulus is Olaudah Equiano's own words, from The Interesting Narrative (1789):

> "The first object which saluted my eyes when I arrived on the coast, was the sea, and a slave ship, which was then riding at anchor, and waiting for its cargo. I was immediately handled, and tossed up to see if I were sound, by some of the crew; and I was now persuaded that I had gotten into a world of bad spirits, and that they were going to kill me."

a. Identify one thing Equiano saw or experienced when he first reached the coast.

Model answer: Equiano saw the sea and a slave ship riding at anchor, waiting for its cargo. He was then handled and tossed up by the crew to see if he was sound.

b. Explain how Equiano's account shows that enslaved people were treated as goods to be inspected and sold.

Model answer: The crew handled Equiano and tossed him up to see if he was sound, the way a buyer inspects goods before a purchase. The ship waited for its cargo, and Equiano was part of that cargo — a person treated as a thing to be checked and sold.

c. Explain one way the passage shows that the Atlantic slave trade linked Africa and the Americas.

Model answer: The ship waited at the African coast for its cargo of enslaved people, and its destination was the Americas, where plantation owners bought them. The trade moved people across the ocean from Africa to the Americas, linking the two continents in one system.

How to think about it: part a asks for one thing from the passage, so name what Equiano saw or felt. Part b asks how the account shows inspection, so follow the crew's hands. Part c asks for a link, so name the ship's two ends — the African coast and the Americas.

### Worked item 2 — apush-saq-0050 (Topic 2.5)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage: the fur trade remade Native societies in the North. Beaver pelts bought European kettles, guns, and cloth, and villages that trapped furs grew rich while villages that did not fell behind. But the trade also carried disease along its routes: after 1634, measles and smallpox killed between half and two-thirds of the Huron people. And as beaver grew scarce, the Iroquois — armed with Dutch guns — pushed into Huron territory, burning their villages in 1649.

a. Identify one way contact with Europeans changed Native societies in the fur-trading regions.

Model answer: One way contact changed Native societies is that the fur trade created new inequalities: villages that trapped beaver grew rich on European goods while villages that did not fell behind. Trade made some villages wealthy and others poor, changing old relationships between neighbors.

b. Explain one cause of the Beaver Wars.

Model answer: One cause of the Beaver Wars was the decline of beaver populations near Iroquois territory as European demand for furs kept growing. The Iroquois needed new hunting grounds, so they pushed into Huron territory to control the beaver trade.

c. Explain one effect of epidemic disease on Native peoples in the fur-trading regions.

Model answer: One effect of epidemic disease was the collapse of the Huron: measles and smallpox killed between half and two-thirds of their people after 1634. The loss left Huron villages too weak to defend themselves when the Iroquois attacked in 1649.

How to think about it: part a asks for a change, so name the new inequality the trade created. Part b asks for a cause, so run the chain from scarce beaver to war. Part c asks for an effect, so name the collapse and what it left the Huron unable to do.

### Worked item 3 — apush-saq-0051 (Topic 2.5)

The stimulus is Mary Rowlandson's own words, from her Narrative of the Captivity and Restoration (1682):

> "It is a solemn sight to see so many Christians lying in their blood, some here, and some there, like a company of sheep torn by wolves, all of them stripped naked by a company of hell-hounds, roaring, singing, ranting, and insulting, as if they would have torn our very hearts out; yet the Lord by His almighty power preserved a number of us from death, for there were twenty-four of us taken alive and carried captive."

a. Identify one way Rowlandson describes the Native peoples who attacked Lancaster.

Model answer: Rowlandson describes the attackers as “hell-hounds” and as wolves tearing sheep — as cruel, godless enemies rather than as human beings. Her language turns the attackers into agents of evil, not people with their own reasons for fighting.

b. Explain one way Rowlandson's situation as a captive shaped the way she wrote about Native peoples.

Model answer: Rowlandson wrote as a grieving captive who had just watched her neighbors killed and her daughter wounded, so her account is shaped by trauma and fear. She also wrote for a Puritan audience that expected her suffering to prove God's providence, so she cast the war as a struggle between Christians and heathens.

c. Explain one consequence of King Philip's War for Native peoples in New England.

Model answer: One consequence was the destruction of Native power in New England: Metacom was killed in August 1676, and many survivors were sold into slavery in the West Indies. The war ended organized Native resistance in southern New England and cleared the way for English expansion.

How to think about it: part a asks for Rowlandson's words, so quote the "hell-hounds" and the wolves. Part b asks how her situation shaped her writing, so name the trauma and the audience. Part c asks for a consequence, so name Metacom's death and the enslavement of the survivors.

## Key terms

- **mercantilism** — the policy that a nation's wealth grows by selling more than it buys, with colonies serving the mother country.

- **favorable balance of trade** — exports worth more than imports, the goal of mercantilist policy.

- **Navigation Acts** — English laws from 1651 requiring colonial trade to travel on English ships and through English ports.

- **enumerated goods** — colonial goods, such as tobacco and rice, that could only be sold to England.

- **salutary neglect** — England's policy from the 1720s of leaving the trade laws on the books but barely enforcing them.

- **triangular trade** — the three-legged Atlantic trade: goods to Africa, captives to the Americas, plantation crops to Europe.

- **Middle Passage** — the Atlantic crossing of enslaved Africans to the Americas, in crowded ships below deck.

- **asiento** — the Spanish contract licensing the supply of enslaved people to Spanish America.

- **repartimiento** — the Spanish system in New Spain under which towns supplied a share of their tributaries for labor each week.

- **mita** — the Andean labor draft that took one man in seven for the silver mines of Potosí.

- **praying town** — a New England settlement where Native converts lived under English rule.

- **encomienda** — a Spanish grant giving a Spaniard the right to demand labor from a Native community.
