How did the American economy change from making things to providing services, and who gained and who lost? This lesson answers that question. Factories closed, unions shrank, trade tied America to the world, and a financial crisis tested what the government would do when the system broke.

## Stagflation and a new answer

In the 1970s, the American economy stopped behaving the way economists expected. Inflation and unemployment rose together, a mix called **stagflation**. The old playbook, **Keynesian economics**, held that government spending could steady the economy in a downturn, and that inflation and unemployment should move in opposite directions. When both rose at once, the playbook had no answer.

Ronald Reagan offered a new answer. In February 1981 he told a joint session of Congress that taxes and regulations had grown so heavy they were strangling American enterprise. His program cut taxes, rolled back rules on business, and cut domestic spending. The idea was that freeing businesses from the tax burden would unleash investment, create jobs, and restore growth.

Growth did return, and inflation fell. But the tax cuts plus a sharp rise in military spending left the government spending far more than it collected, and the national debt more than doubled between 1980 and 1986. The new answer had worked on growth, but it had not solved the problem of paying for government.

The debate over the economy was really a debate over what government should do. One side said the government should spend to take up the slack when business slowed. The other said the government itself was the problem, and that cutting taxes and rules would let the market fix itself. That argument would run through every economic crisis that followed.

## The factories close

While Reagan's program took hold, American manufacturing was shrinking. **Deindustrialization**, the loss of factory jobs as firms closed plants or moved production, remade the industrial heartland. The region that had made steel, cars, and appliances became known as the **Rust Belt**, a name for the shuttered mills and empty factories left behind.

Look at the chart: manufacturing's share of nonfarm jobs fell from 25 percent in 1970 to 9 percent in 2010.

![Manufacturing's share of nonfarm jobs, 1970–2010](u9/fig-manufacturing-share-1970-2010.jpg)

Firms moved production south, then abroad, chasing lower wages. The power that followed jobs shifted to the Sun Belt, the warm-weather states of the South and West. But output did not collapse: machines made more goods with fewer workers, so the country kept producing while the factory jobs disappeared.

Look at the photograph: an abandoned section of the Bethlehem Steel rail mill in 1989, weeds growing in the empty yard, no workers or machinery in sight.

![Abandoned Bethlehem Steel rail mill, 1989](u9/bethlehem-steel-abandoned-mill-1989.jpg)

The loss fed resentment that would later power populist revolts against globalization. For families that had counted on factory wages for generations, the new service jobs paid less and offered less security. A job was not only a paycheck; it was a place in a community, and whole towns lost both at once.

## Unions shrink, services grow

As factories closed, unions shrank. Look at the chart: the share of workers in unions fell from 24.1 percent in 1979 to 11.9 percent in 2010.

![Share of workers in unions, 1979–2010](u9/fig-union-rate-1979-2010.jpg)
Figure 3: The share of workers in unions fell from 24.1 percent in 1979 to 11.9 percent in 2010. Chart: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics.

The fall was sharpest in the private sector, where union membership dropped from 16.8 percent in 1983 to 6.9 percent in 2010, while public-sector unions held steady near 36 percent. Reagan's firing of the air-traffic controllers in 1981 had signaled that the federal government would no longer back organized labor the way it had in the New Deal era. Major strikes fell from more than 380 in 1970 to 25 in 2019.

Meanwhile the **service sector**, jobs in retail, health care, finance, and technology rather than making things, grew to about 86 percent of payroll jobs by 2010. Many of the new jobs paid less than the factory jobs they replaced, and wages stagnated for workers without a college degree. The gap between rich and poor widened, even as the economy grew.

The economy had not stopped growing. It had stopped sharing its growth the way it once did. In the decades after World War II, factory wages had risen with productivity, and a single factory job could support a family. After 1980, productivity kept rising, but the gains went mostly to the top.

## Globalization and its critics

**Globalization**, the tying together of the world's economies through trade and investment, sped up in the 1990s. The North American Free Trade Agreement, known as **NAFTA**, removed trade barriers among the United States, Canada, and Mexico. The **World Trade Organization** opened in 1995, setting rules for trade among more than a hundred countries.

President Clinton broke with organized labor to back free trade, arguing that open markets would create jobs and lower prices. American firms linked themselves to suppliers and customers abroad, and consumers bought cheaper goods. But workers in manufacturing saw their jobs move to countries where wages were lower, and they asked who the trade deals were really for.

The critics showed up in Seattle in November 1999, when protesters shut down a meeting of the World Trade Organization. A declaration from the protests argued that trade rules should answer to people, not only to corporations. The 1990s also brought a technology boom: personal computers and the internet created whole new industries, and stock prices soared.

In December 1996, Federal Reserve chairman Alan Greenspan warned that markets might be acting on irrational exuberance. He was right: the **dot-com bubble**, the surge in internet stocks beyond what the companies earned, burst in 2000 and 2001, ending the longest expansion the country had seen to that point.

## The Great Recession

The next crisis was built in layers. Banks made risky **subprime mortgage** loans to buyers with weak credit. Wall Street bundled those loans into investments sold around the world. The 1999 repeal of most of the **Glass-Steagall Act** had removed the wall between everyday banking and risky investing, so the risk spread through the whole financial system.

When house prices fell in 2007, the layers collapsed together. Banks stopped lending, businesses cut jobs, and foreclosures threw families out of their homes. Unemployment rose from 4.6 percent in 2007 to 9.6 percent in 2010. The downturn, known as the **Great Recession**, was the worst since the Depression of the 1930s.

The government intervened on a scale not seen since the 1930s. In 2008 Congress approved a $700 billion program to buy troubled assets from banks. In 2009 it passed a stimulus of almost $800 billion, including about $288 billion in tax relief. The rescue steadied the banks, but the recovery was slow, and many Americans felt the government had saved Wall Street while leaving them behind.

That anger fed populist fury on both sides: the Tea Party on the right and Occupy Wall Street on the left. The lesson of the decade was that growth alone did not spread its gains evenly, and that a crisis built in the financial system could reach every household in the country.

## Key terms

- **stagflation** — inflation and unemployment rising together, as in the 1970s.

- **Keynesian economics** — the theory that government spending can steady the economy in a downturn.

- **deindustrialization** — the loss of factory jobs as firms closed plants or moved production.

- **Rust Belt** — the former industrial heartland of shuttered mills and empty factories.

- **service sector** — jobs in retail, health care, finance, and technology rather than making things.

- **globalization** — the tying together of the world's economies through trade and investment.

- **NAFTA** — the North American Free Trade Agreement, removing trade barriers among the United States, Canada, and Mexico.

- **World Trade Organization** — the body that opened in 1995 to set rules for trade among nations.

- **dot-com bubble** — the surge in internet stocks beyond what the companies earned, bursting in 2000–2001.

- **subprime mortgage** — a loan made to a buyer with weak credit.

- **Glass-Steagall Act** — the law whose 1999 repeal removed the wall between everyday banking and risky investing.

- **Great Recession** — the downturn of 2007–2009, the worst since the Depression of the 1930s.
