When did slavery become the law in Virginia — and was it built all at once, or one ruling at a time? This lesson answers that question. The answer is that it was built gradually: in court cases, in assembly laws, and in a rebellion that frightened planters into finishing the job.

## Seven years and a small grant

Most people who crossed to the Chesapeake in the 1600s did not come free. They came as **indentured servants**, signing away years of their lives for passage across the ocean. The usual term was seven years, and the work was hard — field labor from dawn to dark, under a master who owned the contract and could sell it on.

Nearly half of them died before their terms ended. Disease, hunger, and overwork killed servants in their first years, so most never lived to collect the small land grant and tools promised at freedom's end. Virginia ran on people it used up and replaced.

The **headright** system fed the flow. From 1618, anyone who paid for a passage to Virginia received 50 acres of land per person brought over. Planters who shipped servants collected the land; the servants collected the risk. The system made servants profitable twice over — once as labor, once as land.

In 1619, about twenty Africans arrived in Virginia. Their status was not yet fixed by law: no statute yet said their bondage was for life, or that their children would be born enslaved. The law would catch up to the practice over the next fifty years — case by case, and then code by code.

## Two court cases

The first case was John Punch's, in 1640. Punch ran away with two white servants; when all three were caught, the white men received four more years of service — and Punch, the Black man, was sentenced to serve for the rest of his natural life. The court drew a line by race, and the line held.

The second case is often dated wrong. Barron's dates the John Casor case to 1640, but that confuses Casor with Punch. Casor's case was decided on March 8, 1655: Anthony Johnson, a free Black planter, sued to keep Casor as his servant for life, and the court ruled for Johnson. A Black man could own, and a Black man could be owned — the law was fixing status one ruling at a time.

Read the two cases together and the pattern shows. In 1640, running away cost a Black man his freedom for life. In 1655, a court said a Black servant could be held for life. No law yet said all Black people were slaves for life — but the courts were building that law, ruling by ruling, before the assembly ever wrote it down.

The cases matter because they show slavery arriving gradually, not all at once. The 1619 arrivals were not yet slaves for life; the 1640 and 1655 rulings made lifetime bondage real for the men they named. The colony's law was learning to divide people by race, and each ruling taught it a little more.

## The law hardens — before Bacon

In 1662, Virginia's assembly passed the **partus rule**: a child's status followed the mother. The rule created **hereditary slavery**: if the mother was enslaved, the child was born enslaved — no matter who the father was. The rule made slavery hereditary, and it made enslaved women's children the planter's property from birth.

Five years later came the baptism law of 1667: baptism did not free an enslaved person. Some enslaved people had sought baptism hoping it would end their bondage; the assembly closed that door. Faith could save the soul, the law said, but it could not free the body.

Both laws came before Bacon's Rebellion of 1676. That order matters: the colony was already hardening slavery into a hereditary system before the rebellion frightened planters into finishing the job. Only the great code of 1705 came after Bacon — the laws of 1662 and 1667 were already on the books when the rebels burned Jamestown.

Look at the painting. It shows enslaved laborers curing tobacco under open sheds in 1670, a planter's house behind them. Its place is unknown, and the palm trees suggest the Caribbean, so it cannot prove what Virginia's sheds looked like. What it can show is the work itself: hand labor, leaf by leaf, under the sheds.

## Bacon's Rebellion

In 1676, **Bacon's Rebellion** began on Virginia's frontier, where Nathaniel Bacon raised an army of 400 to 500 men. They wanted war against Native peoples; Governor Berkeley refused to let them fight, so they fought Berkeley instead. The rebels attacked the Pamunkey and the Occoneechee — peoples at peace with the colony — and then burned Jamestown to the ground.

The rebellion ended as suddenly as it began. Bacon died of dysentery, and Berkeley hanged 23 of the rebels. But the fright lasted longer than the fighting. Servants, **freedmen**, and enslaved Black people had fought side by side against the governor, and Virginia's planters never forgot the sight of a united poor.

Historians argue about what the planters learned. Edmund Morgan argued that the rebellion taught planters to divide their workers by race — to give poor whites a stake in slavery so they would never unite with Black laborers again. Others argue that race ideas came first, and the laws only followed. Either way, after 1676 the laws moved fast toward a system that divided workers by race.

The economics moved the same way. The supply of English servants was shrinking, and the price of enslaved Africans fell after the Dutch monopoly on the slave trade ended in 1682. Planters who wanted labor that could never leave, never age out, and never unite against them found it in enslaved Africans — and the law gave them the system to hold it.

## Slavery, North and South

By 1740, slavery looked different in every region. The table shows how far apart they stood.

| Place | Enslaved people, c. 1740 |
|---|---|
| South Carolina | about 70% of the population |
| Virginia | about 40% of the population |
| New York | about 20% of the inhabitants |

Slavery in the North was smaller but still real. Enslaved people in New York and New England worked as domestic servants, sailors, and artisans' assistants, and lived in about 40 percent of New York households. Northern slavery was personal and household-based, not plantation-based — but it was slavery all the same.

In the South, slavery was the economy. Virginia's tobacco and South Carolina's rice were grown by enslaved labor on a scale the North never matched, and South Carolina's Black majority lived under laws written by a white minority that feared them. The 1705 **slave code** gathered Virginia's slave laws into one set of statutes, defining enslaved people as property — **real estate** — and restricting their movement, assembly, and legal rights.

The numbers also show why the laws differed. Where enslaved people were a majority, the laws were written in fear; where they were a household few, the laws were written for control of a different kind. The next section shows what happened when the enslaved resisted, and who first said the system was wrong.

## Resistance and the first protest

Enslaved people practiced **resistance** every day. They worked slowly, broke tools, and kept their African names — small acts that cost the planter time and money and kept a self the law could not take. Resistance did not need a rebellion to be real; it lived in the work itself.

In September 1739, resistance became the **Stono Rebellion**. On September 9, about twenty enslaved people gathered near the Stono River in South Carolina and marched toward Florida, where Spain offered freedom to those who reached it. The group grew to about a hundred before the militia caught them. South Carolina answered with the Negro Act of 1740 and a prohibitive duty that cut imports for about a decade.

In 1688, four German Quakers at Germantown, Pennsylvania, wrote a petition against slavery — the earliest organized antislavery statement in English America. Their meeting set it aside, unwilling to act.

The same faith could argue the other way. George Whitefield, the great revival preacher, defended slavery from the Bible in 1751 — the same Christianity, opposite conclusions. Faith did not settle the question of slavery; it gave both sides their language.

Look at the engraving of John Rolfe hoeing tobacco, published in 1874. It imagines the 1610s, but it was drawn more than two centuries later, and no one in 1874 had seen Rolfe farm. Like Pyle's Jamestown and Darley's King Philip's War, it is evidence of how later Americans pictured the past — not of the past itself.

## Worked items

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0054 (Topic 2.6)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage: after Bacon's Rebellion in 1676, Virginia's planters turned increasingly to enslaved Africans as their primary labor force. The rebellion had united poor Virginians across race, and planters wanted a workforce that could never unite that way again. At the same time, the supply of English servants was shrinking, and the price of enslaved Africans fell after the Dutch monopoly on the slave trade ended in 1682.

a. Identify one reason Virginia planters shifted from indentured servants to enslaved Africans after 1676.

Model answer: One reason was fear: Bacon's Rebellion had united servants, freedmen, and enslaved Black people against the governor, and planters wanted a workforce that could never unite across race again. A workforce held for life, divided from poor whites by race, promised planters that security.

b. Explain how Bacon's Rebellion contributed to that shift.

Model answer: The rebellion showed planters that a mixed workforce of poor whites and Black laborers could unite against them, since servants, freedmen, and enslaved people fought together against Governor Berkeley. Planters answered by building a system of lifetime, hereditary slavery that divided workers by race, so the poor could never unite that way again.

c. Explain one way Virginia's laws changed to support the new labor system.

Model answer: Virginia's laws made slavery hereditary and permanent: the 1662 law said a child's status followed the mother, so children of enslaved women were born enslaved. The 1705 code then gathered those laws together, defining enslaved people as property held like real estate.

How to think about it: part a asks for one reason, so name the fear, the shrinking supply, or the falling price. Part b asks how the rebellion contributed, so name the united poor and the planters' answer. Part c asks for a legal change, so name the 1662 rule or the 1705 code.

### Worked item 3 — apush-saq-0058 (Topic 2.6)

The stimulus is the Virginia slave code's own words, from 1705:

> "All Negro, mulatto and Indian slaves within this dominion… shall be held to be real estate."

The code gathered decades of Virginia's slave laws into one set of statutes, defining enslaved people as property and restricting their movement, assembly, and legal rights.

a. Identify what the 1705 code declared about enslaved people.

Model answer: The code declared that enslaved people were real estate — property held like land, passing to heirs like land. It gathered decades of Virginia's slave laws into one code defining enslaved people as property.

b. Explain how the code changed the legal status of enslaved people in Virginia.

Model answer: The code fixed in law what earlier statutes had built piece by piece: enslavement for life, hereditary through the mother, with no legal rights of movement, assembly, or appeal. Where earlier laws had hardened slavery case by case, the 1705 code wrote the whole system down at once.

c. Explain one way the code shows that slavery in Virginia had become a system of racial control by 1705.

Model answer: The code defined enslaved people by race — 'Negro, mulatto and Indian' — and held them as property for life, making race itself the mark of bondage. It was not a law about individual crimes or debts; it was a law about a whole class of people defined by race.

How to think about it: part a asks what the code declared, so quote the real estate line. Part b asks how it changed legal status, so name what it fixed in law at once. Part c asks how it shows racial control, so name the racial categories the code itself uses.

## Key terms

- **indentured servant** — a person who signed away years of labor for passage to America, usually for a seven-year term.

- **headright** — the grant of 50 acres per person brought to Virginia, paid to whoever paid for the passage, from 1618.

- **partus rule** — Virginia's 1662 law that a child's status followed the mother, making slavery hereditary.

- **hereditary slavery** — bondage passed from mother to child by law, so children of enslaved women were born enslaved.

- **slave code** — a set of laws defining enslaved people as property and restricting their movement, assembly, and legal rights, gathered in Virginia in 1705.

- **Bacon's Rebellion** — the 1676 uprising of frontier Virginians under Nathaniel Bacon, ending with the burning of Jamestown.

- **Stono Rebellion** — the September 1739 uprising of enslaved people in South Carolina, marching toward Spanish Florida.

- **real estate** — land and the property held like land; the 1705 code defined enslaved people as real estate.

- **freedmen** — people freed from bound labor, such as servants who had finished their terms.

- **resistance** — opposition to enslavement, from slow work and broken tools to open rebellion.
