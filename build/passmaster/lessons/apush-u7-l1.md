By 1890 the United States had filled its continent, coast to coast. The frontier the census called closed left a new question: what would a country that had always grown by taking land do next? This lesson answers that question. Between 1890 and 1902, Americans argued over empire, fought a short war with Spain, and kept the argument going for years after the fighting stopped.

## A country that had filled its continent

In 1890 the superintendent of the census noted that the frontier line had closed. For a century, Americans had measured progress by new land: farms, towns, and states added across the continent. The Louisiana Purchase, Florida, Texas, Oregon, and California had each widened the map. Now the map was full, and the country had to decide what growth meant without new land to take.

The country that faced this question was already an industrial power. Its factories made more steel and more goods than its people could buy, and its farms grew more wheat and cotton than the home market could use. The Panic of 1893 had shown how fast that system could break: banks failed, railroads went bankrupt, and workers lost their jobs. Some Americans looked overseas for buyers, and some looked for a new mission.

At the same time, the country still thought of itself as a republic that had broken with empire. Its founding story was a revolt against a distant king, and its Constitution said government needed the consent of the governed. The question of the 1890s was whether those ideas still fit a nation with a navy, factories, and markets to protect. The answer would come in argument first, and then in war.

## Why some Americans wanted an empire

**Imperialism** is the policy of extending a country's power over other lands and peoples. In the 1890s, its supporters gave three reasons for it. The first was economic: factories and farms produced more than Americans could buy, so the country needed new markets overseas. The second was strategic: a strong navy needed bases and coaling stations far from home. The third was a sense of duty, shaped by ideas about race and civilization, that Americans should carry their ways to other peoples.

Captain Alfred Thayer Mahan gave the strategic case its most-read form. In The Influence of Sea Power upon History, published in 1890, he argued that great nations had always rested on sea power, and sea power needed colonies for bases and trade. Mahan was a serving naval captain when he wrote, not a retired admiral, and his book was read in navies around the world. His readers in Washington took the lesson to heart: the United States began building a modern steel navy.

Albert Beveridge of Indiana made the economic case in his speech "The March of the Flag" in 1898. He said American factories needed the markets of China, and that the country had a duty to spread its institutions abroad. The **Open Door policy**, announced by Secretary of State John Hay in 1899, asked every power to keep China open to trade with all nations. Profit, power, and duty had joined into one call for empire.

## Why others said no

Opposition to taking colonies drew on the oldest American ideas, and its organized voice was the **Anti-Imperialist League**, formed in 1898. Its supporters said a republic could not rule other peoples without their consent and still call itself a republic. The League included former presidents, labor leaders, and writers. Its members warned that empire abroad would change government at home, giving the president new powers over peoples who could not vote.

Mark Twain put the case in plain words. He had first cheered expansion, then changed his mind when he decided the United States meant to rule the Philippines rather than free them. In an interview in the New York World in October 1900, he said the country did not intend to free the Filipinos but to subjugate them, and that Filipinos should be left to deal with their own questions in their own way. Twain served as a vice president of the Anti-Imperialist League.

Rudyard Kipling gave the other side its most famous poem. "The White Man's Burden," published in McClure's Magazine in February 1899, told Americans to take up the work of ruling new peoples. Supporters quoted it as a call to duty. Critics read the same poem as proof that empire meant ruling people who had not asked to be ruled. The poem became a shorthand for the whole debate, quoted by both sides.

## The road to war in Cuba

Cuba gave the debate a war. Cuban rebels had fought Spain for independence since 1895, and Spain's governor answered with **reconcentration**, a policy that crowded Cuban civilians into camps near towns so the rebels could not feed off the countryside. Disease and hunger killed hundreds of thousands in the camps. American newspapers printed sensational stories of Spanish cruelty, and readers demanded action.

**Yellow journalism** is the name for the sensational, often exaggerated newspaper style that sold papers by shocking readers. Joseph Pulitzer's New York World and William Randolph Hearst's New York Journal competed for readers with lurid stories about Cuba. The papers spoke for the **jingoes**, Americans who demanded war and expansion, and made the public ready for it. When a Spanish diplomat's private letter insulting President McKinley leaked in February 1898, the Journal printed it as the worst insult in the country's history.

Six days later, the battleship Maine exploded in Havana harbor, killing 266 sailors. The cause was never proven, but the press blamed Spain, and the cry "Remember the Maine" spread across the country. Look at the photograph: the wrecked Maine lies low in the water, its hull torn open and one mast still standing. President McKinley asked Congress for war in April 1898, and Congress declared it on April 25.

## A four-month war and a long argument

The war itself was short. American forces landed in Cuba in June 1898, and Spanish forces there surrendered in July. Commodore George Dewey had already destroyed the Spanish fleet in Manila Bay on May 1. Spain agreed to an armistice in August, four months after war was declared. The fighting was brief; the argument lasted years.

The **Treaty of Paris**, signed in December 1898, made the United States an overseas empire. Spain gave up Cuba, and the United States took Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines. Congress had promised in the April 1898 **Teller Amendment** not to annex Cuba. The **Platt Amendment** of 1901 then limited Cuban independence, giving the United States the right to intervene in Cuba. The Philippines had no such promise. Emilio Aguinaldo, who had led Filipinos against Spain, expected independence and instead faced American rule.

The **Philippine-American War** followed, a three-year fight, 1899 to 1902, as American forces put down Aguinaldo's resistance. The war cost far more lives than the war with Spain, most of them Filipino. At home, the election of 1900 turned on empire: William Jennings Bryan campaigned against it and lost to McKinley. The expansionists had won in practice, if not in principle.

Look at the cartoon "School Begins," from Puck magazine in January 1899. Uncle Sam stands as a schoolteacher before four new pupils labeled Philippines, Hawaii, Porto Rico, and Cuba, while the blackboard says the United States must govern its new territories, with or without their consent, until they can govern themselves. The cartoon supports empire, yet it shows the anti-imperialists' question: who decides when a people can govern themselves?

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0205 (Topic 7.3)

The stimulus is a paraphrase of 5 Steps to a 5 2024, Chapter 19, and Barron's Premium 2027, Chapter 9, on the causes and consequences of the Spanish-American War.

> Paraphrase of 5 Steps to a 5 2024, Chapter 19, and Barron's Premium 2027, Chapter 9: In the 1890s the United States grew concerned about Spanish rule in Cuba, where rebels had fought for independence since 1895. Spain's governor crowded Cuban civilians into reconcentration camps, where disease and starvation killed hundreds of thousands. American newspapers printed sensational accounts of Spanish cruelty, and the explosion of the battleship Maine in Havana harbor in February 1898 pushed the public toward war. The United States declared war in April 1898 and won in four months, taking Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines from Spain.

Source: Paraphrase of 5 Steps to a 5 2024, Chapter 19, and Barron's Premium 2027, Chapter 9.

a. Identify one cause of the Spanish-American War.

Model answer: One cause was Spain's reconcentration policy in Cuba, which crowded civilians into camps where disease and starvation killed hundreds of thousands. Another was the yellow press, whose sensational accounts of Spanish cruelty outraged the American public. A third was the explosion of the Maine in February 1898, which the press blamed on Spain.

b. Explain how yellow journalism helped bring about the war.

Model answer: Yellow journalism helped bring about the war by printing sensational, often exaggerated accounts of Spanish cruelty in Cuba. These stories reached ordinary Americans through mass-circulation newspapers and turned public opinion against Spain. By the time the Maine exploded, the public was already primed to blame Spain and demand war.

c. Explain one consequence of the war for the United States' role in the world.

Model answer: One consequence was that the United States became an overseas empire. The war gave it Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines, so America now ruled peoples across the Pacific and the Caribbean. The country had gone from expanding across its own continent to holding colonies overseas.

How to think about it: part a asks for one cause, so name reconcentration, the yellow press, or the Maine and say what it did. Part b asks how yellow journalism helped bring about the war, so show how the papers moved public opinion. Part c asks for one consequence, so name what the war won and how it changed America's place in the world.

Supporters pointed to new markets for American goods, naval bases, and a duty to uplift other peoples. a. Identify one motive for American imperialism in the period 1890-1914.

Model answer: One motive was economic: American factories were producing more goods than Americans could buy, especially after the Panic of 1893, so supporters of empire wanted new markets overseas for those goods. Another was strategic: naval thinkers like Mahan argued that a strong navy needed coaling stations and bases abroad to protect trade.

b. Explain how one argument against imperialism challenged the idea that the United States was spreading liberty.

Model answer: Anti-imperialists argued that ruling the Philippines without the consent of Filipinos contradicted the liberty the United States claimed to spread. The republic was founded on the idea that government needs the consent of the governed, yet empire meant ruling a foreign people who had no vote in American government. Spreading liberty by denying it to Filipinos, they said, was hypocrisy.

c. Explain how the outcome of the debate changed the United States' role in the world.

Model answer: The expansionists won: the United States took the Philippines, Puerto Rico, and Guam in 1898 and became an overseas empire. Before 1898 it had expanded mainly across its own continent, but after the debate it ruled peoples overseas and held bases in the Pacific and Caribbean. The country had become a global power rather than a continental one.

How to think about it: part a asks for one motive, so pick markets or naval bases and say why it mattered. Part b asks how the anti-imperialist case challenged the idea of spreading liberty, so use consent of the governed. Part c asks for the outcome, so name what the United States took in 1898.

## Key terms

- **Imperialism** — the policy of extending a country's power over other lands and peoples.

- **Anti-Imperialist League** — the organization formed in 1898 to oppose taking colonies, on the ground that a republic cannot rule peoples without their consent.

- **Yellow journalism** — sensational, often exaggerated newspaper reporting that sold papers by shocking readers.

- **Jingoes** — Americans who demanded war and expansion.

- **Reconcentration** — Spain's policy in Cuba of crowding civilians into camps to cut the rebels off from food and support.

- **Open Door policy** — the 1899 American call for every power to keep China open to trade with all nations.

- **Teller Amendment** — Congress's 1898 promise that the United States would not annex Cuba.

- **Treaty of Paris** — the December 1898 treaty in which Spain gave up Cuba and the United States took Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines.

- **Platt Amendment** — the 1901 amendment that limited Cuban independence and gave the United States the right to intervene in Cuba.

- **Philippine-American War** — the 1899-1902 war in which American forces put down Filipino resistance to American rule.
