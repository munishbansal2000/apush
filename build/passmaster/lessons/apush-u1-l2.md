Why did Europe, after centuries of looking inward, suddenly sail across an ocean in 1492? This lesson answers that question. The answer is a mix of hunger for Asian goods, a faith that had just finished a long war at home, rivalry between crowns, and new ships and tools that made the crossing possible.

## Gold, God, and glory

Historians often sum up Europe's motives in three words: gold, God, and glory. Gold came first. Europe wanted the spices, silk, and other goods of Asia, and the old overland routes to Asia ran through lands ruled by Muslim powers. After Constantinople fell to the Ottoman Turks in 1453, those routes grew costly and uncertain. A crown that controlled its own sea route to Asia would control the trade, and the wealth that came with it.

Gold meant more than luxury. In a Europe of rival kingdoms, gold and silver bought armies, and armies kept crowns in power. A ruler with metal could hire soldiers, build ships, and pay debts. A ruler without it fell behind. That is why the search for wealth was not greed alone. It was the way a crown stayed a crown.

God mattered too. For centuries, Christian kingdoms in Iberia had fought to push Muslim rule off the peninsula, a long struggle called the **Reconquista**. It ended in January 1492, when Ferdinand and Isabella, the Catholic Monarchs of a newly joined Spain, took Granada, the last Muslim-held city. Columbus landed in the Bahamas that October, nine months later. A crown fresh from a holy war carried its habits with it: conversion was the stated mission, and conquest was the method.

Glory was the third motive, and it worked through rivalry. Portugal was growing rich from trade down the African coast while Spain watched from the shore. Renaissance crowns competed for prestige, and a richer rival was a more dangerous one. Nobody sailed for glory alone, but nobody let a rival grow rich in peace either. When a question asks for the most important cause, lead with economics, and let the other two ride along.

## The tools that made the ocean crossable

Motive alone could not cross an ocean. For centuries, European ships hugged the coast, because they could not sail home against the wind. The **caravel** changed that. It was a small, fast ship with **lateen sails**, triangular sails that let it sail into the wind instead of waiting for a fair one. Portuguese crews could work their way down the African coast and, just as important, sail back home again.

Three more tools completed the kit. The **astrolabe** read latitude from the stars, so a captain could tell how far north or south he had sailed. The compass kept a steady course when land was out of sight. **Portolan charts**, hand-drawn coastal maps that sailors actually trusted, recorded what earlier voyages had found. None of these was new by itself. Together, they made the open ocean a road instead of a wall.

The money behind the voyages came from a prince who never sailed. Henry the Navigator of Portugal spent decades funding voyages down the African coast and hiring cartographers to map what they found. You may have heard that he ran a navigation school at Sagres. That is mostly legend: there was no formal academy, only a patron paying for ships. The money was real. The school is the story.

The investment paid off slowly, then all at once. In 1488, Bartolomeu Dias rounded the southern tip of Africa. Ten years later, Vasco da Gama sailed on to India, the first European to reach Asia by sea. Portugal owned the African route, and Spain had none of its own. That is the gap Columbus stepped into.

## Columbus and the wrong map

Christopher Columbus was a Genoese navigator with a mistaken plan. He believed the earth was smaller than it is, and Asia only a few thousand miles west of the Canary Islands. The real distance was closer to ten thousand miles, with a continent in the way. He got the shape right, the distance wildly wrong.

Portugal checked his numbers and refused him. Its experts knew the earth was too big, and Portugal had already spent decades building its African route. Spain said yes anyway: Ferdinand and Isabella, flush from Granada and eager to catch Portugal, gave Columbus three small ships, the Niña, the Pinta, and the Santa María.

On October 12, 1492, Columbus landed in the Bahamas and named the island San Salvador. He believed he had reached the East Indies, so he called the people he met Indians, and the name stuck for centuries. He made four voyages in all, dying still insistent he had found Asia. Theodor de Bry made the engraving in 1594, a century after the landing, and he never visited the Americas. It shows a European picture of the landing, not the landing itself.

His first report home set the pattern. In a letter to Luis de Santángel in 1493, Columbus described fertile land, gentle people, and gold to be found in great quantity. He wrote to win funding for more voyages, and his glowing account did its work. His journal from the first voyage shows his aim: the people he met, he wrote, would make fine servants, and fifty men could subjugate them all. The passage reaches us only through Las Casas's copy.

## A new world gets a name

Columbus called the lands Asia, but others soon argued they were something else. Amerigo Vespucci, an Italian navigator sailing for Portugal and Spain, wrote letters claiming the western lands were a new world, not part of Asia. In 1507, Martin Waldseemüller published a map that called the western lands America, after Vespucci. It was the first map to use the name.

Johannes Stradanus drew the engraving below around 1600, in a series celebrating new inventions. Amerigo Vespucci stands at the left, holding a flag and an astrolabe, while a Native woman reclines in a hammock and his ships wait at anchor. The image tells you how Europe saw the meeting: the explorer as hero, the land as a woman to be claimed. It is a European imagining, not a record.

![Amerigo Vespucci meets America](u1/5s24-ch06-mcq-01.jpg)
Figure 3: Amerigo Vespucci meets America. Engraving after Johannes Stradanus, about 1600. Metropolitan Museum of Art, public domain.

The name stuck because the idea stuck. Once Europe accepted that a new world existed, the question changed from how to reach Asia to who would own these lands.

Not every European admired what Spain was doing. Bartolomé de Las Casas, a Spanish friar, wrote an account in 1542 charging Spaniards with cruelty toward Native peoples. Protestant printers in England, Holland, and Germany translated it and printed it with violent engravings, like the German title page below from 1665. The image of Spain as uniquely cruel that grew from those printings is called the **Black Legend**, a name first used in 1914. Judge each source by its maker and its purpose.

![Title page of a 1665 German edition of Las Casas's account](u1/original-ctx-u1-05.jpg)

## A line through the ocean

Spain and Portugal both claimed the new lands, so they called on the pope to settle it. Pope Alexander VI, himself Spanish, drew a line of demarcation in 1493 about a hundred leagues west of the Cape Verde islands. Everything west of the line went to Spain, everything east to Portugal. Portugal refused to accept a Spanish pope handing Spain the ocean.

Portugal had leverage to demand a better deal: the stronger navy and all the African charts. In 1494, the two crowns signed the **Treaty of Tordesillas**, moving the line to 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde islands, more than a thousand miles of ocean. West of the line, Spain. East of it, Portugal. When Cabral landed on Brazil's coast in 1500, the bulge of South America sat east of the line, on Portugal's side. That is why Brazil speaks Portuguese today.

No other European power ever accepted the division. France explored early — Verrazzano sailed the Atlantic coast in 1524, Cartier the St. Lawrence in the 1530s — but did not settle until Quebec in 1608. England came later still: religious turmoil at home and war with Spain kept it out until the English navy defeated the Spanish Armada in 1588. Roanoke, settled in 1587, vanished, and no English colony lasted until Jamestown in 1607.

Spain, meanwhile, planted its own flag on the North American coast. In 1565, Pedro Menéndez founded St. Augustine in Florida, destroying the French Fort Caroline nearby. It was a military guard post, protecting the shipping lanes that carried Spanish silver home. England's answer came a generation later, when James I granted the First Charter of Virginia in 1606 to a **joint-stock company**, whose investors shared the cost and the risk of a colony.

## Worked items

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0009 (Topic 1.3)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage: in the late 1400s, Europeans wanted the spices and silks of Asia, but the overland routes ran through lands ruled by Muslim powers and grew costly after Constantinople fell in 1453, while new ships and tools, the caravel, the compass, the astrolabe, and portolan charts, made long ocean voyages possible.

a. Identify one economic motive that led Europeans to seek new sea routes to Asia.

Model answer: One economic motive was the demand for Asian spices and silks. Europeans wanted these goods but could reach them only through costly overland routes.

b. Explain how one new ship or tool helped make long ocean voyages possible.

Model answer: The caravel's lateen sails let ships sail against the wind, so Portuguese crews could work their way down the African coast and back home again. Without a ship that could sail into the wind, the long coastal voyages would have been impossible.

c. Explain one way the search for sea routes to Asia changed European rivalries.

Model answer: The search turned trade into a race between crowns, as Portugal built its African route and Spain funded Columbus to find a western one. Rivalry over routes, not only over land, became a cause of conflict between European powers.

How to think about it: part a asks for one motive, so name the demand for Asian spices and silks and the cost of the overland routes. Part b asks how a tool helped, so pick the caravel and explain what its lateen sails let ships do. Part c asks for a change in rivalries, so show how the search for routes turned trade into a race between crowns.

### Worked item 2 — apush-saq-0010 (Topic 1.3)

The stimulus is a paraphrase of Columbus's letter to Luis de Santángel, written in 1493 after his first voyage: Columbus wrote that the islands were fertile and well watered, the people gentle, timid, and well built, with no iron weapons, giving freely what they had, and that gold could be found in great quantity.

a. Identify one claim Columbus makes about the peoples he met.

Model answer: Columbus claims the peoples he met were gentle and timid, and that they had no iron weapons and gave freely what they had. He presents them as unarmed and welcoming, a claim that shaped how his readers imagined the new lands.

b. Explain one purpose of Columbus's letter.

Model answer: Columbus wrote to report his voyage to the Spanish monarchs and to persuade them that the lands were worth further voyages. His glowing account of fertile land and gold served that purpose, since it promised profit from more expeditions.

c. Explain one way Columbus's account shaped later European action in the Americas.

Model answer: His reports of gold and gentle peoples encouraged Spain to fund more voyages and to claim the lands. The picture he painted of unarmed, welcoming peoples also helped justify conquest, since it suggested the lands could be taken cheaply.

How to think about it: part a asks for one claim, so take it straight from the letter: gentle, timid, unarmed, giving freely. Part b asks for a purpose, so ask what Columbus wanted from his readers: funding for more voyages. Part c asks for an effect, so follow the letter's picture of gold and gentle peoples to the voyages and claims that followed.

### Worked item 3 — apush-saq-0011 (Topic 1.3)

The stimulus is a painting by Dioscoro Puebla, 1862, imagining Columbus's landing in 1492: Columbus stands at the center holding a Spanish flag, armed men around him, Indigenous people watching from the trees at the right, ships at anchor behind. The painting was made nearly four centuries after the event it shows.

a. Identify one thing the painting shows about Columbus's landing.

Model answer: The painting shows Columbus standing at the center of the landing party, holding a Spanish flag while armed men stand around him. Indigenous people watch from the trees at the right of the scene.

b. Explain what the painting reveals about how 19th-century viewers saw Columbus.

Model answer: The painting places Columbus at the center as a heroic figure carrying the flag, which shows that 19th-century viewers celebrated him as a founder of the Americas. The image honors the landing rather than questioning it, so it tells us about 1862 as much as about 1492.

c. Explain one limit of the painting as evidence about the landing in 1492.

Model answer: The painting was made in 1862, nearly four centuries after the landing, by an artist who was not there. It shows what a 19th-century painter imagined, not what the shore looked like in 1492.

How to think about it: part a asks what the painting shows, so describe what is in the frame: Columbus at the center with the flag, armed men, watchers in the trees. Part b asks what the painting reveals about 1862, so read the hero at the center as a 19th-century celebration. Part c asks for a limit, so count the centuries between 1492 and 1862 and say what a painter who was not there cannot show.

## Key terms

- **caravel** — a small, fast ship with lateen sails, built for long ocean voyages.

- **lateen sails** — triangular sails that let a ship sail into the wind.

- **astrolabe** — an instrument that reads latitude from the stars.

- **portolan charts** — hand-drawn coastal maps that sailors trusted.

- **Reconquista** — the centuries-long Christian reconquest of Iberia, completed at Granada in 1492.

- **Treaty of Tordesillas** — the 1494 agreement dividing new lands between Spain and Portugal along a line 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde islands.

- **joint-stock company** — a company whose investors shared the cost and the risk of a colony.

- **Black Legend** — the image of Spain as uniquely cruel, spread by its Protestant rivals.
