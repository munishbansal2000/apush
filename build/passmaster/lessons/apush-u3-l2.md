Why did a quarrel over taxes become a revolution? This lesson answers that question. Britain tried to make the colonies pay for the empire, and the colonists answered that only their own assemblies could tax them.

## Paying for victory

The Seven Years' War left Britain with a debt so large that the interest alone consumed more than half of the government's annual revenue. Ministers concluded that the American colonies, which had benefited most from the victory, should contribute to the cost of their own defense. The colonies, who had fought alongside British regulars and borne the war's frontier hardships, answered that they had already paid — in blood, in supplies, and in the taxes their own assemblies had levied.

Parliament began with the **Sugar Act** of 1764. It lowered the duty on foreign molasses from six pence a gallon to three — but it enforced collection for the first time, and sent smuggling cases to admiralty courts without juries. A law that lowered a tax still angered colonists, because it reached into their trade and took away the jury trial. The Currency Act of the same year forbade the colonies to issue paper money.

Then came the **Stamp Act** of 1765, the first tax Parliament imposed directly on the colonies. It touched nearly everyone who handled paper — lawyers, printers, merchants, and ordinary readers. John Dickinson drew the line the colonists would hold: every earlier law had regulated trade, while the Stamp Act was the first meant to raise revenue in America. Regulation the colonists accepted; revenue without their consent they would not.

## Resistance from above and below

That October, nine colonies sent delegates to New York for the Stamp Act Congress, the first time colonial assemblies had met together to answer a British policy. The delegates agreed that only their own assemblies could tax them, and they sent petitions to the king and to Parliament asking for repeal. In the streets, the **Sons of Liberty** harassed stamp agents, crowds attacked the houses of officials, and merchants signed nonimportation agreements refusing British goods.

Resistance reached beyond political leaders. In 1774, fifty-one women in Edenton, North Carolina, signed a declaration giving up tea and other British products. Across the colonies, women organized spinning bees to make homespun cloth. In Boston, shoemaker Ebenezer Macintosh led crowd actions against Stamp Act officials. **Committees of Correspondence** spread the news from town to town and coordinated the resistance.

Look at the cartoon "The Colonies Reduced," published in London in 1768. In the top panel, Britannia sits slumped against a globe, her limbs cut off and lying on the ground around her, each labeled with a colony name. Below, America runs into the arms of the French king. The cartoonist is warning British readers that taxing the colonies without their consent would weaken Britain itself — and that Britain's rivals would profit from the breakup.

![The Colonies Reduced, 1768](u3/barrons-2027-ch05-04.jpg)
Figure 1: "The Colonies Reduced," Political Register, London, 1768. Britannia dismembered, her limbs labeled with colony names. Public domain.

Parliament repealed the Stamp Act early in 1766, but it passed the **Declaratory Act** at the same time, asserting its right to legislate for the colonies "in all cases whatsoever." The colonists had won the battle over the stamp tax, but not the war of principles over Parliament's powers. The next round would test whether the distinction between regulating trade and raising revenue could hold.

## Boston: massacre and tea

It could not. The **Townshend Acts** of 1767 placed duties on imported glass, lead, paper, paint, and tea. Townshend assumed the colonists would accept duties on imports, but Dickinson answered that if Parliament may levy a tax of one penny, it may levy a million: the size of the tax does not change its character. The colonists rejected the distinction between internal and external taxes and answered with nonimportation agreements.

Townshend also used some of the revenue to pay the salaries of royal governors and other officials, removing the assemblies' power to withhold salaries to influence policy. When resistance continued, Britain stationed regular troops in Boston to enforce the customs laws, and tensions rose.

On March 5, 1770, British soldiers fired into a crowd in King Street, killing five Bostonians, including Crispus Attucks. Paul Revere's engraving, "The Bloody Massacre," showed a neat line of soldiers firing a single ordered volley — propaganda, not a record, since the soldiers fired in confusion after being pelted with snow and ice. Copies reached other colonies and London within weeks. At the trial, John Adams defended the soldiers, and the accused got a fair trial.

## The Coercive Acts and the First Congress

The Tea Act of 1773 angered colonists even though it made tea cheaper, because it gave the East India Company a monopoly that bypassed colonial merchants and undercut their business. On December 16, 1773, men dressed as Mohawk Indians threw tea chests into Boston Harbor.

Parliament answered with the **Coercive Acts** of 1774: the Boston Port Act closed the harbor until the tea was paid for, the Massachusetts Government Act gave the king the choice of councillors, the Administration of Justice Act moved trials of accused officials out of Massachusetts, and a stricter Quartering Act put soldiers in civilian homes. Parliament also passed the Quebec Act the same year, extending Quebec's boundaries westward. The acts were meant to isolate Massachusetts and make an example of it.

Instead, other colonies saw them as a threat to their own liberties, and the First Continental Congress met in Philadelphia in September and October 1774 — fifty-six delegates from every colony except Georgia.

It agreed to a **Continental Association**, a pledge of nonimportation, nonexportation, and nonconsumption, enforced by local Committees of Safety, and it petitioned the king for repeal. Most delegates still sought reconciliation within the empire.

## The ideas behind the break

The resistance had a theory behind it, and the theory came from the Enlightenment. John Locke's theory of **natural rights**, set out in 1689, held that all men are by nature free, equal, and independent, and no one can be subjected to political power without his own consent. Government exists to preserve the people's property — life, liberty, and estate. When legislators destroy property or reduce people to slavery under arbitrary power, the people may resist.

Montesquieu, writing in 1748, argued that liberty depends on separating the powers of government. When legislative and executive powers are united in the same person or body, there can be no liberty, because the same body could enact tyrannical laws and execute them tyrannically. The Constitution Americans later wrote would divide power among three branches for exactly this reason.

**Republicanism** gave the theory a moral edge. Americans agreed the new government would be a republic — sovereignty resting with the people, not a monarch — a radical move. Republican citizens were expected to put the community's interests above their own, and writers worried that luxury and corruption would rot a republic from within. Evangelical preaching shared the language of liberty from bondage.

Thomas Paine carried these ideas to readers. In Common Sense, published in January 1776, he called government in its best state "a necessary evil" and argued that the form most likely to ensure security was preferable to all others. He wrote in plain language ordinary colonists could follow, and Common Sense sold more than 100,000 copies in its first three months. Paine also gave the cause a wider meaning: freedom had been hunted round the globe, and America could be an asylum for mankind.

## Declaring independence

By July 1775, fighting had begun, yet the Second Continental Congress still sent the Olive Branch Petition to King George III, blaming the troubles on Parliament. The king rejected it without reading it. Historians estimate that about 40 to 45 percent of colonists were Patriots who wanted independence, about 15 to 20 percent were Loyalists who wanted to keep ties with Britain, and the rest were uncommitted. Independence was not a foregone conclusion.

Congress voted for independence on July 2, 1776, and announced the Declaration on July 4. Its preamble holds that all men are created equal, endowed with unalienable rights among them life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness. Jefferson adapted Locke, replacing property with a broader right. Governments derive their just powers from the **consent of the governed**, and when a government becomes destructive of these ends, the people may alter or abolish it and institute a new one.

John Trumbull's painting of the Declaration was commissioned in 1817 and completed in 1818. It shows the Committee of Five presenting its draft to Congress. The painting is a staged commemoration, not a record of June 28, 1776, but it shows how Americans built a shared heroic story about their founding.

![John Trumbull, Declaration of Independence, 1818](u3/pr25e-test2-q14.jpg)
Figure 3: John Trumbull, "Declaration of Independence," 1818. The Committee of Five presents its draft to Congress. Public domain.

The Declaration's ideals reached beyond its authors' intentions. Its natural-rights language seemed to challenge slavery, and some enslaved people fled to the British or won freedom by serving. Most Native Americans supported the British, fearing the land hunger of the new nation. And revolutionary ideals inspired **republican motherhood**: mothers should be educated to raise sons as citizens of the republic — a civic role for women that still kept them out of politics.

## Worked items

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0072 (Topic 3.3)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage: the Stamp Act of 1765 was the first tax Parliament imposed directly on the colonies, nine colonies sent delegates to New York that October, and crowds, boycotts, and committees carried the argument into the streets.

a. Identify ONE action the Stamp Act Congress took in response to the Stamp Act.

Model answer: The Stamp Act Congress adopted a Declaration of Rights and Grievances, asserting that only the colonial assemblies could tax the colonists. It also sent petitions to the king and to Parliament asking for the Stamp Act to be repealed. By acting together, the nine delegations turned nine separate complaints into one colonial position.

b. Explain ONE reason colonists objected to the Stamp Act.

Model answer: Colonists objected because the Stamp Act was the first tax Parliament levied directly on them, rather than a duty on trade they could avoid by not buying. It also fell hardest on the very people who wrote and argued about public affairs — lawyers, printers, and merchants — so the protest was organized by men who knew how to make a case. Above all, colonists held that a tax laid by a Parliament in which they had no elected representatives violated their rights as Englishmen.

c. Explain ONE way the Stamp Act crisis changed colonial politics.

Model answer: The crisis taught the colonies to act together: the Stamp Act Congress was the first meeting of colonial assemblies called to answer a British policy, and it set a pattern for the Continental Congresses that followed. It also widened politics beyond the assemblies, as the Sons of Liberty, boycotts, and Committees of Correspondence drew artisans, women, and ordinary townspeople into the resistance. After 1765, opposing British policy was no longer a matter for governors and assemblies alone.

How to think about it: Part a asks for one action of the Stamp Act Congress, so name what the delegates agreed or sent. Part b asks for one reason colonists objected, so start with what made this tax different from earlier duties. Part c asks for one change in colonial politics, so name who joined the resistance and what pattern the Congress set.

### Worked item 2 — apush-saq-0074 (Topic 3.3)

The stimulus is Paul Revere's engraving The Bloody Massacre, printed in Boston in March 1770: a line of British soldiers firing a volley into a crowd in King Street, five men fallen in the foreground, the caption naming the dead and calling the event a massacre.

a. Identify ONE feature of the engraving that presents the Boston Massacre as a deliberate killing.

Model answer: Revere arranges the soldiers in a neat line, firing together through a single cloud of smoke, as if on an officer's order. The officer behind them raises a sword, which reads as the signal to fire, and the victims fall in a row before them. Nothing in the scene suggests confusion or a scuffle — the image shows a planned volley, not a street fight.

b. Explain ONE reason Patriots circulated images like this one.

Model answer: Patriots circulated the engraving to turn a confusing street confrontation into a clear story of British cruelty, one that could travel faster than any report. Copies reached other colonies and London within weeks, where readers who had not been in Boston could see the "massacre" for themselves. The image recruited sympathy and support for the colonial cause far beyond the town where the shooting happened.

c. Explain ONE limitation of the engraving as evidence of what happened on March 5, 1770.

Model answer: The engraving is propaganda, not a record: the soldiers fired in confusion after being pelted with snow and ice, and Captain Preston denied giving any order to fire. Revere, who was a Son of Liberty, drew the scene to make the soldiers look like murderers, and he printed it within weeks to shape opinion. A historian can use it to show how Patriots framed the event, but not to reconstruct what the soldiers actually did.

How to think about it: Part a asks for one feature of the engraving, so describe what you can see: the line, the officer, the fallen men. Part b asks why Patriots circulated it, so think about what an image can do that a report cannot. Part c asks for a limit, so ask who made it, when, and what the trial showed instead.

### Worked item 3 — apush-saq-0075 (Topic 3.4)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage: Enlightenment thinkers argued government gains legitimacy from consent and exists to protect natural rights, Jefferson drew on Locke in the Declaration, yet slavery continued, women could not vote, and Native peoples lost land.

a. Identify ONE Enlightenment idea in the passage.

Model answer: One Enlightenment idea in the passage is that government gains its legitimacy from the consent of the governed. Locke argued that people are naturally free and equal, and that no one can be subjected to political power without his own consent.

b. Explain how that idea appears in the Declaration of Independence.

Model answer: The idea appears in the Declaration's preamble, which says governments derive their just powers from the consent of the governed. Jefferson also echoes Locke's natural rights, writing that all men are created equal and endowed with unalienable rights, including life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness.

c. Explain ONE way the reach of the Declaration's ideals was limited.

Model answer: The ideals were limited because slavery continued after 1776, even though the Declaration claimed all men are created equal. Jefferson's first draft blamed slavery on Britain, but Congress removed that passage, and enslaved people won freedom only piecemeal, such as by serving in the army in the North.

How to think about it: Part a asks for one Enlightenment idea in the passage, so name the idea about consent or rights. Part b asks how it appears in the Declaration, so quote the preamble's own words. Part c asks for one limit, so name a group the ideals did not reach and what happened to them.

## Key terms

- **Sugar Act** — Parliament's 1764 law that lowered the molasses duty but enforced collection for the first time, with trials in admiralty courts.

- **Stamp Act** — Parliament's 1765 direct tax on paper goods in the colonies, the first tax meant to raise revenue rather than regulate trade.

- **Townshend Acts** — the 1767 duties on imported glass, lead, paper, paint, and tea, which colonists rejected as revenue taxes.

- **Declaratory Act** — Parliament's 1766 act asserting its right to legislate for the colonies "in all cases whatsoever."

- **Sons of Liberty** — colonial groups that organized resistance to British policies, from harassing stamp agents to the Boston Tea Party.

- **Committees of Correspondence** — colonial networks that spread news and coordinated resistance from town to town.

- **Coercive Acts** — Parliament's 1774 laws punishing Massachusetts, including the Boston Port Act and the Massachusetts Government Act.

- **Continental Association** — the First Continental Congress's 1774 pledge of nonimportation, nonexportation, and nonconsumption.

- **natural rights** — rights people hold by nature, such as life, liberty, and property, which government exists to protect.

- **consent of the governed** — the idea that governments derive their just powers from the consent of the people they govern.

- **republicanism** — the belief that sovereignty rests with the people and citizens must put the community's interests above their own.

- **republican motherhood** — the idea that mothers should be educated to raise sons as virtuous citizens of the republic.
