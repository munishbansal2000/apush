What happens when a country that farms for itself starts buying and selling across a continent? In the early 1800s, the United States did exactly that. New machines, new roads and canals, and new ways of paying for work turned local economies into one national market. This lesson follows that change, called the **market revolution**, and the people who lived through it.

## Cut off from Britain: embargo, machines, and mills

The change began with a quarrel. In 1807, President Jefferson's Embargo Act cut off trade with Britain and France, and the War of 1812 kept British goods out of American ports. Jefferson had meant to punish Europe. Instead, he forced Americans to make their own cloth, tools, and household goods.

When the war ended in 1815, British goods flowed back. Congress answered with the Tariff of 1816, a 25 percent duty on imported manufactured goods. The tariff gave American makers room to grow.

Before factories, most manufacturing ran through the **putting-out system**. Merchants gave raw materials to farm families, who spun thread or stitched shoe parts at home for pay. The factory gathered that scattered work under one roof, where machines spun and wove and workers tended them.

Two inventions by one man show how fast the change ran. Eli Whitney's cotton gin, built in 1793, cleaned short-staple cotton far faster than human hands. Southern planters could then grow cotton for the mills. Whitney's second idea was **interchangeable parts**: musket parts made to exact patterns, so any part fit any musket. Clock, watch, and tool makers soon used it.

Look at the print below: Lowell, Massachusetts, a factory town whose brick mills stand along the Merrimack River, whose falling water powered them.

![View of Lowell, Massachusetts, 1840-1860](u4/barrons-2027-ch06-02.jpg)
Figure 1: View of Lowell, Massachusetts, 1840-1860. Brick mills line the Merrimack River. Public domain.

Lowell's mills ran on the **Waltham-Lowell system**, which put every step of making cloth under one roof, with workers housed in company boardinghouses. The workers were mostly young farm women, paid cash wages for a few years before returning home. By the early 1830s, more than 40,000 women worked in New England textile mills. The mill girls even published their own magazine, the Lowell Offering.

## Moving goods, growing cotton

Machines were only half the revolution: goods had to move, and **internal improvements** — roads, canals, and later railroads — moved them. The Erie Canal, finished in 1825 and built by New York State, joined the Hudson to the Great Lakes. Freight from Buffalo to New York City cost about 90 percent less, making New York the nation's commercial capital. New York built it because presidents doubted the Constitution allowed federal spending on internal improvements.

Look at the canal boat below, traveling at night, lanterns lit.

![View on the Erie Canal, 1832](u4/pr25e-ch08-q10.jpg)
Figure 2: View on the Erie Canal, 1832. Public domain.

Steam did the same work on the rivers: Fulton's Clermont ran up the Hudson in 1807, and within twenty years steamboats dominated river and canal shipping. Railroads came next; by 1853 New York and Chicago were linked by rail — though different track widths kept cars from running line to line. The telegraph, first used by Samuel Morse in 1844, moved news in minutes along lines usually built beside railroad tracks.

Each region sold what it produced best — regional specialization: the North built banks, mills, and ships, the South grew cotton — **King Cotton**. Production rose from about 700,000 bales in 1830 to nearly five million by 1860, 58 percent of exports.

The regions needed each other: Northern mills spun Southern cotton, and New York merchants and banks bought, sold, financed it — on opposite kinds of labor, free wage workers in the North and enslaved workers in the South, and as cotton spread, so did slavery.

The new economy was fragile: easy credit fed a western land boom, and when the Second Bank called in its loans, the Panic of 1819 followed, borrowers losing farms and shops — a crash in one place now spreading everywhere.

## Newcomers: immigrants and settlers remake the map

The market revolution moved people as well as goods. Between 1821 and 1850, immigration to the United States rose from about 143,000 in a decade to more than 1.7 million. Most newcomers came from northern and western Europe — above all the Irish and the Germans. John L. O'Sullivan called America a 'nation of many nations,' and the numbers backed him. But the celebration sat uneasily with another movement of the same years: the forced removal of Native peoples in the East, driven from their lands as settlers moved west.

Alexis de Tocqueville, visiting from France, found a general equality of conditions that shaped public opinion, laws, and the ordinary practices of life — and voluntary associations Americans formed to pursue common aims.

The Irish came in the greatest wave, driven out by the potato famine of 1845-1852, when blight ruined the crop that fed the Irish poor. About a million Irish starved, and about a million more crossed the Atlantic. Four-fifths settled in Northeast port cities like New York and Boston, building canals and railroads, working in the mills, and crowding into immigrant neighborhoods.

German immigrants, the second largest group, often arrived with skills and savings and moved on past the ports to the **German triangle** of Cincinnati, St. Louis, and Milwaukee. Both groups were met with hostility, above all from workers who feared competition for low-paying jobs. Newcomers who could not read English or worshipped as Catholics found the welcome colder still.

Native-born Americans moved west too. More than four million crossed the Appalachian Mountains between 1800 and 1840. Southern planters carried slavery into Alabama and Mississippi, hoping to rebuild the Cotton Kingdom on cheaper land, while New England families settled the Old Northwest. In time the new settlements' towns and churches resembled the places settlers had left.

## Wages, class, and the first unions

Look at the engraving below: the Boott Cotton Mills at Lowell stretch along the river in long brick rows, far bigger than any workshop of the old craft world.

![Engraving of the Boott Cotton Mills at Lowell, Massachusetts](u4/original-misc-us-culture-03.jpg)
Figure 3: The Boott Cotton Mills at Lowell, Massachusetts. Long brick mill buildings along the river. Public domain.

The market revolution made some rich and left many poor. Merchants, bank owners, and mill owners gathered fortunes, while factory hands and laborers lived just above poverty. On the eve of the Civil War, about 5 percent of Americans controlled half of the country's wealth, sharper than after the Revolution.

Between rich and poor grew a middle class. Banks, corporations, law offices, and transport companies needed clerks, accountants, lawyers, and managers, white-collar jobs that gave families a path upward. In a middle-class home, the wife was expected to keep house rather than earn wages, a mark of having arrived.

For workers, the change meant less independence than the old craft world offered. A farm daughter who tended looms for wages answered to a boss and a clock, not to her family. Yet many workers held to the **free-labor ideology** — wage work as a step toward owning land and becoming independent, free labor making the North better than the slave South.

When pay was cut, workers organized. The Lowell mill women formed the **Factory Girls Association** and struck in 1834 over a wage cut and in 1836 over higher boardinghouse rents. In 1842 the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court's **Commonwealth v. Hunt** declared unions lawful when they used legal means, including striking. The gains stayed limited: the Panic of 1837 and Irish immigrants willing to work for less undercut the strikers.

## Women's lives and the cult of domesticity

Look at the cover below: the August 1845 Lowell Offering shows a well-dressed young woman holding a book, calling itself a repository of articles by 'factory girls.'

![Cover of the Lowell Offering, August 1845](u4/5s24-ch13-mcq-04.png)
Figure 4: Cover of the Lowell Offering, August 1845. Public domain.

The market revolution redrew women's lives. In Lowell, farm daughters earned cash wages and published their own magazine, defending the dignity of their labor. Charles Dickens, visiting in 1842, found that in July 1841 no fewer than 978 of these girls were depositors in the Lowell Savings Bank, with $100,000 saved between them.

But the same decades told middle-class women their place was home. Catharine Beecher's Treatise on Domestic Economy (1841) said women had an equal interest in all concerns, yet must take a subordinate station at home, leaving voting and lawmaking to men. That ideal, the **cult of domesticity**, made the home a woman's separate sphere.

Not everyone accepted the bargain. The English visitor Harriet Martineau wrote in 1837 that the test of a civilisation was the condition of the half of society over which the other half has power, and judged that America had fallen below its own democratic principles in its treatment of women.

The law backed the ideal. Under **feme covert**, a married woman's property became her husband's, and she had no independent legal standing. Women generally could not vote or sit on a jury. The market revolution changed women's work without changing their legal standing — that gap between new work and old law is the story of this topic.

Southern women's lives ran on a different track: enslaved women worked field and house, their families unprotected by law, while planter wives managed households built on enslaved labor. The market revolution gave Northern women wages and the South cotton wealth.

## Key terms

- **Market revolution** — The early-1800s shift from local, semi-subsistence farming to production for distant markets, tied together by transport, credit, and wage labor.

- **Putting-out system** — Manufacturing in which merchants gave raw materials to families who worked on them at home for pay.

- **Waltham-Lowell system** — The factory system that put all stages of textile production under one roof, with workers living in company housing.

- **Interchangeable parts** — Machine parts made to exact patterns, so any part fits any product of the same kind.

- **Internal improvements** — Roads, canals, and railroads built to link regions and move goods, often by state governments.

- **King Cotton** — The name for Southern cotton's dominance: by 1860 it was most of American exports and most of the world's supply.

- **German triangle** — The Midwestern cities of Cincinnati, St. Louis, and Milwaukee, where many German immigrants settled.

- **Free-labor ideology** — The belief that wage work could lead to owning land and independence, and that free labor made the North better than the slave South.

- **Factory Girls Association** — The Lowell mill women's organization, which struck in 1834 over a wage cut and in 1836 over higher rents.

- **Commonwealth v. Hunt** — The 1842 Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court decision declaring unions lawful when they used legal means, including striking.

- **Cult of domesticity** — The ideal that a respectable middle-class woman's place was the home, a separate sphere apart from business and politics.

- **Feme covert** — The legal doctrine under which a married woman had no independent legal standing, and her property became her husband's.

## Worked items

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0110

Paraphrase of Barron's Premium 2027, Chapter 6, Topic 4.5 D: The market revolution affected the North and the South in different ways. The North developed banking, manufacturing, and shipping industries, while the South focused on increasing its cotton production. Both regions were growing economically in the first decades of the nineteenth century, and the growth of each region reinforced the growth of the other.

Cotton was bought and sold in New York City and processed into cloth in New England. Northern banks lent money to plantation owners, and Northern insurance companies insured their slaves.

a) Briefly explain ONE way the growth of cotton production in the South shaped the economy of the North in the period 1800-1848.

Model answer: Southern cotton fed New England textile mills, so mill towns like Lowell grew around spinning that cotton into cloth. Northern merchants in New York bought and sold the crop, and Northern banks lent money to the planters who grew it.

b) Briefly explain a SECOND way the growth of cotton production in the South shaped the economy of the North in the same period.

Model answer: The cotton trade gave work to Northern shipping and insurance. Northern ships carried the fiber to Britain and to New England, and Northern insurance companies insured enslaved workers, so the trade spread beyond the mills into finance and transport.

c) Briefly explain ONE way the growth of cotton production deepened the differences between the North and the South in the same period.

Model answer: Cotton made the South more committed to enslaved labor, since more acres of cotton meant more enslaved workers. The North, meanwhile, moved toward free wage labor in its mills, so the two regions built their wealth on opposite kinds of labor.

The reasoning to practice: parts (a) and (b) must name two different Northern effects, and part (c) must tie a difference to cotton itself, not just list one.

### Worked item 2 — apush-saq-0111

"Dignity of Labor," The Lowell Offering, 1842:

> "From whence originated the idea, that it was derogatory to a lady's dignity, or a blot upon the female character, to labor? . . . Homer tells us of princesses who used to draw water from the springs, and wash with their own hands the finest of the linen of their respective families. The famous Lucretia used to spin in the midst of her attendants; and the wife of Ulysses, after the siege of Troy, employed herself in weaving, until her husband returned to Ithaca. . . . Few American fortunes will support a woman who is above the calls of her family. . . . To be able to earn one's own living by laboring with the hands, should be reckoned among female accomplishments."

a) Briefly identify the group of women who wrote the Lowell Offering.

Model answer: The Lowell Offering was written by the young women working in the textile mills of Lowell, Massachusetts. They were mostly farm daughters who had come to the mills to earn cash wages for a few years.

b) Briefly explain ONE purpose of the essay "Dignity of Labor."

Model answer: The essay defends the respectability of women who earn wages by manual labor. It answers the charge that factory work was degrading to a lady by showing that respected women of the past, from princesses to Lucretia, labored with their hands.

c) Briefly explain ONE limitation of the Lowell Offering as evidence about the lives of all mill workers.

Model answer: The Offering was written by literate young women who chose to write, and the mill owners approved of it. It shows the views of a small group of workers, not the views of all mill hands, and it says little about later immigrant workers or about those who left unhappy.

The reasoning to practice: part (a) names the authors, part (b) gives the essay's purpose from its own argument, and part (c) tests the source's limits by asking whose voices it leaves out.

### Worked item 3 — apush-saq-0112

A secondary text paraphrases Barron's on Topic 4.6: between 1800 and 1860, most immigrants came from northern and western Europe, the largest groups from Ireland and the German states. Four-fifths of Irish immigrants settled in Northeast port cities, while Germans often moved on to Midwestern cities. The market revolution also widened the rich-poor gap: on the eve of the Civil War, about 5 percent of Americans controlled half of the country's wealth.

a) Briefly explain ONE way immigration changed the cities of the North in the period 1800-1848.

Model answer: Irish immigrants crowded into Northeast port cities like New York and Boston, where they took work building canals and railroads and laboring in mills. Their arrival made Northern cities larger, poorer, and more crowded, with whole immigrant neighborhoods forming near the docks and factories.

b) Briefly explain a SECOND way immigration changed the cities of the North in the same period.

Model answer: Immigration gave Northern employers a large supply of workers willing to take low wages, which undercut earlier workers. In Lowell, Irish immigrants replaced the farm daughters in the mills in the 1840s, so the mill workforce became a permanent class of low-paid laborers instead of a passing group of farm girls.

c) Briefly explain ONE way the market revolution widened the gap between the rich and the poor in the same period.

Model answer: The market revolution let merchants, bank owners, and industrialists gather vast fortunes, while factory hands and laborers stayed in low-wage work. By the eve of the Civil War, about 5 percent of Americans controlled half of the country's wealth, so growth enriched a few while the laboring poor crowded into the cities.

The reasoning to practice: parts (a) and (b) must name two different effects of immigration, and part (c) must name a mechanism for the rich-poor gap, not just say the gap grew.
