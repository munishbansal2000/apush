Between 1880 and 1900, more than nine million immigrants arrived in the United States, most of them from places Americans had never seen as home. At the same time, a new **middle class** of office workers, shoppers, and suburban families took shape. This lesson answers one question: who came, how were they received, and who rose in the middle?

## A new pattern of arrival

Before 1880, most immigrants came from northern and western Europe, above all Ireland, Germany, and Britain. The Irish shared a language with Americans, and the Germans came from a society much like America's, so both groups settled in fairly easily. After 1880 the pattern changed. The **new immigrants** came from southern and eastern Europe: Italy, Russia, Poland, and the Balkans. They spoke little English, most were very poor, and many were Catholic or Jewish in a largely Protestant country.

Push factors drove them out: poverty, land hunger, and persecution, including pogroms against Jews in the Russian Empire. Pull factors drew them in: factory jobs, higher wages, and land that seemed cheap beside Europe's crowded fields. Most arrived by steamship at New York, where they passed through Castle Garden until 1890 and then, after a stop at the Barge Office, through Ellis Island, which opened in 1892. The Chinese came by a different route, across the Pacific to the West Coast, where they had helped build the western railroads.

Migration moved Americans too. Black Southerners left the South for Kansas in 1879 and 1880, the **Exodusters**, pushed by violence, poverty, and broken promises, while farm families moved from the countryside into the cities. The country was moving in every direction at once, and the cities were where the streams met.

## The city that received them

The new immigrants crowded into cities, and the cities were not ready. They lived in **tenements**, apartment houses packed with families, often without running water or light. Jacob Riis photographed the worst of it. Look at the photograph: a narrow alley between tall tenement buildings, laundry on lines overhead, and men standing in doorways and on a wooden balcony. Riis called the alley "Bandits' Roost" and published the photograph in 1890, in How the Other Half Lives, to show middle-class readers how the other half lived.

![A narrow alley between tall tenement buildings, laundry on lines overhead](u7/5s24-exam2-mcq-12.jpg)
Figure 1: "Bandits' Roost," Mulberry Street, New York, photographed by Jacob Riis in 1888. Public domain.

Riis counted tenant houses in New York rising from fifteen thousand to thirty-seven thousand, and he linked that growth to the drift of the population to the cities. His point was not that every immigrant lived in a crime-ridden alley. His point was that the city had built housing for profit and left the people inside it to cope. The photograph is evidence of crowding and poverty, not of the character of the people who lived there.

The immigrants changed the cities as much as the cities changed them. They filled the factories, built churches and synagogues, opened shops, and made whole neighborhoods speak Italian, Yiddish, or Polish. In New York, the Lower East Side became the most densely populated place in the world. The city became a patchwork of new Americas, each keeping its language and customs while learning the new country's ways.

## Cheated at the gate

Arrival itself could be dangerous. Look at the cartoon: six swindlers wait in holes with spring-loaded steel jaws for newly arrived immigrants outside Castle Garden, while two policemen look on without helping. Four of their signs are legible: "Money Changer," "Temptress," "Confidence Man," and "Friend from the Old Country."

Frederick Burr Opper drew it for Puck in June 1882, and his point runs against most cartoons of the era. The danger in his drawing is not the immigrant. It is the swindler waiting to rob the immigrant, and the policeman who watches and does nothing.

![Swindlers wait in holes with steel jaws for immigrants outside Castle Garden](u6/original-u6-castle-garden-emigrant-catchers.jpg)
Figure 2: Frederick Burr Opper, "Castle Garden Emigrant-Catchers," Puck, June 14, 1882. Public domain.

The Chinese faced a different welcome: the law itself. The **Chinese Exclusion Act** of 1882 was the first federal law banning immigrants by nationality, and it barred Chinese laborers from entering the country.

Lee Chew, a Chinese immigrant who wrote about his life in 1903, explained that so many Chinese worked in laundries because the work required little capital and was one of the few openings allowed to them. More than half the Chinese in the country, he wrote, would become citizens if allowed to do so. The law would not allow it, and the Chinese could not make America their home on equal terms.

## Nativism answers back

Many native-born Americans answered the new immigration with fear. **Nativism** is the belief that native-born Americans should be favored over immigrants, and in the 1890s it took the form of organized demands for restriction.

Henry Cabot Lodge made the case in the North American Review in January 1891: "Thus it is proved, first, that immigration to this country is increasing, and, second, that it is making its greatest relative increase from races most alien to the body of the American people and from the lowest and most illiterate classes among those races."

Lodge's language reflects **nativism**, the fear that the new arrivals would weaken American identity. Nativists pressed for a literacy test that would bar immigrants who could not read, and the Immigration Restriction League, founded in 1894, carried the campaign. President Cleveland vetoed a literacy test in 1897, but the demand did not go away, and restriction would win in the next century.

William Graham Sumner gave inequality a scientific-sounding defense: **Social Darwinism**, the idea that inequality came from a natural law of competition in which the fittest survived, so the rich owed the poor nothing and government aid would only interfere with nature.

The **settlement house** movement gave a different answer. Jane Addams opened **Hull House** in Chicago in 1889, in an immigrant neighborhood, and offered kindergartens, English classes, clubs, and a place for neighbors to meet. Addams believed a settlement house was needed because the foreign colonies easily isolated themselves, and Hull House could change the neighborhood by bringing neighbors of different classes together in social intercourse and adding a social function to democracy. Where the nativists saw a threat to keep out, the settlement workers saw neighbors to welcome in.

## A new middle class

Industrialization did not only create millionaires and factory hands. It created a new **middle class** of white-collar workers: clerks, bookkeepers, managers, salesmen, and stenographers whose work needed a desk, not a machine. Women filled many lower-level office jobs, learning to type as the typewriter came into use, and women were hired as schoolteachers in growing numbers. Their wages rose faster than working-class wages, and their workday was shorter.

Schooling fed the new class. On the eve of the Civil War there were only 100 public high schools in the United States. By 1900 there were about 6,000, and the office needed the graduates. The middle class also changed how Americans shopped. **Department stores** like Macy's and Marshall Field's offered fixed prices, advertising, and brand names, and mail-order catalogs from Montgomery Ward and Sears brought the same goods to farm families.

Leisure became something to buy as well. Electric streetcars let families move to early suburbs, the **streetcar suburbs**, separating home from workplace and making homeownership the class's defining aspiration.

Look at the trade card: boys in suits dig in the sand at Coney Island, with a sailboat and paddle steamer on the water and a crowded beach, amusement buildings, and an elephant-shaped hotel behind them. The card sold clothing, but it also sold the new idea that a day at the beach was a product, bought with a train ticket and an afternoon off.

![Boys digging in the sand at Coney Island, with a crowded beach behind them](u6/u6-coney-island-scene.jpg)
Figure 3: Chromolithograph trade card, "Scene at Coney Island," Lindner, Eddy and Clauss, ca. 1885-1896. Public domain.

## Key terms

- **New immigrants** — immigrants after 1880 from southern and eastern Europe, including Italy, Russia, Poland, and the Balkans.

- **Exodusters** — Black Southerners who migrated to Kansas in 1879 and 1880, pushed by violence and poverty.

- **Tenement** — a crowded apartment house, often without running water or light, where many immigrants lived.

- **Chinese Exclusion Act** — the 1882 federal law barring Chinese laborers, the first immigration ban by nationality.

- **Nativism** — the belief that native-born Americans should be favored over immigrants.

- **Social Darwinism** — the idea that inequality came from a natural law of competition in which the fittest survived, so the rich owed the poor nothing and government aid would only interfere with nature.

- **Settlement house** — a neighborhood center in an immigrant district offering classes, clubs, and services.

- **Hull House** — Jane Addams's settlement house, opened in Chicago in 1889.

- **Middle class** — the new white-collar class of clerks, managers, teachers, and salespeople.

- **Department store** — a large store offering many kinds of goods at fixed prices, like Macy's and Marshall Field's.

- **Streetcar suburb** — a residential district built along electric streetcar lines, separating home from workplace.

Original secondary passage, written for this question from the grounding books:

> Before 1880, most immigrants to the United States came from northern and western Europe, above all Ireland, Germany, and Britain. The Irish shared a language with Americans, and the Germans came from a society much like America’s, so both groups settled in fairly easily. After 1880 the pattern changed. The “new immigrants” came from southern and eastern Europe: Italy, Russia, Poland, and the Balkans. They spoke little English, most were very poor, and many were Catholic or Jewish in a largely Protestant country. Many fled religious persecution and the breakdown of old farming ways at home. Source: Original secondary passage, written for this question from Barron’s Premium 2027 Chapter 8 (Topic 6.8), 5 Steps to a 5, 2024, Chapter 18, and Princeton Review, Chapter 10.

a. Identify one difference between the “old” immigration before 1880 and the “new” immigration after 1880.

Model answer: The old immigrants came mainly from northern and western Europe, such as Ireland, Germany, and Britain, while the new immigrants came mainly from southern and eastern Europe, such as Italy, Russia, and Poland. The two waves differed in origin, language, and religion.

b. Briefly explain one push factor that drove new immigrants from southern and eastern Europe to the United States.

Model answer: Many new immigrants fled religious persecution, such as Jews leaving Russia to escape anti-Semitic massacres called pogroms. Others fled poverty and the lack of social mobility as old farming ways broke down in their home regions.

c. Briefly explain one way the new immigrants changed American cities.

Model answer: The new immigrants crowded into tenements in the big coastal cities, making neighborhoods like New York’s Lower East Side the most densely populated places in the world. They also built ethnic neighborhoods with their own churches, newspapers, and shops, remaking the city’s map by nationality.

How to think about it: part a asks for one old-versus-new difference, so name origin, language, or religion. Part b asks for a push factor, so name poverty, land hunger, or persecution. Part c asks how the new immigrants changed cities, so name tenements, factories, and new neighborhoods.

A man got me work as a house servant in an American family, and my start was the same as that of almost all the Chinese in this country. . . . The reason why so many Chinese go into the laundry business in this country is because it requires little capital and is one of the few opportunities that are open. . . . >
> Lee Chew, “The Biography of a Chinaman,” The Independent, February 19, 1903, recalling his arrival in San Francisco before the Exclusion Act.

Source: Lee Chew, “The Biography of a Chinaman,” The Independent, February 19, 1903. Public domain; text checked against the Re-imagining Migration and Gilder Lehrman excerpts.

a. Identify one job Lee Chew held in the United States.

Model answer: Lee Chew first worked as a house servant in an American family in San Francisco, and he later opened a laundry business with a partner. Both jobs were among the few open to Chinese immigrants.

b. Briefly explain one reason Lee Chew gives for why so many Chinese immigrants worked in laundries.

Model answer: Lee Chew says the laundry business required little capital and was one of the few opportunities open to Chinese immigrants. With most doors closed to them, Chinese workers took the work they could start cheaply.

### Worked item 3 — apush-saq-0189 (Topic 6.10)

The stimulus is an original secondary passage written for this question, from Barron's Premium 2027, Chapter 8 (Topic 6.10) and 5 Steps to a 5, 2024, Chapter 18.

Original secondary passage, written for this question from the grounding books:

> In the last decades of the nineteenth century, a class of white-collar employees became essential to industrial capitalism. Their wages rose faster than those of working-class men and women, and their workday was shorter. Women filled many lower-level white-collar jobs, learning to type as the typewriter came into use, and women were also hired as schoolteachers in growing numbers.
>
> On the eve of the Civil War there were only 100 public high schools in the United States; by 1900 there were about 6,000. The growth of the middle class went hand in hand with the commercialization of leisure: town fairs and dances gave way to for-profit ventures in the city, and Brooklyn's Coney Island, with its boardwalk, vaudeville theaters, and attractions, became the most successful large-scale amusement park.

Source: Original secondary passage, written for this question from Barron's Premium 2027 Chapter 8 (Topic 6.10, The Rise of the Middle Class, The Commercialization of Leisure) and 5 Steps to a 5, 2024, Chapter 18 (Uneven Affluence). High-school figures: Barron's Chapter 8 (100 in 1860, about 6,000 by 1900), corroborated by the United States Bureau of Education, Statistics of Public High Schools 1919-1920, Table 1 (2,526 reporting in 1890; 6,005 in 1900).

a. Identify one way the growth of corporations changed the American workforce in the Gilded Age.

Model answer: Corporations needed managers, clerks, salespeople, and accountants to run their operations, creating a white-collar workforce that worked with paper rather than tools. These office workers earned salaries and worked shorter days than factory laborers.

b. Explain how the expansion of public high schools was connected to the growth of the middle class.

Model answer: The new offices and schools needed literate workers, and high schools supplied them: graduates became clerks, typists, and teachers. In turn, middle-class families sent their children to high school in larger numbers, so schooling and the middle class grew together.

c. Evaluate the extent to which the commercialization of leisure changed American life in the Gilded Age.

Model answer: It changed leisure a great deal for city dwellers: for-profit parks, vaudeville, and circuses replaced community fairs and gave the middle class new ways to spend free time and money. But the change was limited, because leisure still depended on income and free time, which factory workers had far less of, and rural Americans saw little of it.

How to think about it: part a asks for one workforce change, so name white-collar office jobs. Part b asks how high schools connected to the middle class, so link schooling to office work. Part c asks you to judge the extent of change, so weigh real new leisure against who could afford it.
