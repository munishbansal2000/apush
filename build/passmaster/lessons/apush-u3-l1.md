Why did a war over the Ohio Valley end with Britain ruling a continent — and the colonies on the road to rebellion? This lesson answers that question. The Seven Years' War gave Britain victory, debt, and a vast new empire to govern.

## A war over a valley

In the 1740s and 1750s, Virginia settlers crossed the Appalachians into the Ohio Valley, land France also claimed. France's claims ran from Quebec and Detroit in the north to New Orleans in the south, and France built Fort Duquesne where the Allegheny and Monongahela rivers meet, at present-day Pittsburgh. The valley mattered to France because it linked Canada with Louisiana. It mattered to Britain's settlers because its farmland promised room to grow.

In 1754 Virginia's governor sent a young militia officer, George Washington, to challenge the French. His men built a makeshift post, Fort Necessity, nearby, and skirmishes there opened the fighting. The next year General Edward Braddock marched on Fort Duquesne with British regulars and was ambushed and destroyed. The fighting in North America became known as the **French and Indian War**, part of the wider **Seven Years' War**.

That spring Benjamin Franklin published a cartoon in the Pennsylvania Gazette: a snake cut into eight pieces under the words "JOIN, or DIE." Franklin's point was that the colonies would fail unless they united against France. At the Albany Congress that year, Franklin proposed the **Albany Plan of Union** — one intercolonial government that could tax for defense. No colonial legislature would give up its power to tax itself, and the plan failed.

Look at Franklin's cartoon. The snake is cut into eight labeled pieces, and the caption gives the colonies a choice: join together or die separately. The cartoon's warning came true in reverse: the colonies stayed divided, and the plan died where it was born, in the assemblies themselves.

## Pitt's war and the fall of Canada

The war went badly for Britain until William Pitt took charge in 1757. Pitt poured money and troops into North America — a fleet and 25,000 regular soldiers, called redcoats — and paid the full cost of raising colonial troops. About 24,000 Americans served alongside the British. Pitt also worked with the colonial assemblies and reimbursed their war expenses, which built goodwill.

The turning point came at Louisbourg in 1758, whose fall opened the St. Lawrence, and at Quebec in 1759, where General Wolfe died in the moment of victory. Montreal followed, and by the **Treaty of Paris** of February 10, 1763, France gave up Canada and all its land east of the Mississippi. France kept its Caribbean sugar islands, which its ministers judged worth more than Canada's fur trade, and Spain took Louisiana west of the Mississippi.

Look at Benjamin West's painting of Wolfe's death at Quebec, painted in 1770, eleven years after the battle. Wolfe lies dying in the center, surrounded by his officers, with a Native man kneeling at the lower left. The painting idealizes the scene — the Native figure was not a documented witness — but it shows how Britain remembered the victory that made it the dominant power in North America.

![Benjamin West, The Death of General Wolfe, 1770](u3/death-of-general-wolfe-1770.jpg)
Figure 2: Benjamin West, "The Death of General Wolfe," 1770. Wolfe dies at Quebec in 1759, surrounded by officers. Public domain.

Colonial soldiers had fought bravely, but British officers treated them as inferiors — denying them equal rank, paying them less, and punishing them harshly. The shared fighting left colonists more confident in their own arms and more aware of how London saw them. The war gave them military experience, a sense of shared identity, and grievances that would shape later resistance.

## A map of a new empire

Look at the map. The thirteen colonies run down the Atlantic coast in pink, Canada stretches to the north, and Florida sits to the south — all British now. France is reduced to the tiny islands of Miquelon and St. Pierre off Newfoundland, with fishing rights there. Spain holds Louisiana west of the Mississippi. A red line runs along the Appalachians: the Proclamation Line of 1763.

![British North America, 1763–1775, Shepherd's Historical Atlas](u3/barrons-2027-ch05-02.png)
Figure 3: British North America, 1763–1775, Shepherd's Historical Atlas. The Proclamation Line of 1763 runs along the Appalachians. Public domain.

The map shows the scale of Britain's problem. The empire was now vast, and governing it would be expensive. Britain's national debt had nearly doubled in the war, from about £75 million in 1754 to about £133 million in 1763, with annual interest of more than £4.4 million. Ministers concluded the colonies should help pay for their own defense, since the victory had removed the French threat.

The Treaty of Paris made Britain the dominant power in North America, but it also left Britain with new lands to govern, new peoples to manage, and a debt to pay. The victory created the empire that the next decade would strain — and the strain would fall first on the colonies.

## Debt, resistance, and a line on the map

Before 1763, Native nations of the Ohio Valley and Great Lakes kept their independence by playing Britain and France against each other, trading with both and refusing to be ruled by either. Most sided with France during the war. The French, few in number and dependent on the fur trade, treated Native peoples as allies and partners, while British settlers wanted the land itself.

France's defeat removed the counterweight that had protected Native independence. In the spring of 1763, the Ottawa chief Pontiac organized the peoples of the Great Lakes and Ohio Valley to strike the British forts that had replaced French ones, in what became known as **Pontiac's Rebellion**. Warriors attacked Fort Detroit in May and six more forts, killing or capturing more than 400 British soldiers and 2,000 colonists.

London answered with the **Royal Proclamation of 1763**, issued October 7, 1763, reserving the lands west of the Appalachians for Native peoples and forbidding colonial settlement there. The line was meant to keep the peace and avoid another costly war with Native nations. Settlers who had fought for those lands and begun to settle them saw a decision made in London without consulting them — and many crossed the line anyway.

The rebellion showed London that governing the new empire would be expensive, since Native resistance could require costly wars and garrisons. It also showed the colonists that British policy could stand directly against their interests. Both lessons pointed in the same direction: the empire would cost more to run, and the colonies would be asked to pay.

## What the war changed

The war ended the long era when Britain left its colonies largely alone. Under **salutary neglect**, the **Navigation Acts** stayed on the books but were barely enforced, and the colonies governed themselves. After 1763, Britain ruled more directly: new revenue measures, tighter enforcement, and a standing army in America. **Mercantilism** — the idea that colonies exist to supply raw materials and buy the mother country's goods — now came with the bill attached.

Some things held steady across the whole period that followed. Enslaved people remained in bondage through the Revolution and the founding of the republic, even as the nation proclaimed liberty. Settlers kept pushing west onto Native peoples' lands, before and after independence. The war changed who ruled; it did not change who labored or whose land was taken.

The war also set the pattern for the rest of the period: a British government determined to make its empire pay, and colonists who had learned to fight together and to guard the right to tax themselves. The next lesson follows the quarrel that turned that tension into revolution.

John Dickinson's Letters from a Farmer in Pennsylvania (1767–68) drew the line the colonists would hold: Parliament could regulate trade, but it could not raise revenue in America. That argument turned a quarrel over money into a quarrel over rights, and it echoed in pamphlets, resolutions, and the Continental Congress. The Constitution later answered the question Dickinson raised: it gave the federal government the power to tax — but through an elected Congress, so Americans taxed themselves.

## Worked items

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0067 (Topic 3.1)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage: between 1754 and 1800 the colonies passed through three great changes — imperial crisis, independence and a new Constitution, and the shaping of the republic — while slavery and westward expansion continued throughout.

a. Identify one way Britain tried to tighten its control over the colonies after the Seven Years' War.

Model answer: After the war, Britain taxed the colonies for revenue with measures such as the Stamp Act. It also restricted western settlement with the Proclamation of 1763.

b. Explain one way the new republic's government under the Constitution differed from the government under the Articles of Confederation.

Model answer: Under the Articles, the national government could not tax or enforce its laws, and the states kept most power. The Constitution created a stronger federal government with the power to tax, raise an army, and regulate trade.

c. Explain one continuity in American life across the period 1754–1800.

Model answer: Slavery continued through the Revolution and the founding of the republic, even though the Declaration proclaimed that all men are created equal. Settlers also kept pushing west onto Native peoples' lands throughout the period, before and after independence.

How to think about it: Part a asks for one tightening measure from the passage, so name a tax or a restriction. Part b asks for a difference between the Articles and the Constitution, so name a power the Constitution added. Part c asks for a continuity, so name something that held steady through all three changes.

### Worked item 2 — apush-saq-0069 (Topic 3.2)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage: the Seven Years' War left Britain deeply in debt, ministers concluded the colonies should help pay for their own defense, and the colonists answered that they had already paid in blood, supplies, and their own assemblies' taxes.

a. Identify ONE way in which the Seven Years' War changed the relationship between Britain and its American colonies.

Model answer: The war left Britain deeply in debt, and ministers began to treat the colonies as a source of revenue rather than as self-governing communities that taxed themselves. Where earlier policy had left colonial taxation to the assemblies, after 1763 Parliament imposed its own revenue measures — the Sugar Act, the Stamp Act, and the Townshend duties — and the colonies answered by questioning Parliament's right to tax them at all.

b. Explain ONE reason why British ministers believed the colonies should help pay the costs of the war.

Model answer: Ministers argued that the colonies had benefited most from the victory: the French threat was gone, and British troops would remain in North America to protect them. Since the colonies gained the security the war had bought, London reasoned it was only fair for them to share the expense of their own defense.

c. Explain ONE reason why colonists objected to paying new taxes imposed by Parliament after the war.

Model answer: Colonists argued they had already paid for the war in blood and in supplies — about 24,000 provincials had served alongside British regulars — and that their own assemblies had taxed them throughout the conflict. More fundamentally, they held that only their elected assemblies could tax them, so a tax imposed by a Parliament in which they had no representation violated their rights as Englishmen.

How to think about it: Part a asks for one change the war caused, so name what London started doing differently. Part b asks for London's reason, so follow the money: who benefited from the victory. Part c asks for the colonists' objection, so name what they had already paid and who they said could tax them.

### Worked item 3 — apush-saq-0071 (Topic 3.2)

The stimulus is Shepherd's Historical Atlas map of North America in 1763–1775: British territory in pink along the coast, Canada, and Florida; France reduced to the green islands of Miquelon and St. Pierre; Spanish Louisiana in orange west of the Mississippi; and the Proclamation Line of 1763 in red along the Appalachians.

a. Identify ONE territorial change shown on the map that resulted from the Treaty of Paris of 1763.

Model answer: The map shows Canada and all the lands east of the Mississippi colored as British territory, where before the war they had belonged to France. France is reduced on the map to the two small green islands of Miquelon and St. Pierre off Newfoundland, with its former mainland empire gone.

b. Explain ONE way in which the territorial changes shown on the map affected the British colonies.

Model answer: With France removed from the continent, the colonies no longer faced a rival empire on their frontier, which encouraged settlers to look west toward the lands Britain had gained. At the same time, the map's red Proclamation Line shows London trying to hold those settlers back, creating a new source of friction between the colonies and the imperial government.

c. Explain ONE limitation of the map as a source for understanding the situation in North America in 1763.

Model answer: The map was drawn in 1911, nearly 150 years after the events it depicts, so it reflects a historian's summary rather than a contemporary view. It also shows territory as solid blocks of color, which hides the reality on the ground: Native nations still controlled much of the interior, and British authority in the newly gained lands was thin and contested.

How to think about it: Part a asks for one territorial change on the map, so name a place that changed color. Part b asks for an effect on the colonies, so follow the map's two messages at once: France gone, and a red line holding settlers back. Part c asks for a limit, so ask when the map was drawn and what solid colors hide.

## Key terms

- **Seven Years' War** — the war of 1754–1763 between Britain and France, fought in North America as the French and Indian War.

- **French and Indian War** — the North American fighting of the Seven Years' War, named for Britain's enemies: France and its Native allies.

- **Albany Plan of Union** — Benjamin Franklin's 1754 plan for one intercolonial government that could tax for defense, rejected by the colonial assemblies.

- **mercantilism** — the idea that colonies exist to supply raw materials to the mother country and buy its manufactured goods.

- **salutary neglect** — Britain's policy before 1763 of leaving the trade laws on the books but barely enforcing them.

- **Navigation Acts** — British laws regulating colonial trade, defended as necessary for the empire and resented when enforced.

- **Treaty of Paris** — the peace signed February 10, 1763, giving Britain Canada and all French land east of the Mississippi.

- **Royal Proclamation of 1763** — the royal order of October 7, 1763, forbidding colonial settlement west of the Appalachians.

- **Pontiac's Rebellion** — the 1763 uprising of Native peoples of the Great Lakes and Ohio Valley against British rule after France's defeat.
