Why did people in North America live so differently from one another before 1492? This lesson answers that question. The answer is a continent already full of cities, farms, and trade, where the land shaped each society, and where Europe first learned that a new world existed.

## A crowded continent in 1491

In 1491, the Americas were already home to millions of people. Between one and five million lived in the land that is now Canada and the United States, and about twenty million more lived in Mexico. Their ancestors had walked from Asia thousands of years earlier, in waves stretching back as far as 40,000 years, and spread across two continents.

The meeting of three peoples, American Indians, Europeans, and West Africans, on land held by American Indians created what we call a new world. From the late 1400s to the early 1600s, contact across the Atlantic tied once-separate regions into a single global system. It brought great wealth to some and utter destruction to others.

To set any event of this period in context, start with what came before it. That is the skill of **contextualization**: placing a development inside the wider story around it. The societies you meet in this lesson are that wider story. They were not a blank land waiting for Europe. They were a crowded, varied world of their own.

## Maize: the crop that built cities

**Maize**, or corn, was not found. It was bred. Farmers in what is now Mexico shaped it from a wild grass over thousands of years, until it could feed whole towns. Then it traveled north, century by century: first into the Southwest, then into the river valleys of the Southeast and Midwest, then east into the Northeast woodlands.

Where maize grew well, food surpluses followed. Surplus grain fed workers, chiefs, and priests, people who did not farm. That is how farming towns grew into cities, and how a **chiefdom**, a society ruled by a chief, took shape along the rivers.

The greatest of these cities was Cahokia, near present-day St. Louis. The great mound was raised around 1100, and at the city's peak in the 1200s it held about 10,000 to 20,000 people, roughly the size of London at the same time. Its people raised great earthen mounds, including Monks Mound, about a hundred feet tall, built basketful by basketful. Look at the photograph: the mound still rises above a farm field.

![Monks Mound at Cahokia, 1907](u1/anim-cahokia-mound-1907.jpg)
Figure 1: Monks Mound at Cahokia, photographed in 1907. The mound still rises above a farm field near St. Louis. Library of Congress, public domain.

Cahokia, a city of the **Mississippian culture**, was a trading hub, not an isolated town. Copper from the Great Lakes and shells from the Gulf Coast reached the city on foot over hundreds of miles. Its mounds, plazas, and wooden **palisade**, a fence of tall stakes, show a city that could organize large building projects. Far to the south, **Tenochtitlan**, capital of the Mexica (the Aztec) in central Mexico, held about 200,000 people, more than any city in Europe in 1500. Both cities ran on maize.

## Farming dry land, following the herds

The land decides the food, the food decides whether you stay or move, and that decides how you live. Start with the driest places.

In the dry Southwest, the **Pueblo peoples** farmed land that looked unfarmable. They built villages of **adobe**, sun-dried mud brick, and watered their fields with irrigation. Their towns around the Four Corners region, some with hundreds of rooms at Chaco Canyon, date back to about 900. Drought in the 1200s and 1300s scattered many of these towns, and their people joined other villages to the west and along the Rio Grande.

On the Great Plains, the main food was the bison, and bison move. Before Europeans brought horses, Plains peoples hunted on foot and lived in portable hide tents, following the herds with the seasons. Only along the eastern rivers did Plains peoples farm and settle. In the dry **Great Basin**, between the Rocky Mountains and the Sierra Nevada, Shoshone, Paiute, and Ute bands moved with the seasons, hunting and gathering.

## Towns of the woodlands and the coast

In the Northeast woodlands, Iroquois villagers farmed the **three sisters**, maize, beans, and squash planted together in one field, and added hunting and fishing. The tall maize held the bean vines, and broad squash leaves shaded the soil. The five nations of the **Iroquois League**, Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca, formed in the 1400s to end fighting among themselves, bound themselves together under the Great Law of Peace, and decided war and diplomacy by consensus in a Grand Council.

Iroquois clans ran through the mother's line. Clan mothers chose the council leaders, and they could remove them. That gave Iroquois women real political power, something that surprised nearly every European who met them.

On the rainy Pacific coast, the **Chinook** peoples fished, hunted, and foraged along the Columbia River. They farmed little, yet they lived in settled villages with marked social ranks, in large shared houses holding up to fifty people. Rich fishing could support a settled, ranked society without farming, proof that maize was one road to complexity, not the only one.

Look at the engraving below. It shows Florida Indians planting beans and maize in cleared fields. Theodor de Bry made it in Europe in 1591, from watercolors by Jacques Le Moyne de Morgues, who had visited Florida. He never saw these fields himself, so read it as a European view of Native farming, not a photograph. Notice the poses: the figures look like classical European art, because a European artist drew them.

## A managed land, not an empty one

Europeans who reached North America often called it wilderness, land no one had touched. That word misleads. Native peoples had shaped the land for centuries. They set controlled burns to clear brush and drive game. They cleared forests for fields. They built weirs across rivers to catch fish.

The historian William Cronon made this case in his book Changes in the Land: what Europeans called wilderness was a landscape people had been working for centuries. The land looked empty to newcomers for a second reason. After 1492, disease traveled faster than any European. Epidemics swept through villages before Europeans ever saw the interior, emptying land that people had worked and then lost.

So when a source describes empty land, ask what emptied it. The answer may be an epidemic, not nature. A common mistake is to picture North America before 1492 as untouched. The evidence points the other way: a tended land, worked for centuries, then emptied in places by disease.

## Europe learns a new world exists

Columbus crossed the Atlantic in 1492, sailing for Spain. He made four voyages between 1492 and 1504. His third voyage, in 1498 to 1500, first reached the mainland of South America. In a letter of 1493, he told the Spanish court that the islands held gentle people, gold, and spices, a report written to win support for more voyages.

Look at the map below. Martin Waldseemuller published it in 1507, and it was the first map to call the western lands America, after Amerigo Vespucci. Vespucci had argued that these lands were a new world, not part of Asia. At the top, Vespucci stands beside Ptolemy, the ancient geographer: new knowledge beside old.

![Waldseemuller world map, 1507](u1/original-ctx-u1-03.jpg)
Figure 3: Martin Waldseemuller's world map, 1507, the first to name the western lands America. Library of Congress, public domain.

Spain and Portugal moved fast to divide the new lands. The Treaty of Tordesillas of 1494 drew a line through the Atlantic, giving Portugal lands to the east and Spain lands to the west. A Portuguese map of 1502, the Cantino planisphere, already showed the Atlantic coast of South America, with the dividing line and each crown's flags.

Bartolome de las Casas, a Spanish friar, wrote A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies in 1542 to persuade the Spanish crown to stop the cruel treatment of Indigenous peoples. He claimed the people of Hispaniola had fallen from about three million to only two hundred. His figures are contested, because he wrote to persuade, so use his account for his purpose and his charges, not for an exact population count.

Goods, crops, animals, diseases, and ideas soon crossed the Atlantic in both directions. Historians call that exchange the Columbian Exchange. Its full story belongs to the next lessons.

## Worked items

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0001 (Topic 1.1)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage: before 1492 the Americas held many different societies, from Cahokia to the Aztec and Inca empires, while Europeans sought a sea route to Asian spices, and Portuguese sailors had already sailed down the west coast of Africa, learning to use the winds and currents of the Atlantic.

a. Identify one characteristic of Indigenous societies in the Americas before 1492.

Model answer: One characteristic is variety: the Americas held large cities such as Cahokia, empires such as the Aztec and the Inca, and many smaller farming and hunting societies, each adapted to its own region.

b. Explain how one European development before 1492 helped make transatlantic contact possible.

Model answer: Portuguese sailing down the African coast taught Europeans to use Atlantic winds and currents, and the search for a sea route to Asian spices gave them a reason to sail west, so by 1492 Europeans had both the skill and the motive to cross the ocean.

c. Explain one way the arrival of Europeans changed Indigenous societies in Period 1.

Model answer: European arrival brought conquest and forced labor, and it also brought diseases such as smallpox to peoples with no immunity, so Indigenous populations fell sharply and many societies lost people, land, and political independence.

How to think about it: part a asks for one fact from the passage, so name the variety and give examples. Part b asks for a cause, so link a European skill to a European motive. Part c asks for a change, so name conquest, forced labor, and disease together.

### Worked item 2 — apush-saq-0004 (Topic 1.2)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage: Mississippian chiefdoms farmed maize in river valleys, Pueblo peoples farmed dry land in the Southwest, and Iroquois villagers farmed the three sisters in the Northeast.

a. Identify one way in which environment shaped how a Native society in the passage lived.

Model answer: In the arid Southwest, Pueblo peoples farmed dry land and built adobe villages, a way of life fitted to a dry climate. With little rain, they had to farm dry land and build with sun-dried earth, so the climate itself set the shape of their homes and fields.

b. Explain how maize farming helped Mississippian chiefdoms like Cahokia grow large.

Model answer: Maize farming in the fertile river valleys produced surpluses that fed dense populations, and those surpluses supported chiefs, priests, and the labor that raised the mounds. Surplus food freed some people from farming, so a community could feed chiefs and priests and organize the labor that raised the mounds.

c. Explain one similarity between the Pueblo peoples and the Iroquois villagers described in the passage.

Model answer: Both were settled farming peoples: Pueblo peoples farmed dry land in the Southwest and Iroquois villagers farmed the three sisters in the Northeast, and both lived in permanent villages. In both places, farming gave a steady food supply, so families could stay in one place year after year instead of moving to find food.

How to think about it: part a asks you to match one society to its land, so start with the environment. Part b asks for a chain, so run it from surplus to dense population to chiefs and building. Part c asks for a similarity, so name what both share, settled farming, before you name how they differ.

### Worked item 3 — apush-saq-0008 (Topic 1.2)

The stimulus is the photograph you have already seen as Figure 1: the Great Cahokia Mound, also called Monks Mound, rising above a cultivated field near present-day St. Louis, photographed on October 16, 1907.

a. Identify what the mound in the photograph shows about Cahokia.

Model answer: The mound shows that Cahokia's people could organize the labor to raise a huge earthen structure, the mark of a large and organized society. Raising tons of earth basketful by basketful took many workers over many years, and only a society that could feed and direct them could finish such a mound.

b. Explain how maize farming made building on this scale possible.

Model answer: Maize farming in the river valleys produced food surpluses, and those surpluses fed the workers and supported the chiefs who directed the building. Without a surplus, everyone would have to farm for their own meals, so no workers or chiefs could be spared for building.

c. Explain one limitation of using this 1907 photograph as evidence about life at Cahokia around 1100.

Model answer: The photograph was taken eight centuries after Cahokia's height, so it shows the mound as it survived into modern times, not the city, the people, or the building work itself. The camera can only record what was still standing in 1907, so the photograph proves the mound endured, not how Cahokia looked or worked around 1100.

How to think about it: part a asks what the mound proves, so connect the size of the structure to the size of the society. Part b asks for the chain again, from surplus to workers to building. Part c asks for a limit, so count the centuries between the photograph and the city, and say what the photograph cannot show.

## Key terms

- **maize** — corn, bred by Native farmers in Mexico from a wild grass over thousands of years.

- **three sisters** — maize, beans, and squash planted together in one field.

- **Mississippian culture** — the maize-farming peoples of the Mississippi River valley, builders of Cahokia.

- **Pueblo peoples** — Native peoples of the arid Southwest who farmed with irrigation and built adobe villages.

- **Iroquois** — the five nations of the Northeast woodlands, bound as the Iroquois League by the Great Law of Peace.

- **adobe** — sun-dried mud brick used for building in the Southwest.

- **palisade** — a fence of tall wooden stakes, used to ring a town such as Cahokia.

- **Chinook** — Native peoples of the Pacific Northwest who lived by fishing, hunting, and foraging.

- **Tenochtitlan** — capital of the Mexica in central Mexico, home to about 200,000 people.

- **chiefdom** — a society ruled by a chief, supported by farming surpluses.

- **Great Basin** — the dry region between the Rocky Mountains and the Sierra Nevada.

- **contextualization** — placing a development inside the wider story around it.

## Closing check

After this lesson, you should be able to explain why Native societies in different environments lived so differently, using the land, the food, and the choice to stay or move.

You should be able to explain how maize farming made cities such as Cahokia and Tenochtitlan possible, and why rich fishing could do the same work for the Chinook without farming.

You should be able to read a European source, such as the Waldseemuller map or Las Casas, for what its maker wanted and what limits it as evidence.

You should be able to place any event of Period 1 in its context: a crowded continent that Europeans met, not an empty land they found.
