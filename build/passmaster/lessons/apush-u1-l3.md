What happened when two worlds that had been apart for thousands of years suddenly met? This lesson answers that question. The answer is an exchange of crops, animals, and diseases that remade both sides of the Atlantic, and a conquest that moved faster than steel alone could explain.

## What crossed the Atlantic

In 1972, the historian Alfred Crosby gave a name to the great transfer that followed 1492: the **Columbian Exchange**. It was the two-way movement of crops, animals, diseases, and ideas between the Americas and the rest of the world. Nobody planned it, and nobody controlled it. The trade in goods was the smallest part of it. The largest cargo crossed without a ticket.

Westbound, Europe sent wheat and sugarcane, and the animals the Americas had never seen: horses, cattle, and pigs. For about ten thousand years, no horses had lived on this continent; the native herds had died out in the Ice Age. Every mustang goes back to Spanish imports. Eastbound, the Americas sent maize, potatoes, tomatoes, tobacco, and cacao. One farm animal broke the rule: the turkey sailed east, alongside the maize and potatoes.

The exchange changed what people ate on both sides. Maize did not stop in Europe: it sailed on to Africa, where it became a staple, and to Asia, feeding growth on both continents. The potato fed a population boom across Europe. In the 1840s, Ireland depended on one potato variety so completely that a blight became a famine. The rule is simple: the Americas had almost no domesticated farm animals, so the livestock went west, and the crops went both ways.

The table below sums up the exchange. Read it as a map of the new world economy: food and animals in both directions, and disease in one.

| Westbound: Europe to the Americas | Eastbound: Americas to Europe |
|---|---|
| Wheat, sugarcane | Maize, potatoes, tomatoes, tobacco, cacao |
| Horses, cattle, pigs | Turkey (the only farm animal) |
| Smallpox, measles, influenza | — |

Table 1: The Columbian Exchange in both directions. Disease traveled west only.

## Disease did the conquering

The deadliest cargo was the one nobody loaded on purpose. **Smallpox**, measles, and influenza sailed west with the colonists, striking Native peoples who had been separated from the rest of humanity's germs for thousands of years. They had no immunity, no childhood exposure, and no resistance. In the hardest-hit towns, eight or nine out of ten people died.

The numbers are hard to hold in mind, so hold two. On Hispaniola, the island Columbus first settled, the Native population fell from over one million in 1492 to about five hundred by the 1540s. Across the Americas, Barron's estimates that between 50 and 90 percent of Native peoples died between 1500 and 1650. War and slavery helped the dying along, and broken food systems made it worse, but disease led.

The dying had a witness. The **Florentine Codex**, compiled by the Franciscan friar Bernardino de Sahagún with **Nahua** scholars in the 1500s, preserves a Nahua account of smallpox in Tenochtitlan. The sick lay in their houses and sleeping places, no longer able to move or stir, while the healthy could not bury the dead fast enough. Whole neighborhoods fell ill at once. The account reaches us through Spanish mediation, so check its details against other records, but the scale of the dying it describes is confirmed by the numbers.

Disease traveled one way. The one illness that might have sailed east is **syphilis**, and historians still argue about its origin. Some say it crossed from the Americas; others say it was already in Europe before 1492. Nobody has settled it. The asymmetry is the point: the side that got the germs emptied out, and empty land is conquerable land.

## How two empires fell so fast

Steel and horses get the paintings. The germs did the conquering. In 1519, Hernando Cortés, a Spanish **conquistador**, led about five hundred men from Cuba to Tenochtitlan, the Aztec capital. The Aztecs did not know what to make of him. Montezuma received him as a guest, then relations broke down, Montezuma was killed, and Cortés was driven from the city.

Cortés rebuilt his forces, and his real army was not Spanish. He organized a coalition of Native peoples who hated their Aztec overlords. The **Tlaxcalans**, the Aztecs' enemies, joined him in large numbers. In 1521, the coalition besieged and overran Tenochtitlan, ending the Aztec Empire. Smallpox had already struck the city in 1520, killing defenders who had no immunity, and the epidemic did what siege engines could not.

Theodor de Bry engraved the view of Cusco below, after the mapmaker Giacomo Gastaldi, showing the Inca capital as a walled grid city in the mountains. Pizarro's conquest of the Inca in 1532 followed the same pattern: a small Spanish force, an empire weakened by civil war, and disease running ahead of the soldiers. The Inca civil war had already divided the empire before Pizarro arrived; it followed the death of the emperor Huayna Capac, probably from smallpox.

![Cusco, capital of the Inca Empire](u1/u1-debry-cusco-fall.jpg)
Figure 1: Cusco, capital of the Inca Empire. Engraving by Theodor de Bry after Giacomo Gastaldi, 16th century. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

Cortés wrote home. In his Second Letter to Charles V, dated October 30, 1520, he described casting the Aztec idols down the temple steps and setting up images of the Virgin and the saints in their place. He was writing to win the king's support, so read his letter for its purpose. The letter shows the conquest as Cortés wanted it seen: a Christian mission.

## Silver made Spain rich and poor

The wealth that paid for Spain's empire came from a mountain. In 1545, silver was found at **Potosí**, in the high Andes of what is now Bolivia. The town that grew at the foot of the mountain existed for one reason: to dig silver out of it. The woodcut below was printed in Pedro Cieza de León's Chronicle of Peru in 1553. The mountain towers over the town, and its ore veins are named on the slopes, like streets on a map.

![The Cerro de Potosí over its mining town](u1/barrons-2027-ch03-01.jpg)
Figure 2: The Cerro de Potosí over its mining town. Woodcut from Pedro Cieza de León, Chronicle of Peru, 1553. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

Silver poured into Spain, and Spain spent it as fast as it arrived. The crown paid for armies and wars, and borrowed heavily from European bankers to pay for more. Taxes rose more than fivefold. At the same time, the growing supply of silver money pushed prices up across Western Europe. Silver made Spain the richest crown in Europe and one of the most indebted.

The paradox is that silver did not make most Spaniards richer. The crown spent it on soldiers, ships, and interest, and it flowed out of Spain almost as fast as it flowed in. A small landowning class saw no reason to modernize farms or workshops, so the wealth passed through Spain instead of building its economy.

The labor that dug the silver was coerced. Spain kept the Inca tribute and labor systems it found, including the **mit'a**, the rotational labor draft, which Viceroy Francisco de Toledo organized in the 1570s. Native workers were drafted to the mines for set terms, and many never returned. The silver that filled Spanish ships was dug by hands with no choice in the work.

## Sugar built a new kind of labor

Silver was not the only wealth, and it was not the first to demand forced labor. Sugar was the hungriest crop of all: it needed vast fields, hard cutting, and constant boiling. The engraving below comes from Stradanus's Nova Reperta series around 1600. Workers cut cane, crush it, boil the juice in great vats, and mold the sugar into cones.

![Making sugar: cutting cane, crushing it, boiling the juice, molding loaves](u1/gap-g-v01-1-columbian-sugar-engraving.jpg)
Figure 3: Making sugar: cutting cane, crushing it, boiling the juice, molding loaves. Engraving after Johannes Stradanus, about 1600. Public domain.

Europe already had the machine before the Americas did. On the Atlantic islands of Madeira and São Tomé, Portuguese planters were growing sugar with enslaved African labor in the 1500s. Spain's first answer in the Americas was different: it forced Native workers into the **encomienda**, on plantations and in the mines. As disease emptied the Native towns, the encomienda faded, and the island machine moved across the ocean.

The scale that followed is hard to overstate. Thousands of enslaved Africans crossed at first, then millions, in the holds of ships sailing west. The same routes that carried sugar west carried people in chains. Those captives were not only taken by Europeans: African kingdoms and merchants had been raiding and selling captives for centuries, and European buyers bought into a trade that already existed. The exchange does not end with crops. It ends with the Middle Passage, the forced Atlantic crossing.

Keep the order straight. The chains came first, on the Atlantic islands, before the silver boom. Silver scaled the machine up; it did not start it. And the Spanish were shipping enslaved Africans across the Atlantic in the 1500s, a century before 1619. The story of slavery in the Americas does not start at Jamestown.

## Worked items

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0014 (Topic 1.4)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage: after 1492, plants, animals, and diseases crossed the Atlantic in both directions. Wheat, sugarcane, horses, cattle, and pigs sailed west to the Americas. Maize, potatoes, tomatoes, and tobacco sailed east to Europe. Smallpox, measles, and influenza sailed west with the colonists, striking Native peoples who had no immunity.

a. Identify one crop or animal that moved from the Americas to Europe.

Model answer: Maize moved from the Americas to Europe. First grown in Mexico, it crossed the Atlantic after 1492 and became a staple food in Europe, Africa, and Asia.

b. Explain one way the Columbian Exchange changed life for Native peoples in the Americas.

Model answer: Smallpox and other Old World diseases struck Native peoples who had no immunity, killing between 50 and 90 percent of them by 1650. The dying emptied towns, broke food systems, and made Spanish conquest possible on a scale steel alone never could.

c. Explain one way the Columbian Exchange changed life for Europeans.

Model answer: Maize and potatoes from the Americas gave Europeans new foods that fed more people. The extra calories supported population growth, which gave Europe more workers and soldiers in the centuries that followed.

How to think about it: part a asks for one item moving east, so pick maize and say where it came from and where it went. Part b asks for a change for Native peoples, so name the diseases, the death rate, and what the dying broke. Part c asks for a change for Europeans, so run the chain from new foods to more people to more workers and soldiers.

### Worked item 2 — apush-saq-0015 (Topic 1.4)

The stimulus is a paraphrase of a Nahua account of smallpox in Tenochtitlan, recorded in the Florentine Codex, compiled by the Franciscan friar Bernardino de Sahagún with Nahua scholars in the 1500s: the sick lay in their houses and sleeping places, no longer able to move or stir, while the healthy could not bury the dead fast enough, and whole neighborhoods fell ill at once.

a. Identify one feature of the epidemic described in the account.

Model answer: The account describes the sick lying in their houses, unable to move or stir. So many fell ill at once that the healthy could not bury the dead.

b. Explain how the epidemic described in the account helped Spanish conquest.

Model answer: The epidemic emptied towns and broke the systems that fed and defended them. With so many dead or too sick to fight, Spanish forces faced far less resistance than an untouched empire would have offered.

c. Explain one limitation of using this account as evidence about the epidemic.

Model answer: The account was compiled by a Spanish friar working with Nahua scholars, so it reaches us through Spanish mediation. A student must therefore check its details against other records before treating any single claim as settled fact.

How to think about it: part a asks for one feature, so take it straight from the account: the sick unable to move, the dead unburied. Part b asks how the epidemic helped conquest, so connect the emptied towns to the weakened resistance. Part c asks for a limit, so name the mediation: a Spanish friar compiled a Nahua account, so check it against other records.

## Key terms

- **Columbian Exchange** — the two-way movement of crops, animals, diseases, and ideas between the Americas and the rest of the world after 1492.

- **conquistador** — a Spanish conqueror of the Americas.

- **Tlaxcalans** — the Native allies of Cortés who helped him take Tenochtitlan.

- **Nahua** — the peoples of central Mexico, including the Aztecs, whose accounts survive in the Florentine Codex.

- **Florentine Codex** — the history of central Mexico compiled by Bernardino de Sahagún with Nahua scholars in the 1500s.

- **mit'a** — the rotational labor draft, organized by Viceroy Toledo in the 1570s, that sent Native workers to the mines.

- **Potosí** — the silver mountain in the high Andes, mined from 1545.

- **encomienda** — the grant of Native labor to a colonist, in exchange for protection and Christian instruction.

- **smallpox** — the Old World disease that killed Native peoples who had no immunity to it.

- **syphilis** — the disease that may have sailed east from the Americas; its origin is still argued.
