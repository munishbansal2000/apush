In 1886 an Atlanta editor told New York businessmen that the South had been reborn. Railroads, mills, and Northern capital, he said, would make the region rich and close the gap with the North. This lesson answers one question: how new was the New South, and what did new machines do for the whole country?

## Grady's promise

Henry W. Grady, editor of the Atlanta Constitution, spoke to the New England Society of New York in December 1886. He opened with words borrowed from Benjamin H. Hill: "There was a South of slavery and secession — that South is dead. There is a South of union and freedom — that South, thank God, is living, breathing, growing every hour." The **New South** was his creed: the region would rise through commerce, not cotton and slavery, and reconciliation would follow trade.

Boosters had real things to point to. Birmingham, Alabama, grew around iron and steel works. Textile mills spread across the Carolina Piedmont, and tobacco factories rose in North Carolina. Southern cities grew fast, and Northern money helped build them. Look at the cartoon: a muscular Black worker labeled "Free Labor" stands with hammer and plow before the Atlanta Exposition fairground, where a crowd passes a statue of Lincoln toward a skyline marked "Prosperity." The artist's message is Grady's own: free labor, not slavery, would build the South.

![A Black worker labeled Free Labor before the Atlanta Exposition fairground](u6/original-u6-newsouth-05.jpg)
Figure 1: Udo J. Keppler, "The New South — The Triumph of Free Labor," Puck, October 23, 1895. Public domain (Library of Congress).

Grady spoke to Northern investors as much as to Southerners. His promise was that the South was safe for capital now, that the old quarrel was over, and that mills and railroads would do what armies could not. The crowd that heard him believed the pitch. The question is whether the pitch was true.

## How new was it?

The mills were real. Look at the chart: cotton spindles in the Southern states rose from 561 thousand in 1880 to 1,570 thousand in 1890 and 4,368 thousand in 1900. Textile towns grew, and thousands of Southerners, many of them women and children, took mill jobs. Birmingham made iron, and railroads tied the region together.

![Bar chart of cotton spindles in the Southern states, 1880 to 1900](u6/u6-southern-spindles-1880-1900.png)
Figure 2: Cotton spindles in the Southern states, 1880-1900. Source: U.S. Census Bureau, Statistical Abstract, 1925.

But the region stayed the nation's poorest, and most Southerners stayed farmers. **Sharecropping** trapped them: landless tenants farmed someone else's land and paid rent with a share of the crop. The **crop-lien system** made it worse. Tenants bought seed and supplies on credit at high prices from the landowner's store and paid in cotton at harvest, ending each year deeper in debt. Critics called it wage slavery in a new form, and the name fits the cycle: the crop never quite cleared the debt.

Mill villages were often company towns, with wages below Northern levels and the mill owning the houses, the store, and the church. The capital that built the mills mostly came from the North, and much of the profit went back there. The South industrialized, but only partway, and the old order's shape survived inside the new one.

## White supremacy made law

The New South was also a political project. Starting with Mississippi's constitution of 1890, Southern states rewrote their laws with poll taxes and literacy tests — and later, **grandfather clauses** — that stripped Black men of the vote. Louisiana's 1898 constitution shows the result: Black voter registration fell sharply between 1896 and 1900. Disfranchisement was the legal name for it, and it worked by design.

In 1896 the Supreme Court blessed the system in **Plessy v. Ferguson**, which upheld "separate but equal" facilities for Black and white Americans. **Jim Crow** laws then divided daily life by race: trains, schools, parks, and waiting rooms. The promise of the Reconstruction amendments survived on paper while daily life preserved the old racial order.

Booker T. Washington answered this world at the Atlanta Exposition in 1895. He told Black Southerners to "cast down your bucket where you are," to build their lives through work in agriculture and the trades in the South, and to cultivate friendly relations with the Southern white man, who was their "nextdoor neighbour."

He accepted being "as separate as the fingers" in things purely social while urging the races to be as one hand in economic life. Historians often call Washington's address the **Atlanta Compromise**, for the bargain it struck between economic progress and delayed political rights. Later critics, above all W. E. B. Du Bois, argued for immediate political rights instead. The debate between the two strategies would shape Black politics for a generation.

## Machines that made steel cheap

While the South debated its future, new machines remade the whole economy. The **Bessemer process**, adopted in the 1870s, blew air through molten iron to burn out impurities and make steel cheap. Look at the engraving: a converter vessel throws off a shower of white-hot sparks, with a ladle and tools lying in the foreground. Cheap steel meant rails that lasted, bridges that spanned rivers, and buildings that rose higher than brick could reach.

![A Bessemer converter at work in a steel mill](u6/original-u6-tech-07.jpg)
Figure 3: A Bessemer converter at work, nineteenth-century engraving. Public domain.

Andrew Carnegie built his fortune on that steel. He bought iron mines, coal fields, railroads, and mills, so his company controlled every step from raw material to finished rail. Oil followed the same path: Edwin Drake drilled the first oil well at Titusville, Pennsylvania, in 1859, and kerosene lit American homes more brightly and cheaply than candles or whale oil. John D. Rockefeller's Standard Oil soon controlled the refining of that oil.

Communication and light changed just as fast. Alexander Graham Bell patented the telephone in 1876, and Thomas Edison patented the electric lamp in 1879. Within a few years, Edison's Pearl Street station lit part of New York City, and telephones reached into offices and homes. The country was wiring itself together even as it argued over who owned the wires.

## Wealth and its defenders

Great fortunes needed a defense, and Carnegie supplied one. In "Wealth," published in the North American Review in June 1889, he argued that the millionaire should be "but a trustee for the poor," administering surplus wealth for the public good during his lifetime. The Gospel of Wealth was his name for that duty, and he spent his later years giving money to libraries, schools, and universities.

Social Darwinism gave a harsher defense. Borrowing Charles Darwin's phrase "survival of the fittest," its supporters argued that competition in business was natural law and that the rich had earned their place while the poor had earned theirs. Yale professor William Graham Sumner argued that the state owed the classes little beyond leaving them alone. The two ideas pulled in opposite directions: one said wealth carried a duty, the other said it carried no duty at all.

Workers, farmers, and reformers answered both. They said the fortunes rested on low wages, long hours, and government help, not on fitness alone. That argument is the subject of the next lesson, where the people who dug the coal and tended the furnaces begin to organize.

## Key terms

- **New South** — the postwar creed, preached by Henry W. Grady, that railroads, mills, and Northern capital would industrialize the South.

- **Sharecropping** — a system in which landless tenants farmed someone else's land and paid rent with a share of the crop.

- **Crop-lien system** — credit for seed and supplies at high prices, repaid in cotton at harvest, which kept tenants in debt.

- **Grandfather clause** — a voting rule that excused men whose grandfathers had voted before 1867, shielding white voters from literacy tests.

- **Plessy v. Ferguson** — the 1896 Supreme Court decision upholding "separate but equal" facilities by race.

- **Atlanta Compromise** — the name historians give Washington's 1895 Atlanta address, trading economic progress for delayed political rights.

- **Jim Crow** — the system of laws and customs that segregated daily life by race in the South.

- **Bessemer process** — the 1870s method of making cheap steel by blowing air through molten iron.

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0175 (Topic 6.4)

The stimulus is a paraphrase of the argument in C. Vann Woodward, Origins of the New South, 1877–1913 (1951).

> Historian C. Vann Woodward argued that the “New South” was less new than its promoters claimed. Industry did grow in the South, he wrote, but the region stayed poor and mostly rural, and it still depended on Northern capital. Meanwhile, he argued, Southern politics were rebuilt on white supremacy, so the new order kept much of the old order’s shape.

Source: Paraphrase of the argument in C. Vann Woodward, Origins of the New South, 1877–1913 (1951).

a. Briefly describe Woodward’s argument about the “New South.”

Model answer: Woodward argues that the New South changed less than its boosters promised. He says Southern industry did grow, but the region stayed poor, mostly rural, and dependent on Northern capital. At the same time, he argues, Southern politics were rebuilt on white supremacy, so the new order carried much of the old order forward.

b. Briefly explain ONE specific historical development between 1865 and 1900 that supports Woodward’s argument.

Model answer: Sharecropping supports his argument because most Black Southerners worked land they did not own and paid rent with a share of the crop. The crop-lien system kept them in debt to white landowners year after year. That economic dependence shows the old order surviving inside the new economy.

c. Briefly explain ONE specific historical development between 1865 and 1900 that challenges or qualifies Woodward’s argument.

Model answer: The growth of Southern textile mills qualifies his argument because cotton spindles in the South rose from 561,000 in 1880 to 4,368,000 in 1900. New mill towns and steel cities like Birmingham showed real industrial change. That growth proves the New South was not only the old order in new clothes.

How to think about it: part a asks for Woodward's argument in your own words, so say the New South kept much of the old order's shape. Part b asks for supporting evidence, so name sharecropping or white supremacy. Part c asks for evidence against it, so name real industry like the textile mills.

### Worked item 2 — apush-saq-0177 (Topic 6.5)

The stimulus is an original secondary passage written for this question, from Barron's Premium 2027, Chapter 8, 5 Steps to a 5, 2024, Chapter 18, and Princeton Review, Chapter 10.

Original secondary passage, written for this question from the grounding books:

> In the 1850s, George Bissell showed that oil from the ground could be refined into kerosene, a lamp fuel brighter and cheaper than candles or whale oil. In 1859, Edwin Drake drilled the first oil well at Titusville, Pennsylvania, and the oil business was born. Refined oil lit American homes, and it also lubricated the machines of the new factories and railroads. By the 1870s, oil had become one of the nation's great industries, and the men who controlled its refining, above all John D. Rockefeller's Standard Oil, were among the richest in the country.

Source: Original secondary passage, written for this question from Barron's Premium 2027 Ch 8 (Coal and Oil), 5 Steps to a 5 2024 Ch 18, and Princeton Review Ch 10.

a. Identify one way petroleum was used in the United States in the late nineteenth century.

Model answer: Petroleum was refined into kerosene, which lit American homes in lamps. Refined oil also lubricated factory machines and railroad equipment, keeping the new industrial machinery running.

b. Briefly explain how kerosene changed daily life for ordinary Americans.

Model answer: Kerosene lamps were brighter and cheaper than candles or whale oil, so ordinary families could afford light after dark. Evening hours opened up for reading, work, and school, and homes no longer went dark at sundown.

c. Briefly explain one way the oil industry was connected to the growth of the railroads.

Model answer: Railroads carried crude oil and refined kerosene to markets across the country, and the oil business depended on rail transport to grow beyond Pennsylvania. At the same time, railroads needed oil to lubricate their locomotives and machinery, so each industry fed the other.

How to think about it: part a asks for one use of petroleum, so name kerosene lamps or machine lubrication. Part b asks how kerosene changed daily life, so think about light after dark. Part c asks how oil connected to railroads, so think about what the railroads carried and lubricated.

### Worked item 3 — apush-saq-0178 (Topic 6.5)

The stimulus is an excerpt from Andrew Carnegie, 'Wealth,' North American Review, June 1889.

> "The problem of our age is the proper administration of wealth, so that the ties of brotherhood may still bind together the rich and poor in harmonious relationship. ... Thus is the problem of rich and poor to be solved. The laws of accumulation will be left free; the laws of distribution free. Individualism will continue, but the millionaire will be but a trustee for the poor."
>
> — Andrew Carnegie, "Wealth," North American Review, June 1889

Source: Andrew Carnegie, "Wealth," North American Review, June 1889. Public domain. Text verified against the North American Review text (Wikisource) and the Fordham Internet History Sourcebooks transcription.

a. Identify Carnegie's main claim about what wealthy people should do with their fortunes.

Model answer: Carnegie claims that the rich should act as trustees for the poor, administering their surplus wealth during their own lives for the good of the community. He argues that giving wealth away wisely, not hoarding it or leaving it to heirs, solves the problem of rich and poor.

b. Briefly explain how technological innovation made a fortune like Carnegie's possible.

Model answer: The Bessemer process made steel cheap, and cheap steel built the railroads, bridges, and skyscrapers of the Gilded Age. Carnegie built a steel empire by controlling every step of production, from iron ore and coal mines to mills and transport, and the new technology let him produce steel on a scale no earlier ironmaster could match.

c. Briefly explain one way other Americans in the Gilded Age responded differently to concentrated wealth.

Model answer: Workers organized unions such as the Knights of Labor and the American Federation of Labor and struck for higher wages and shorter hours, arguing that wealth should be shared through pay, not charity. Reformers and farmers also demanded government action, from railroad regulation to the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890.

How to think about it: part a asks for Carnegie's main claim, so state it plainly: the rich should act as trustees for the poor. Part b asks how technology made the fortune possible, so name the Bessemer process and cheap steel. Part c asks for a different response, so name labor organizing, or reformers who wanted government action.
