Who gets to vote — and who gets to decide what voting means? In the 1820s, the answer changed — the age of **Jacksonian democracy**, politics built on expanded white male suffrage, mass parties, popular campaigning, and a strong presidency. State after state dropped the rule that a voter must own property, and ordinary white men crowded into politics as voters, campaigners, and officeholders. This lesson follows that opening, the men who rode it to power, and the people it left outside.

## The ballot box opens — for white men


In 1800, only five states let voters choose presidential electors directly. Most states required a voter to own property, shutting out landless men. The new states of the West led the change: six states admitted between 1816 and 1821 wrote broader voting rules into their constitutions. Land was cheap in the West, so fewer men were landless. By 1840, about 90 percent of adult white men could vote. By 1824, eighteen of twenty-four states let voters choose electors directly.

New voters changed how campaigns ran: candidates campaigned harder and tailored their appeal to a broader audience, and voters judged character and personality as much as policy. The election of 1828 showed it: Jackson's supporters painted John Quincy Adams as out of touch and elitist, while Adams's supporters portrayed Jackson as ill-tempered.

Turnout told the same story, rising from 26.9 percent of eligible voters in 1824 to 57.6 percent in 1828, and holding high — 55.4 percent in 1832, 57.8 percent in 1836, and 80.2 percent in 1840.

A French visitor saw the change as a way of life, not only as voting rules. Alexis de Tocqueville toured the United States in 1831 and wrote that nothing struck him more than "the general equality of conditions." He watched Americans form associations — for business, religion, charity, and politics — and concluded that equality shaped laws, opinions, and habits alike.

Tocqueville spent nine months traveling, mostly in the free states, and his account reflects what he saw there. But the opening had a boundary: the voters were free white men, while women, enslaved people, and many free Black men stayed outside the ballot box.

## The people's president — and his critics


Andrew Jackson rode the new politics to the White House. In 1824 he won the most popular and electoral votes, yet the House chose John Quincy Adams, who then named Henry Clay secretary of state. Jackson's supporters called it a **corrupt bargain**, and Jackson kept the charge alive for four years, writing in 1825 that how Adams reached the office was a question "for the succeeding canvass." A disputed count would decide a later election by bargain too: in 1876 Hayes became president after a disputed count.

He won the rematch in 1828, and his victory swept in the **spoils system**, giving government jobs to political supporters. Jackson defended it on the ground that any capable citizen could do the work.

Look at the cartoon below. Jackson's opponents answered his strong presidency with a royal portrait: crown, robes, scepter, a "VETO" scroll and the torn Constitution under his feet.

![King Andrew the First, 1832](u4/pr25e-test2-q27.jpg)
Figure 1: King Andrew the First, 1832. Library of Congress, public domain.

The cartoon's fear was real even though its charge was partisan. Jackson's veto of the Bank's recharter in 1832 made him a hero to friends and a king to enemies. A second print of 1836 cheered him on, slaying the Bank's many-headed monster with his "VETO" cane.

## The opening's limits

In Massachusetts, Daniel Webster led conservatives arguing that "power naturally and necessarily follows property," and the state kept its rule that voters must be taxpayers after dropping the property rule. In Rhode Island, the cause was the same squeeze: the state kept property qualifications while a growing landless industrial workforce could not vote.

Rhode Island reformers organized a People's Convention in 1841, wrote a new constitution, and won an overwhelming referendum — then watched the state militia put the **Dorr Rebellion** down and Thomas Dorr go to prison, after President Tyler declined to send federal troops.

Martin Van Buren, Jackson's vice president and successor, inherited the Panic of 1837 and lost the election of 1840 to William Henry Harrison, a military hero of Tippecanoe, Fort Meigs, and the Thames, sold with log cabins and hard cider. Harrison died a month after taking office, and Vice President John Tyler succeeded him — the first vice-presidential succession after a president's death.

## The Bank War: Jackson against the "monster"


Jackson's fiercest fight was the **Bank War**, his campaign against the Second Bank of the United States. The Bank held the government's deposits, issued notes that served as a national paper currency, and kept state banks from printing paper money without restraint. The Bank's president, Nicholas Biddle, ran it through branches across the country — one powerful banker behind the whole system.

Jackson called the Bank a privilege for the rich. In 1832, four years before its charter expired, Henry Clay pushed a recharter bill through Congress, betting a veto would hurt Jackson's reelection. Jackson vetoed it, and his veto message — the rich bending government to their selfish purposes — played so well that he won reelection easily. His veto message also claimed each branch of government must judge the Constitution for itself — the Supreme Court's decision upholding the Bank did not settle the question.

He then moved federal deposits to state banks his critics called "pet banks." He vetoed twelve bills in all, more than the ten vetoed by all his predecessors combined. In 1836 he issued the Specie Circular, requiring gold or silver for land to curb speculation.

## Nullification: a state defies the Union


The same president who fought the Bank defended the Union against South Carolina in the **nullification crisis**. The Tariff of 1828, called the "Tariff of Abominations," raised the cost of imported goods and hurt cotton-exporting South Carolina, which depended on selling cotton abroad and buying British goods.

John C. Calhoun, Jackson's own vice president, argued that the Constitution was a compact among sovereign states, so a state could declare a federal law void within its borders. In November 1832, South Carolina's convention did just that, nullifying the tariffs of 1828 and 1832.

Jackson answered with a proclamation: the Constitution "forms a government, not a league," and no state could annul a federal law. Daniel Webster made the same argument in his Second Reply to Hayne in January 1830: no single state could void a federal law and stay in the Union.

Congress settled the crisis with two laws on March 2, 1833: the Force Bill, authorizing troops to collect the tariff, and a compromise tariff cutting rates to 20 percent by 1842. South Carolina accepted the lower tariff and nullified the Force Bill as a parting gesture.

## Removal and the Whig answer


Jackson used federal power differently against Native peoples: the **Indian Removal Act** of 1830 authorized moving the eastern tribes west of the Mississippi.

Removal was already federal policy before Jackson: Monroe had called for it in 1824, and Jackson's 1829 message repeated the call, presenting it as protection for the tribes.

Georgia wanted Cherokee land, especially after gold was found there. The Cherokee fought back in the courts: in Worcester v. Georgia (1832) the Supreme Court ruled Georgia's laws had no force in Cherokee territory, and Jackson refused to enforce the ruling. The Cherokee had built their own written language — Sequoyah's syllabary — and their own press, the Cherokee Phoenix.

Look at the map below. Routes run from the Cherokee homelands west to Tahlequah in Oklahoma, by way of Nashville, Hopkinsville, and Cape Girardeau, down the Tennessee and Mississippi rivers.

![Trail of Tears National Historic Trail map](u4/pr25e-test3-q19.jpg)
Figure 2: Trail of Tears National Historic Trail map. Land, water, and other routes. U.S. National Park Service, public domain.

In 1838 federal troops escorted the Cherokee west, and thousands died on the march, remembered as the **Trail of Tears**. The Bank War and Specie Circular contributed to the Panic of 1837. After removal, the Cherokee established a new homeland and government in Indian Territory. After Harrison's death, Tyler vetoed the Whigs' bank bills.

The opposition to Jackson became the Whig Party, and the rivalry between the Whigs and Jackson's Democratic Party — the Second Party System — shaped American politics for a generation. Democrats and Whigs offered opposing visions of federal economic power — the Whigs a national bank, tariffs, and internal improvements, the Democrats none. A voter who favored Henry Clay's American System would vote for Clay, the Whigs' leading figure in 1832.

## An American culture of their own


After the War of 1812, Americans wanted a culture to match their political independence. In 1828 Noah Webster published An American Dictionary of the English Language with American spellings — color for colour, center for centre.

The new culture borrowed from Europe. **Romanticism** reacted against the market revolution — against routine work and nature treated as a resource — prizing feeling, imagination, and wild nature over cold reason. Americans loved Sir Walter Scott's Ivanhoe, but American writers set that romantic feeling in forests and frontiers instead.

Look at the painting below. Thomas Cole's The Oxbow (1836) splits in two: storm-darkened wilderness on the left, sunny farmland on the right. Cole founded the **Hudson River School**, which made the American wilderness its subject. His Course of Empire (1833–36) carried an imaginary city from wilderness to ruin, a warning that republics could fall.

![The Oxbow, 1836](u4/original-misc-us-culture-13.jpg)
Figure 3: The Oxbow, 1836, by Thomas Cole. Metropolitan Museum of Art, public domain.

Irving's "Rip Van Winkle" (1819) turned an American village into popular fiction — its hero wakes to find King George's portrait replaced by Washington's, the Revolution remaking everyday life — and Irving invented Diedrich Knickerbocker, a humorous, homegrown voice. Cooper's The Last of the Mohicans (1826) won readers in Europe as well as America, though his Native peoples are a romantic stereotype, the vanishing Indian. Then came the **American Renaissance**: Hawthorne's The Scarlet Letter (1850), Melville's Moby-Dick (1851), Thoreau's Walden (1854), and Whitman's Leaves of Grass (1855).

## Transcendentalism and its reach

Behind them stood **Transcendentalism**, a small circle around Ralph Waldo Emerson and Henry David Thoreau. They argued that truth lived inside each person, reached by intuition and nature rather than by churches or tradition. Emerson's "Self-Reliance" (1841) told readers to trust themselves, and his 1837 "American Scholar" address to the Phi Beta Kappa Society at Harvard, an audience of educated men, demanded intellectual independence.

Thoreau lived it at Walden Pond, went to the woods to live simply and learn what life truly required, and later argued in "Civil Disobedience" (1849) that conscience outranks law and unjust government should not be supported. Margaret Fuller carried transcendentalism to women in Woman in the Nineteenth Century (1845), arguing for women's equality and self-development — the argument the women's rights movement took up at Seneca Falls in 1848.

Emerson's call for intellectual independence — an American Declaration of Independence for the mind — shaped the writers who followed.

## Key terms


- **Jacksonian democracy** — The politics of the 1820s and 1830s built on expanded white male suffrage, mass parties, popular campaigning, and a strong presidency.

- **Corrupt bargain** — Jackson supporters' charge that Adams won the presidency in 1824 through a House deal with Henry Clay, who then became secretary of state.

- **Spoils system** — The practice of giving government jobs to political supporters, defended by Jackson on the ground that any capable citizen could do the work.

- **Dorr Rebellion** — The 1841–1842 Rhode Island uprising in which democratic reformers wrote their own constitution and won a referendum, only to be put down by the state militia.

- **Bank War** — Jackson's campaign against the Second Bank of the United States, fought with his 1832 veto and the removal of federal deposits.

- **Nullification crisis** — South Carolina's 1832–1833 attempt to declare federal tariff acts void inside the state, answered by Jackson's proclamation and the Force Bill.

- **Indian Removal Act** — The 1830 law authorizing the removal of eastern tribes to land west of the Mississippi.

- **Trail of Tears** — The forced Cherokee march west in 1838, on which thousands died.

- **Romanticism** — A movement valuing feeling, imagination, and nature over cold reason and routine, imported from Europe and given American settings.

- **Hudson River School** — American landscape painters, founded by Thomas Cole, who made the wilderness itself the subject of grand paintings.

- **American Renaissance** — The flowering of American literature in the early 1850s, including Hawthorne, Melville, Thoreau, and Whitman.

- **Transcendentalism** — The belief, centered on Emerson and Thoreau, that truth lives inside each person and is reached by intuition and nature.

Model answer: Most states had reduced or removed property qualifications for voting. Most free white men could now vote, so candidates had to win over a much larger and less elite electorate.

Model answer: The expanded electorate still covered only free white men. Women, enslaved people, and many free Black men could not vote, so the “common man” in politics was a white man.

The reasoning to practice: part (a) names a change in how campaigns worked, part (b) gives a cause for that change, and part (c) tests the boundary by asking who was still left out.
