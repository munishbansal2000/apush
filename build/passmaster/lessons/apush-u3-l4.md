Why did the government that won the war fail in peacetime — and what did Americans build to replace it? This lesson answers that question. The **Articles of Confederation** left Congress unable to tax, enforce, or keep order, so Americans wrote a Constitution that could govern.

## A league, not a nation

The **Articles of Confederation**, adopted by Congress on November 15, 1777, created a firm league of friendship — a league of separate, sovereign states, not a single nation. Article II said each state retained its sovereignty, freedom, and independence, and every power not expressly delegated to Congress. Article III bound the states to assist each other for their common defense and the security of their liberties. There was no president, no national court, and no way for Congress to reach individual citizens.

Article VIII shows the flaw. The expenses of the common defense were to be paid from a common treasury, supplied by the states in proportion to the value of land in each state — but the taxes were laid and levied by the state legislatures, not by Congress. Congress could only send each state a **requisition**, a request for money or men that the state could meet late or not at all. It could not tax citizens, regulate trade, or raise an army of its own.

Hamilton later named the fault: the Confederation legislated for states, not for the individuals who made them up. Congress's resolutions were laws in theory but recommendations in practice, since any important measure required the agreement of thirteen distinct sovereign wills. Amending the Articles required all thirteen states to agree, so Robert Morris's 5 percent impost, approved by twelve states, failed.

Under the Articles, each state had one vote in Congress, whatever its size. The 1790 census counted 747,610 people in Virginia and 59,094 in Delaware, yet Virginia and Delaware cast equal votes. Congress could act abroad — it could make treaties and coin money — but it could not tax or raise an army at home. That gap between foreign power and domestic weakness is what the 1780s exposed.

## The West and the crisis

The Articles produced one great success: a plan for the West. After the states ceded their western land claims, the Land Ordinance of 1785 ordered the land north of the Ohio surveyed before sale or settlement. Surveyors divided it into townships six miles square, each cut into thirty-six sections of 640 acres. Lot 16 was reserved for public schools, and the smallest purchase was one whole section — 640 acres for at least $640.

The **Northwest Ordinance** of July 13, 1787, went further: it gave the United States a plan for turning a territory into new states. In the first stage, Congress named a governor and three judges to rule the territory. When 5,000 free adult men lived there, voters could elect an assembly. At 60,000 free inhabitants, it could join the Union on an equal footing.

Article VI banned slavery in the territory — though its fugitive proviso let enslavers reclaim people who escaped into it — and Article III promised schools and good faith toward Native peoples, who already lived there.

The crisis came in Massachusetts. Farmers facing debt and foreclosure rose in **Shays's Rebellion** in 1786–1787, and Congress could do nothing to stop it — a Massachusetts militia put it down, proving the national government had no army of its own.

Washington wrote to Henry Lee in October 1786: "Influence is no government. Let us have one by which our lives, liberties, and properties will be secured, or let us know the worst at once." Jefferson, writing from Paris in January 1787, took the opposite view: "a little rebellion now and then is a good thing."

The road to Philadelphia ran through Annapolis: in September 1786, commissioners from only five states met there and called for a broader convention at Philadelphia. Shays's Rebellion gave that call urgency.

## The Convention's bargains

The delegates who met in Philadelphia in the summer of 1787 had been sent to fix the Articles. Within weeks they agreed to scrap them and write a new frame of government. The hardest fights were over representation. The Virginia Plan, proposed by James Madison, gave Congress two houses with seats by population. The New Jersey Plan, proposed by William Paterson, kept one house with one vote for each state. Small states feared the larger states would outvote them; large states said votes should follow people.

The **Great Compromise** settled it: the House of Representatives by population, the Senate with two seats per state. The 1790 census made the stakes plain — Virginia, with 747,610 people, would hold ten House seats to Delaware's one. The compromise gave each side half of what it wanted, and it locked the small states' equal Senate voice into the Constitution so firmly that Article V protects it from amendment without that state's consent.

Slavery was bargained over too. The **Three-Fifths Compromise** counted enslaved people as three-fifths of a person for representation: Virginia counted 630,559 people out of 747,610 in total, because 292,627 enslaved people counted at three-fifths. Northern delegates objected that enslaved people could not vote and were treated as property, so they should add no seats — but the compromise gave southern states extra seats for enslaved people who could not vote. The Convention also agreed to protect the slave trade for twenty years, postponing the question rather than settling it.

Historians still argue about why the Constitution replaced the Articles: Charles Beard's 1913 study stressed that wealthy leaders wanted a stronger government to protect their property, while others stress that ordinary farmers feared any distant power.

## Ratification: Federalists and Anti-Federalists

The finished Constitution then went to the states, where ratification was far from certain.

Madison made the **Federalists**' case in The Federalist No. 10, published November 22, 1787. A **faction**, he wrote, is citizens united by a passion or interest against the rights of others. Extending the republic over a large territory helps control faction, because more interests stop any one majority from forming.

In Federalist No. 51, he added that ambition must be made to counteract ambition, because men are not angels. That is **separation of powers**: legislative, executive, and judicial branches, each checking the others.

The title page of The Federalist, first collected edition, 1788, says the essays were "written in favour of the new Constitution" — addressed to the people of New York, to win support for ratification.

![Title page of The Federalist, first collected edition, 1788](u3/federalist-title-page-1788.jpg)
Figure 1: Title page of "The Federalist," first collected edition, New York, 1788. "Written in Favour of the new Constitution." Public domain.

Brutus, writing for the **Anti-Federalists** in October 1787, asked whether the thirteen states should be reduced to one great republic or continue as thirteen confederated republics. He feared one consolidated national government.

Patrick Henry asked who authorized the framers to speak "We, the People" instead of "We, the States." George Mason's objections, written in September 1787, began with what was missing: a declaration of rights, since federal law is paramount to state laws.

The votes told the story. Delaware ratified first, on December 7, 1787, by 30 to 0. Small states ratified quickly; large states were slow — Massachusetts by 187 to 168. New Hampshire, the ninth state, ratified on June 21, 1788; Virginia followed on June 25. North Carolina and Rhode Island held out until 1789 and 1790.

## The Constitution at work

The engrossed Constitution begins "We the People of the United States" — its authority comes from the people, not from the states.

![First page of the engrossed Constitution, 1787](u3/constitution-first-page-1787.jpg)
Figure 2: First page of the engrossed Constitution, signed September 17, 1787. "We the People" and Article I, Section 2. Public domain.

The Preamble says the Constitution was ordained "to form a more perfect Union" and to serve "ourselves and our Posterity," a lasting frame for future generations.

Article I, Section 8 lists Congress's powers: to tax, borrow, regulate commerce, coin money, and raise armies. The list ends with the necessary and proper clause, often called the **elastic clause**, which lets Congress carry out its powers.

Power was also divided between two levels of government. The national government received listed powers, such as taxing and declaring war, while the states kept the powers not given away — and some powers, like levying taxes and establishing courts, belong to both. The **supremacy clause** of Article VI makes the Constitution and federal law the supreme law of the land, and binds the judges in every state to follow it. Article VI also says no religious test shall ever be required for federal office.

Each branch could check the others. The president could veto a bill, and only a two-thirds vote of both houses could override it. The Senate had to consent to treaties and appointments, and the House could impeach and the Senate try a president. Judges held office during good behaviour, so they could rule against the other branches.

The system was tested at once. Congress debated a national bank, the Senate weighed treaties, and the Judiciary Act of 1789 set up the federal courts. The new government began operating in 1789, with Washington inaugurated and the first Congress meeting.

## The Bill of Rights

Article V lets the Constitution be amended, but only with broad agreement: two-thirds of Congress to propose, three-fourths of the states to ratify.

The Bill of Rights added a further limit — not on how the branches share power, but on what the government may do to people. Congress proposed twelve amendments on September 25, 1789; ten were ratified on December 15, 1791. One of the other two later became the Twenty-seventh Amendment.

The amendments were the promise Federalists had made to win ratification: Massachusetts had ratified only after Federalists promised to recommend them, and the promise carried the close conventions.

![The engrossed Bill of Rights, proposed September 25, 1789](u3/bill-of-rights-engrossed-1789.jpg)
Figure 3: The engrossed Bill of Rights, proposed September 25, 1789. Congress proposed twelve amendments; ten were ratified in 1791. Public domain.

The First Amendment bars Congress from establishing a church or abridging speech, press, assembly, or petition. The Fourth requires a warrant on probable cause naming the place to be searched. The Fifth requires a grand jury indictment and bars trying a person twice for the same crime. The Sixth promises a speedy and public trial.

The Eighth bars excessive fines and cruel punishments. The Ninth says that listing some rights does not deny others retained by the people. The Tenth reserves to the states, or to the people, the powers the Constitution neither gives to the national government nor forbids to the states. Even a law passed by both houses and signed by the president cannot do what the Bill of Rights forbids.

Model answer: Shays's Rebellion showed the weakness because Congress could do nothing to stop it. The rebellion was put down by a Massachusetts militia, which proved the national government had no army of its own and could not keep order.

### Worked item 3 — apush-saq-0089 (Topic 3.9)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage: the Constitution built a national government of three branches that check each other, divided power between the national government and the states, and could be amended — and the Bill of Rights added further limits.

a. Identify ONE feature of the Constitution's structure described in the passage.

Model answer: One feature is the division of the national government into three branches: Congress, the president, and the courts. Each branch has its own job, and each can check the others, so no one branch holds all power.

b. Explain ONE way a feature described in the passage limited the power of the national government.

Model answer: The veto and the override limit power because a bill needs both Congress and the president to become law, and a two-thirds vote of Congress can still defeat a veto. Neither side can act alone, so each must win the other's agreement or a supermajority.

c. Explain ONE way the Bill of Rights added a further limit on the national government.

How to think about it: Part a asks for one feature of the structure, so name the three branches or the two levels. Part b asks how a feature limited the national government, so follow the veto and the override. Part c asks what the Bill of Rights added, so name a right no branch may take away.

## Key terms

- **Articles of Confederation** — the 1777 frame of government that made the United States a firm league of friendship among sovereign states.

- **requisition** — Congress's request that a state supply money or men, which the state could meet late or not at all.

- **Northwest Ordinance** — the 1787 law that set the path from territory to statehood and banned slavery north of the Ohio River.

- **Shays's Rebellion** — the 1786–1787 uprising of Massachusetts farmers over debt and foreclosure, which Congress could not stop.

- **Great Compromise** — the Convention's bargain: House seats by population, Senate two seats per state.

- **Three-Fifths Compromise** — the rule counting enslaved people as three-fifths of a person for representation.

- **Federalists** — supporters of ratification, who argued that checks and balances in a large republic would keep power safe.

- **Anti-Federalists** — opponents of ratification, who feared a distant national government would grow too strong to control.

- **faction** — citizens united by a passion or interest against the rights of others, in Madison's Federalist No. 10.

- **separation of powers** — the division of government among legislative, executive, and judicial branches, each checking the others.

- **supremacy clause** — Article VI's rule that the Constitution and federal law are the supreme law of the land.

- **elastic clause** — the necessary and proper clause, letting Congress carry out its listed powers.
