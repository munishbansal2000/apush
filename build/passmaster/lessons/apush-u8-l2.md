The soldiers came home to a country that had never been richer and never felt safer, and most Americans believed the good years would last. Yet the boom rested on choices that left many people out, and the peace at home was already cracking. This lesson answers how the postwar boom remade where Americans lived and what they watched, and how the first cracks in segregation opened.

## The boom

The economy boomed because the war had left the United States rich and its people ready to spend. Workers had saved wartime wages, since there was little to buy, and after 1945 they spent those savings on cars, houses, and appliances. Credit made it easier: installment buying spread, and families bought now and paid later. Gross national product grew strongly despite recessions, and by 1960 most families owned a car, a refrigerator, and a television set.

The **GI Bill** turned soldiers into students and homeowners. It paid college tuition for millions of veterans and offered low-interest loans to buy houses. College enrollment more than doubled, and a generation of first-generation students entered the professions. But the bill worked best for white veterans: Black veterans faced banks that would not lend and colleges that would not admit them, and the benefits flowed unevenly by race.

The boom did not reach everyone. In 1962, Michael Harrington's book The Other America said that 40 to 50 million Americans still lived in poverty, in slums and in rural counties the boom had passed by. The census put the poverty rate at 22.4 percent in 1959. Labor had its own battles: after a wave of strikes in 1946, Congress passed the **Taft-Hartley Act** in 1947, over President Truman's veto, limiting union power and banning the closed shop.

Johnson's Great Society answered Harrington with the War on Poverty and the Office of Economic Opportunity, and with Medicare and Medicaid in 1965, giving health care to older and poor Americans — and he signed Medicare with Harry Truman beside him, honoring the man who first proposed national health insurance in 1945.

## Suburbs and highways

**Levittown** showed how the suburbs were built. On Long Island, William Levitt used assembly-line methods to raise thousands of identical houses, and buyers moved in as fast as builders could finish them. Federal loans from the FHA and the VA made the houses cheap to buy. Levittowns rose across the country, and the suburbs grew faster than the cities around them.

The suburbs were not open to everyone. Deeds carried covenants barring Black families, and banks used redlining, refusing loans in Black neighborhoods while lending freely in white ones. Of 3,200 VA-backed loans in Mississippi, only two went to Black veterans. The suburbs grew whiter as the cities grew Blacker.

Families filled the new houses. The **baby boom** ran from 1946 to 1964, and 1957 saw 4,302,000 births, the largest number of the boom. New schools, new shopping centers, and new churches followed the children into the suburbs. The children of the boom would grow up to fill the classrooms, the colleges, and the protest movements of the 1960s.

Parents raising the boom's children turned to experts: Dr. Benjamin Spock's The Common Sense Book of Baby and Child Care (1946) told parents to trust their own judgment, not strict schedules and harsh discipline.

The federal government paved the way out of the cities. The **Interstate Highway System**, created in 1956, built more than 40,000 miles of highways, letting workers live far from their jobs and letting trucks move goods cheaply. The highways also carried people south and west, to the Sunbelt, where warm weather, air conditioning, and defense plants drew jobs and families from the older industrial states.

In 1973 Arab producers cut off oil exports to the United States, and drivers waited in long lines at empty pumps.

## One nation, watching together

Television made the suburbs one audience. In 1950, only 9.0 percent of American households owned a set. By 1955 the share was 64.5 percent, and by 1960 it was 87.1 percent. Families gathered each evening around the same programs, and the country began to watch the same shows and hear the same jokes. The photograph shows a family of five in a living room, watching a small television together.

![Family of five watching television in a living room, 1958](u8/family-watching-tv-1958.jpg)
Figure 1: A family watches television together, 1958. Public domain (National Archives photograph).

Television also changed politics. In the 1960 presidential debates, people who watched on television thought John F. Kennedy had won, while people who listened on the radio thought Richard Nixon had won. The camera rewarded the younger, calmer man, and candidates learned that how they looked mattered as much as what they said. Politics had become a visual medium.

Work itself changed. White-collar jobs in offices, sales, and management grew faster than factory jobs, and by 1956 more Americans worked in services than in production. Defense spending stayed high even in peacetime. When President Eisenhower left office in January 1961, he warned against the influence of the military-industrial complex, a partnership of defense firms and the armed services that profited from the Cold War.

The culture of the 1950s prized fitting in. William Whyte's book The Organization Man described executives who traded independence for security inside big companies. Families were told that the suburban home, the steady job, and the well-behaved children were the measure of a good life. The message reached millions, and those who did not fit felt the gap.

The domestic Cold War enforced conformity too: McCarthyism punished people accused of disloyalty, so many kept quiet rather than drawing attention to themselves.

## New sounds and new voices

**Rock 'n' roll** began in Black music: rhythm and blues, gospel, and country, played loud for teenagers. White performers carried it to white audiences, and Elvis Presley carried it furthest. The photograph shows Elvis in 1956, at the start of his fame. Parents heard noise and danger; teenagers heard their own music for the first time, and the record industry followed the money.

![Elvis Presley in 1956, arms folded](u8/elvis-presley-1956.jpg)
Figure 2: Elvis Presley, 1956. Public domain (publicity photograph).

Writers pushed back against the same conformity. The **Beat writers**, Jack Kerouac and Allen Ginsberg among them, wrote about life on the road and in the cities, outside the suburban routine. Kerouac's On the Road appeared in 1957, and Ginsberg's poem Howl attacked a society he called starving and hysterical. They spoke to young readers who wanted something looser than the Organization Man's life.

Painters and thinkers joined them. Jackson Pollock's **abstract expressionism** abandoned recognizable scenes for drips and splashes, and critics called it the first truly American painting. In 1963, Betty Friedan's The Feminine Mystique named the problem that has no name: educated women trapped in the suburbs, with no work that used their minds. The book sold millions and gave a name to a feeling many women already had.

Friedan's book led to action: in 1966 she helped found the National Organization for Women, which pressed for equal pay and equal opportunity.

By the late 1960s the challenge had become a counterculture: young people chose peace, rock music, and communal living over the Organization Man's life, and the New Left's Students for a Democratic Society carried the revolt onto campuses. In August 1969 some 400,000 people gathered at Woodstock, a music festival that became the counterculture's high point.

## Early steps toward equality

The push began in the war — and in Washington. In 1941 President Roosevelt's Executive Order 8802 banned discrimination in defense work and created the Fair Employment Practices Committee, the first federal agency for fair employment.

Black newspapers called for the **Double V**: victory over fascism abroad and over racism at home. After the war, President Truman ordered the armed forces desegregated by **Executive Order 9981** in July 1948. The services carried it out in 1949 and 1950, and the Korean War sped it along.

In baseball, the color line fell. On April 15, 1947, Jackie Robinson took the field for the Brooklyn Dodgers, the first Black player in the modern major leagues. The photograph shows Robinson in his Dodgers uniform, bat ready, the number 42 on his back. Rookie of the Year in 1947, his steady play under abuse opened the door for other Black players.

![Jackie Robinson in Dodgers uniform, bat ready](u8/jackie-robinson-1954.jpg)
Figure 3: Jackie Robinson with the Brooklyn Dodgers, 1954. Public domain (Library of Congress photograph).

The courts moved next. In 1950, Sweatt v. Painter and McLaurin v. Oklahoma ordered Black students admitted to graduate schools on equal terms. Then, in 1954, **Brown v. Board of Education** ruled separate schools unequal by nature, overturning Plessy v. Ferguson. A year later, Brown II told districts to desegregate with all deliberate speed, words that let many delay for years.

The Cold War pushed these steps along. Truman spoke out in 1947 partly because segregation hurt America abroad, where the Soviet Union pointed to racism as proof that American democracy was false. Seven years later, the Court decided Brown while the world watched the same contradiction.

## Boycott, Little Rock, and the movement grows

On December 1, 1955, Rosa Parks refused to give up her seat on a Montgomery bus. The photograph shows her being fingerprinted after her re-arrest in February 1956. Her arrest set off the **Montgomery Bus Boycott**, which lasted more than a year until the Supreme Court ruled bus segregation unconstitutional. In 1957, Eisenhower sent the 101st Airborne to escort nine Black students into Little Rock's Central High School, and Congress passed the Civil Rights Act of 1957, the first such law since Reconstruction.

The boycott made its young leader, Martin Luther King Jr., a national figure, and it showed later campaigns that disciplined nonviolent protest could win.

The act mainly protected the right to vote, and its enforcement was weak — but it created the Civil Rights Commission and a Civil Rights Division in the Justice Department, new federal machinery for the fights ahead.

The movement kept growing: in 1957 King helped found the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, and in 1960 students in Greensboro, North Carolina, began the sit-ins at a segregated lunch counter.

## Worked items

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0242 (Topic 8.4)

The stimulus is a paraphrase of Michael Harrington's The Other America (1962).

> The writer Michael Harrington argued in The Other America (1962) that 40 to 50 million Americans lived in poverty in the early 1960s, many in decaying city slums and many in isolated rural towns. He wrote that machines and automation, which raised output for the nation, took jobs from farm workers and factory workers and left them poor.

Source: Paraphrase of Michael Harrington, The Other America (1962), as summarized in Barron's Premium 2027, Chapter 10, Poverty Amid Affluence.

a. Identify one group Harrington says lived in poverty.

Model answer: Harrington says many poor Americans lived in decaying city slums, where old neighborhoods had fallen into disrepair. He also names poor Americans in isolated rural towns, far from the jobs and services of the cities.

b. Explain how Harrington's account challenged the image of postwar affluence.

Model answer: Harrington challenged the affluent image by counting 40 to 50 million poor Americans beside a growing middle class. His number showed that prosperity had not reached everyone, since automation took jobs even as national output rose.

c. Explain one way the federal government responded to the poverty Harrington described.

Model answer: The federal government answered with the Great Society, creating the Office of Economic Opportunity to oversee antipoverty programs. It also created Medicare and Medicaid in 1965, giving health care to older and poor Americans.

How to think about it: part a asks for a group, so name the city slums or the isolated rural towns. Part b asks how Harrington challenged the affluent image, so use his count of 40 to 50 million poor beside a growing middle class. Part c asks what the book's argument meant for the 1960s, so connect it to the War on Poverty.

### Worked item 2 — apush-saq-0245 (Topic 8.5)

The stimulus is a paraphrase of Betty Friedan's The Feminine Mystique (1963).

> Paraphrase of Betty Friedan, The Feminine Mystique (1963). Friedan wrote that many suburban housewives felt a deep dissatisfaction they could not name, which she called 'the problem that has no name.' These women had homes, husbands, and children, yet they felt empty and asked, in effect, 'Is this all?'

Source: Paraphrase of Betty Friedan, The Feminine Mystique, 1963. Paraphrased, not quoted, except for two short phrases.

a. Identify one group Friedan says felt 'the problem that has no name.'

Model answer: Friedan says suburban housewives felt the problem. These women had homes, husbands, and children, yet they felt empty and unsatisfied with their lives.

b. Explain one reason many suburban women felt frustrated in the 1950s and early 1960s.

Model answer: The postwar ideal told women that a home, a husband, and children should satisfy them fully. Women who wanted work, study, or a life beyond the house found that ideal left them with no respected role outside homemaking.

c. Explain one effect of Friedan's book on the women's movement of the 1960s.

Model answer: Friedan's book gave a name to feelings many women shared, which helped them organize together. In 1966 Friedan helped found the National Organization for Women, which pressed for laws on equal pay and equal opportunity.

How to think about it: part a asks for a group, so name the suburban housewives. Part b asks how the book explained it, so use the culture that told women fulfillment came only through home and family. Part c asks what the book changed, so name the National Organization for Women, founded in 1966.

### Worked item 3 — apush-saq-0246 (Topic 8.6)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage on the Montgomery Bus Boycott.

> The Montgomery Bus Boycott did not begin with a single act of defiance. For years before Rosa Parks was arrested, the Women's Political Council in Montgomery had planned a boycott of the city buses.

> When Parks was arrested on December 1, 1955, its leader, Jo Ann Robinson, wrote a leaflet that night, and she and her students ran off thousands of copies overnight, and Black residents of Montgomery stayed off the buses the next day. The boycott then lasted for more than a year, held together by carpools, church networks, and the Montgomery Improvement Association, led by the young minister Martin Luther King Jr.

Source: Original secondary passage written for this question; facts from the grounding books.

a. Identify one group the passage says helped plan or carry out the Montgomery Bus Boycott.

Model answer: The passage says the Women's Political Council had planned a boycott. Its leader, Jo Ann Robinson, wrote a leaflet the night of the arrest and ran off thousands of copies overnight.

b. Explain one reason the boycott was able to last for more than a year.

Model answer: The boycott lasted because the community built its own way to travel, with carpools and church networks carrying people who refused to ride the buses. That support system let ordinary residents keep the boycott going day after day.

c. Explain one effect of the Montgomery Bus Boycott on the civil rights movement.

Model answer: The boycott made Martin Luther King Jr. a national figure, since he led the Montgomery Improvement Association through the long campaign. It also showed that disciplined nonviolent protest could win, which encouraged the movement's later campaigns.

How to think about it: part a asks for a group, so name the Women's Political Council, the Montgomery Improvement Association, or the church networks. Part b asks how the boycott lasted, so use the carpools and church networks that carried people who refused to ride. Part c asks what the boycott showed, so name the power of sustained nonviolent protest — and that it made King a national figure.

## Key terms

- **GI Bill** — the 1944 law that paid college tuition and offered low-interest home loans to returning veterans.

- **Levittown** — the mass-produced suburban community on Long Island, built by assembly-line methods and copied across the country.

- **Baby boom** — the sharp rise in births from 1946 to 1964, peaking at 4,302,000 births in 1957.

- **Taft-Hartley Act** — the 1947 law, passed over Truman's veto, that limited union power and banned the closed shop.

- **Interstate Highway System** — the federally built network of more than 40,000 miles of highways, created in 1956.

- **Rock 'n' roll** — the new popular music of the 1950s, rooted in Black rhythm and blues, gospel, and country, and carried to teenagers by performers like Elvis Presley.

- **Beat writers** — the writers of the 1950s, led by Jack Kerouac and Allen Ginsberg, who rejected suburban conformity for life on the road and in the cities.

- **Abstract expressionism** — the postwar American painting style, led by Jackson Pollock, that abandoned recognizable scenes for drips, splashes, and fields of color.

- **Double V** — the Black press's call during the Second World War for victory over fascism abroad and victory over racism at home.

- **Executive Order 9981** — President Truman's July 26, 1948 order desegregating the armed forces, carried out in 1949 and 1950.

- **Brown v. Board of Education** — the Supreme Court's 1954 decision that separate schools were unequal by nature, overturning Plessy v. Ferguson.

- **Montgomery Bus Boycott** — the boycott of Montgomery's buses, lasting more than a year after Rosa Parks's arrest in 1955, that ended bus segregation.
