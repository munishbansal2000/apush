By 1900, Americans could see that industrial growth had made the country rich and unequal at once. This lesson answers a question that ran through the next twenty years: could the government fix what the market had broken, and could it do so without breaking the country's freedoms? Reformers tried at home, and then a world war tested their answers.

## What the Progressives wanted

**Progressivism** was the reform movement of the early 1900s that used government power to curb big business, clean up cities, and expand democracy. Its supporters were mostly middle-class city people who had watched trusts fix prices, bosses run cities, and factories injure workers. They believed that experts, honest government, and new laws could fix these problems. They did not agree on everything, but they shared a faith in reform through government.

**Muckrakers** were the investigative journalists whose exposés built public pressure for reform. Ida Tarbell showed how Standard Oil crushed rivals. Lincoln Steffens showed how city bosses traded favors for bribes. Upton Sinclair's novel The Jungle, published in 1906, described filthy conditions in Chicago's packing plants. Readers who had never seen a packing plant demanded laws, and Congress answered within months.

Reform started in cities and states. Jane Addams opened Hull House in Chicago in 1889, a settlement house where immigrants could find classes, child care, and help with city services. Reform mayors took over water and gas systems to serve citizens rather than private companies. Wisconsin governor Robert La Follette regulated railroads and pushed the direct primary, letting voters choose candidates instead of party bosses. Progressives also pushed the initiative, the referendum, and the recall, tools that let voters make laws and remove officials directly.

The Triangle Shirtwaist fire showed why reformers wanted safety laws. In March 1911, a fire swept through a New York shirtwaist factory where workers, mostly young immigrant women, found the doors locked. One hundred forty-six workers died. The fire convinced many Progressives that the government had to set workplace rules, because owners left alone would not. New York passed new factory safety laws within two years.

## Three presidents and reform

Theodore Roosevelt made reform a presidential cause. His **Square Deal** promised a fair deal for workers, consumers, and business alike, with the government as referee. In 1902 he ordered the government to break up the Northern Securities Company, a railroad holding company, and the Supreme Court upheld the breakup in 1904. Roosevelt called his use of the Sherman Antitrust Act **trust-busting**, and he brought more than forty cases.

Roosevelt also protected consumers and nature. After The Jungle, Congress passed the Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act in 1906, giving the federal government power to inspect meat and regulate food and drugs. Roosevelt set aside 230 million acres of public land for forests and parks, arguing the country should use its resources without wasting them. Conservation, he said, was a duty to the future.

Roosevelt's successor, William Howard Taft, continued the trust cases but split the Republican Party. In 1912 Roosevelt ran against Taft on the new Progressive Party, the Bull Moose Party, and the split let Democrat Woodrow Wilson win the presidency. Wilson's New Freedom aimed at the same trusts through laws, not lawsuits. The **Seventeenth Amendment**, ratified in 1913, gave voters the direct election of senators; the income tax came under the 16th Amendment, and the **Federal Reserve** Act, which created the central banking system, passed the same year.

Wilson also signed the Clayton Antitrust Act of 1914, which strengthened the Sherman Act and protected labor unions from antitrust suits. A 1916 law against child labor, the Keating-Owen Act, was struck down by the Supreme Court in Hammer v. Dagenhart in 1918, a reminder that the courts could still block reform. The Progressives had changed the government's reach, but they left much undone, above all for Black Americans living under Jim Crow.

## The road to war

When war broke out in Europe in 1914, President Wilson declared the United States neutral. Most Americans agreed: the war was Europe's quarrel, and the country had immigrants from both sides. But American trade tied the country to the Allies. Britain controlled the seas, so American goods reached Britain and France, while Germany's market was closed off. Neutrality on paper leaned toward the Allies in practice.

German submarines broke the balance. On May 7, 1915, a German U-boat sank the British liner Lusitania, killing nearly 1,200 people, including 128 Americans. Germany promised to warn merchant ships before attacking, then broke the promise when it torpedoed the Sussex in 1916. Wilson won reelection that year with the slogan "He kept us out of war," but German leaders had already decided to resume unrestricted submarine warfare.

On January 31, 1917, Germany announced it would resume unrestricted submarine warfare, and Wilson broke off relations with Germany on February 3. In March the **Zimmermann telegram** became public. In it, Germany offered Mexico help to reconquer Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona if Mexico attacked the United States. British intelligence had intercepted the telegram and passed it to Washington. On April 2, 1917, Wilson asked Congress to declare war. Congress agreed on April 6.

## Fighting the war

The United States raised its army by draft. The Selective Service Act of May 1917 required men to register, and about 2.8 million were drafted. By the fall of 1918, about two million American troops had reached France. They helped stop German offensives at Belleau Wood and Chateau-Thierry, then took part in the Meuse-Argonne Offensive, where one million Americans fought from September to November 1918. Germany signed the armistice on November 11, 1918.

Look at the poster "True Sons of Freedom," published in 1918 to recruit Black soldiers. It shows Black American troops charging under the American flag, with Abraham Lincoln watching above them and the words "Liberty And Freedom Shall Not Perish." The poster promised Black men a place in the nation's fight. About 367,000 Black Americans served, most in labor and supply units, while facing segregation in the army itself.

![Black American soldiers charging under the American flag, with Abraham Lincoln above](u7/true-sons-of-freedom-1918.jpg)
Figure 1: Charles Gustrine, "True Sons of Freedom," 1918. Public domain (Library of Congress).

Wilson's war aims shaped the peace. His **Fourteen Points**, announced in January 1918, promised open diplomacy, freedom of the seas, self-determination for peoples, and a **League of Nations** to keep the peace. But the Treaty of Versailles, signed in June 1919, blamed Germany for the war, took its colonies, and demanded reparations. Wilson brought the treaty home, and the Senate refused it.

Senators objected above all to Article X of the League Covenant, which asked members to preserve each other's territory. Senator Henry Cabot Lodge wanted reservations stating that only Congress could send American troops abroad. Wilson refused to compromise and told loyal Democrats to vote against the treaty with reservations. The Senate rejected it in November 1919 and again in March 1920, and the United States never joined the League.

## The war at home

The war reached into factories, farms, and kitchens. The War Industries Board, led by Bernard Baruch, set production goals and allocated raw materials. Herbert Hoover's Food Administration asked families to save food with Wheatless Mondays and Meatless Tuesdays. The government sold Liberty Bonds to ordinary Americans, raising about two-thirds of the war's cost. The federal government had never managed the economy on this scale before.

The war also moved people. The **Great Migration** carried hundreds of thousands of African Americans from the rural South to factory jobs in northern cities. The newcomers found work and crowded housing, and white hostility boiled over in the Chicago riot of 1919. Women also stepped into jobs that had been closed to them, from factories to offices, though most left those jobs when the soldiers came home.

The government also used the war to silence dissent. The **Espionage Act** of 1917 made it a crime to obstruct recruiting or cause disloyalty in the armed forces, and the Sedition Act of 1918 widened the net to speech against the government. More than a thousand people were convicted.

The Socialist leader Eugene Debs was sentenced to ten years in prison for a speech at Canton, Ohio, in June 1918. In Schenck v. United States in 1919, the Supreme Court upheld the act, saying speech that created a clear and present danger could be punished.

The war also advanced women's suffrage. Women had marched for the vote in Washington in March 1913, the day before Wilson's inauguration, and their wartime work gave the cause new force. Wilson backed the amendment in 1918. Congress passed the **Nineteenth Amendment** in 1919, and the states ratified it in 1920, giving women the vote nationwide.

## Selling the war

Look at the poster "Destroy This Mad Brute," from 1917. A German soldier drawn as a gorilla in a spiked helmet marked "Militarism" carries off a woman while his club, marked "Kultur," drips blood, and ruined cities stand behind him. The poster was meant to make enlistment feel urgent and to make the enemy seem less than human. It shows how the government asked Americans to see the war: not as a distant quarrel, but as a rescue.

![A German soldier drawn as a gorilla in a spiked helmet, carrying off a woman](u7/destroy-mad-brute-1917.jpg)
Figure 2: Harry R. Hopps, "Destroy This Mad Brute," 1917. Public domain (Library of Congress).

Look at the second poster, "Food is Ammunition," also from 1917. A basket of vegetables and fruit sits below soldiers on horseback under an American flag. The message was that saving food was a form of fighting. The war asked ordinary families to change what they ate, and it asked them to lend their savings, their sons, and their silence.

![A basket of vegetables and fruit below soldiers on horseback under an American flag](u7/food-is-ammunition-1917.jpg)
Figure 3: John E. Sheridan, "Food is Ammunition," 1917. Public domain (U.S. Food Administration).

The government sold the war through the Committee on Public Information, its propaganda office under George Creel. Its speakers, the Four-Minute Men, gave short pro-war talks in movie theaters, so the message reached Americans even between films.

## Worked items

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0208 (Topic 7.4)

The stimulus is a verbatim excerpt, abridged, from Theodore Roosevelt's speech "The Man with the Muck-Rake," April 14, 1906.

> The men with the muck-rakes are often indispensable to the well-being of society; but only if they know when to stop raking the muck, and to look upward... — Theodore Roosevelt, 'The Man with the Muck-Rake,' April 14, 1906 (abridged)

Source: Verbatim excerpt (abridged) from Theodore Roosevelt, 'The Man with the Muck-Rake,' April 14, 1906, public domain.

a. Identify the group Roosevelt calls 'the men with the muck-rakes.'

Model answer: The 'men with the muck-rakes' were the muckrakers, the investigative journalists who exposed corruption and unsafe business practices in popular magazines. Roosevelt gave them the name in this 1906 speech, borrowing the image from Bunyan's Pilgrim's Progress. Writers like Upton Sinclair, Ida Tarbell, and Lincoln Steffens were the best-known examples.

b. Explain Roosevelt's attitude toward the muckrakers.

Model answer: Roosevelt thought muckrakers were often indispensable because their exposés forced the country to face corruption and unsafe products. But he warned them to know when to stop raking the muck and look upward, meaning criticism should serve reform rather than become endless attack. He valued their work while warning that constant scandal-mongering could do more harm than good.

c. Explain one way muckraking influenced Progressive reform.

Model answer: Muckraking built public pressure that moved lawmakers to act. Upton Sinclair's The Jungle disgusted readers with its account of Chicago's packing plants, and the outcry helped Congress pass the Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act in 1906. Without the exposés, the public would have had little reason to demand the laws.

How to think about it: part a asks who Roosevelt means, so name the group and give examples. Part b asks for his attitude, so hold both halves of the speech together: useful, but only if they know when to stop. Part c asks for one influence on reform, so trace one exposé to one law.

### Worked item 2 — apush-saq-0211 (Topic 7.5)

The stimulus is verbatim excerpts from President Woodrow Wilson's War Message to Congress, April 2, 1917.

> President Woodrow Wilson, War Message to Congress, April 2, 1917 (verbatim excerpts):
>
> "The present German submarine warfare against commerce is a warfare against mankind. It is a war against all nations."
>
> . . .
>
> "With a profound sense of the solemn and even tragical character of the step I am taking and of the grave responsibilities which it involves, but in unhesitating obedience to what I deem my constitutional duty, I advise that the Congress declare the recent course of the Imperial German Government to be in fact nothing less than war against the Government and people of the United States..."

Source: Verbatim excerpts from Woodrow Wilson, War Message to Congress, April 2, 1917 (public domain).

a. Identify one reason Wilson gives for asking Congress to declare war on Germany.

Model answer: Wilson says German submarine warfare is a warfare against mankind and a war against all nations. He presents the submarine campaign as an attack not only on the United States but on the rights of all neutral nations. That claim turns a German campaign at sea into a cause for war.

b. Explain how German submarine warfare changed the relationship between the United States and Germany.

Model answer: Submarine warfare ended the pledges of restraint Germany had made after the Lusitania, Arabic, and Sussex sinkings. When Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare in January 1917, Wilson broke off diplomatic relations and then asked Congress for war. The campaign moved the two countries from tense neutrality to open war in a few months.

c. Explain one additional factor, besides submarine warfare, that pushed the United States toward war in 1917.

Model answer: One additional factor was the Zimmermann telegram, in which Germany offered Mexico help to reconquer Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona if Mexico attacked the United States. British intelligence intercepted the telegram and gave it to Washington, and American newspapers printed it in early 1917. The offer made many Americans see Germany as a direct threat to American territory.

How to think about it: part a asks for one reason Wilson gives, so take it from his own words about submarine warfare. Part b asks how that warfare changed the relationship, so trace the broken pledges to the break in relations. Part c asks for one additional factor, so name the Zimmermann telegram.

### Worked item 3 — apush-saq-0213 (Topic 7.6)

The stimulus is a verbatim excerpt from Section 3 of the Espionage Act, June 15, 1917.

> Espionage Act, June 15, 1917, Section 3 (verbatim excerpt):
>
> "Whoever, when the United States is at war, shall willfully cause or attempt to cause insubordination, disloyalty, mutiny, or refusal of duty, in the military or naval forces of the United States, or shall willfully obstruct the recruiting or enlistment service of the United States, to the injury of the service of the United States, shall be punished by a fine of not more than $10,000 or imprisonment for not more than twenty years, or both."

Source: Verbatim excerpt from the Espionage Act, June 15, 1917, Section 3 (public domain).

a. Identify one action the Espionage Act made a crime.

Model answer: One crime was willfully obstructing the recruiting or enlistment service of the United States. The act also made it a crime to cause insubordination, disloyalty, mutiny, or refusal of duty among soldiers and sailors.

b. Explain how the government used the Espionage Act against critics of the war.

Model answer: The government charged thousands of Americans under the act, and more than 1,000 were convicted. The Socialist leader Eugene Debs was sentenced to ten years in prison for his Canton speech, and the government used the law to break up radical groups such as the Industrial Workers of the World.

c. Explain one way the Supreme Court responded to a challenge to the Espionage Act.

Model answer: In Schenck v. United States in 1919, the Court upheld the Espionage Act and affirmed Schenck's conviction. Justice Holmes wrote that speech creating a clear and present danger could be punished, so wartime limits on antiwar speech stood.

How to think about it: part a asks for one crime the act names, so quote the section. Part b asks how the government used it, so name who was charged and what happened to Debs. Part c asks how the Court responded, so name Schenck and the clear and present danger test.

## Key terms

- **Progressivism** — the early-1900s reform movement that used government power to curb big business, clean up cities, and expand democracy.

- **Muckrakers** — investigative journalists whose exposés of corruption and unsafe products built public pressure for reform.

- **Square Deal** — Theodore Roosevelt's promise of a fair deal for workers, consumers, and business, with the government as referee.

- **Trust-busting** — the use of the Sherman Antitrust Act to break up or restrain monopolies.

- **Seventeenth Amendment** — the 1913 amendment that gave voters the direct election of senators.

- **Federal Reserve** — the central banking system created in 1913 to stabilize credit and regulate the money supply.

- **Nineteenth Amendment** — the amendment, ratified in 1920, that prohibited denying the vote on the basis of sex.

- **Zimmermann telegram** — Germany's 1917 offer of a Mexican alliance against the United States, which helped push Wilson to ask for war.

- **Fourteen Points** — Wilson's 1918 plan for peace, including open diplomacy, self-determination, and a League of Nations.

- **League of Nations** — the international organization at the heart of the Treaty of Versailles, which the Senate refused to join.

- **Espionage Act** — the 1917 law that made it a crime to obstruct recruiting or cause disloyalty in the armed forces.

- **Great Migration** — the movement of hundreds of thousands of African Americans from the rural South to northern cities during and after World War I.
