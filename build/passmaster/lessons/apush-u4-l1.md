How does a small coastal republic become a continental nation in fifty years? In 1800 the United States held about five million people, most of them farmers who rarely traveled far from home. By 1848 it held more than seventeen million, its farms and factories tied into one market and its politics run by parties. This lesson follows the first steps: the parties that formed, the president who promised a small government, and the purchase, the court, and the war that made his promises hard to keep.

## A country remade in fifty years

The first thing to grasp about this period is its scale of change. The population more than tripled, from about 5.3 million in 1800 to about 17.1 million in 1840. Land grew with it: the **Louisiana Purchase** of 1803 doubled the nation's territory, and by the 1840s settlers were pushing toward the Pacific. Growth was the period's defining feature, and it forced every old argument — about government, about slavery, about who belonged — into new territory.

The economy changed as fast as the map. An older way of life, in which farm families grew most of what they used, gave way to a market economy: canals, steamboats, and railroads tied distant regions into one commercial web, and factory towns like Lowell drew young women off farms into wage work for cash. The North began to industrialize, while slavery grew stronger in the South on the strength of cotton. The regions grew more linked and more divided at the same time.

Politics changed too. Property requirements for voting fell away, and mass parties carried ordinary white men into public life. But two things did not change across the whole period. Slavery did not fade as the nation grew; it grew stronger. And Native peoples were forced off their lands as settlers moved west, before and after the market revolution. Keep that double movement in mind — change and continuity together — because the exam asks you to trace both.

## Two parties, one peaceful transfer

By 1800 two parties had formed, though the founders had never planned for them. The **Federalists**, following Alexander Hamilton, wanted a strong national government that would promote commerce and manufacturing through a national bank and tariffs. The **Democratic-Republicans**, following Thomas Jefferson, wanted a limited national government, with most power kept in the states, and a republic of independent farmers. The parties disagreed about what the republic was for, and the election of 1800 would settle which vision governed.

The election was bitter. Federalists warned that Jefferson would bring a reign of terror like the French Revolution; Democratic-Republicans painted President John Adams as a would-be king. Jefferson seemed to win, but a mistake in the Democratic-Republican electoral voting left him tied with his running mate, Aaron Burr.

Under the rules of the time, each elector cast two ballots with no separate vote for vice president, so the choice went to the Federalist-led House of Representatives. Hamilton urged Federalists to back Jefferson, and on the 36th ballot Jefferson won. The Twelfth Amendment, ratified in 1804, fixed the flaw by separating the votes for president and vice president.

Jefferson called the transfer the **Revolution of 1800**, claiming it returned the nation to its founding principles. It was the first peaceful transfer of power from one party to another in American history, and no violence accompanied it.

In his inaugural address on March 4, 1801, Jefferson tried to calm the losers: "We are all Republicans, we are all Federalists." He promised equal and exact justice to all, and peace, commerce, and honest friendship with all nations, entangling alliances with none. But the old arguments did not vanish: the Federalist program lived on in the Supreme Court under Chief Justice John Marshall, and later in Henry Clay's American System.

## Louisiana: the purchase that doubled the nation

Jefferson believed in **strict construction**: the government could do only what the Constitution plainly allowed — buying foreign land was nowhere in it. Yet in 1803 he bought the **Louisiana Purchase** anyway — a vast territory west of the Mississippi, from France for $15 million. The purchase doubled the nation's land — more land meant more Americans could become independent farmers — and gave the United States New Orleans, the port at the mouth of the Mississippi.

Napoleon sold because war made Louisiana a burden: his failure to crush the slave revolt in Haiti cost him an army, and war with Britain loomed. He needed cash more than a colony, and Jefferson got Congress to pay without waiting for an amendment. The small-government president used a power the Constitution never named — the prize was too large.

Jefferson sent Meriwether Lewis and William Clark to explore the new land. Their **Lewis and Clark expedition** of 1804 to 1806 mapped the territory and sought a practical route through the mountains — but it depended on Native peoples at every step: guides who knew the way, interpreters like Sacagawea, a Shoshone woman traveling with them, and diplomacy with the nations they crossed. Their reports of fertile land and a Pacific route encouraged Americans west.

## The Court speaks: Marbury and federal power

Jefferson promised a smaller presidency, but his actions stretched it — Louisiana, the embargo — and the courts pushed further. Chief Justice John Marshall led the Supreme Court from 1801 to 1835, claiming powers no one had spelled out.

The first came in **Marbury v. Madison** in 1803. President Adams had named Federalist judges in his final days, and William Marbury sued when Jefferson's secretary of state, James Madison, refused to deliver his commission.

Marshall ruled that Marbury did have a right to the commission — but that the Court could not order Madison to deliver it, because Section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789, the law Marbury relied on, was itself unconstitutional. In striking it down, Marshall claimed **judicial review**, the power of the Court to strike down laws that conflict with the Constitution.

The Marshall Court then used that power against the states. In **McCulloch v. Maryland** (1819) it barred Maryland from taxing the Second Bank of the United States. In Gibbons v. Ogden (1824) it struck down a New York steamboat monopoly, leaving interstate trade to Congress.

In Cohens v. Virginia (1821) it claimed review of state court decisions, and in Worcester v. Georgia (1832) it ruled Georgia's laws had no force in Cherokee territory. South Carolina answered with the Exposition and Protest (1828): a state could nullify a federal law — the doctrine of nullification. The party that lost the election kept its program alive through the courts.

That is why the election of 1800 changed less than Jefferson promised: Federalist ideas — a national bank, federal power over the states, a judiciary with the last word — carried on in Marshall's courtroom, and Henry Clay's American System — a bank, a tariff, internal improvements — later restated Hamilton's program.

## War, embargo, and the end of the Federalists

Jefferson's second great test came from abroad. Britain and France were at war, and both seized American ships trading with the other side. Britain went further, stopping American ships and pressing sailors into its navy — some 6,000 between 1803 and 1812. In 1807 the British warship Leopard fired on the American frigate Chesapeake, killing three Americans and seizing four more. Jefferson answered with "peaceful coercion": the **Embargo Act** of 1807 banned American ships from sailing to foreign ports.

The embargo hurt Americans more than it hurt Britain or France. American exports fell from about $108 million in 1807 to about $22 million in 1808, and New England merchants hated the law. Look at the cartoon below. A snapping turtle — "Ograbme," embargo spelled backward — bites an American merchant carrying goods for export. The joke was on Jefferson: his policy bit his own countrymen. But the cutoff had an unintended effect: shut off from British goods, Americans began making those goods at home.

![Ograbme, or The American Snapping-turtle, 1807-1808](u4/original-misc-worldstage-02.jpg)
Figure 3: Ograbme, or The American Snapping-turtle, 1807-1808. The embargo bites an American merchant, not a British ship. Public domain.

Congress replaced the embargo with the Non-Intercourse Act of 1809, reopening trade except with Britain and France, but the seizures went on. In 1812 President Madison asked Congress for war. The war ended in stalemate at the Treaty of Ghent in 1814, and Jackson's victory at New Orleans in early 1815 — fought after the treaty was signed — made him a hero.

The Federalists, who had opposed the war and met at the **Hartford Convention** in December 1814, demanding limits on federal power, looked disloyal when peace arrived. The party faded away, and the years that followed — the **Era of Good Feelings** — left the Democratic-Republicans alone.

## A nation defines itself

The war's end opened a new question: what was America, now that it stood alone? The Erie Canal, finished in 1825, gave one answer. It joined the Hudson River to the Great Lakes and gave New York City the only water route from the Great Lakes to the Atlantic. Western goods that once floated down the Mississippi to New Orleans could now move east to New York City, which became the nation's leading port — the market revolution in a single ditch.

In 1823 President Monroe warned Europe to found no new colonies in the Americas and to stay out of the hemisphere. The warning came as Latin American republics won independence from Spain, and the United States feared Europe might help Spain win them back. The Monroe Doctrine claimed the Americas as a sphere closed to new European colonization.

A French visitor watched the new democracy and wrote it down. Alexis de Tocqueville's *Democracy in America* (1835) found Americans valued equality of conditions above all — a passionate love of equality. But he also saw the tension: a majority that loved equality could still deny it to enslaved people, Native peoples, and women.

American artists began to paint their own landscape as a national symbol. Thomas Cole's *The Oxbow* (1836), a Hudson River School landmark, shows wild, stormy forest on one side and sunny, settled farmland on the other — Americans saw wilderness becoming farmland, and felt both pride and loss.

## Key terms

- **Revolution of 1800** — Jefferson's name for the peaceful transfer of power from the Federalists to the Democratic-Republicans in the election of 1800.

- **Federalists** — The party of Alexander Hamilton, favoring a strong national government that promoted commerce and manufacturing.

- **Democratic-Republicans** — The party of Thomas Jefferson, favoring a limited national government and a republic of independent farmers.

- **Strict construction** — The view that the government may do only what the Constitution plainly allows.

- **Louisiana Purchase** — The 1803 purchase of the Louisiana Territory from France for $15 million, doubling the nation's land.

- **Lewis and Clark expedition** — The 1804–1806 exploration of the Louisiana Territory led by Meriwether Lewis and William Clark.

- **Judicial review** — The Supreme Court's power to strike down laws that conflict with the Constitution.

- **Marbury v. Madison** — The 1803 Supreme Court decision establishing judicial review.

- **McCulloch v. Maryland** — The 1819 decision barring Maryland from taxing the Second Bank of the United States.

- **Embargo Act** — Jefferson's 1807 ban on American ships sailing to foreign ports, meant to pressure Britain and France.

- **Hartford Convention** — The December 1814 meeting of New England Federalists demanding limits on federal war and trade powers.

- **Era of Good Feelings** — The years about 1815 to 1825, when the Democratic-Republicans stood alone in national politics.

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0101

Between 1800 and 1848, the United States changed more in fifty years than it had in the century before. A market economy replaced much of the old semi-subsistence farming: canals, steamboats, and railroads tied distant regions into one commercial web, and factory towns like Lowell drew young women off farms into wage work.

The nation's land grew as well, doubling with the Louisiana Purchase in 1803 and stretching toward the Pacific by the 1840s. Politics changed too, as property requirements for voting fell away and mass parties carried ordinary white men into public life. Yet some things did not change. Slavery grew stronger in the South on the strength of cotton, and Native peoples were forced off their lands as settlers moved west.

a) Identify one economic change of the Market Revolution described in the passage.

Model answer: Canals, steamboats, and railroads tied distant regions into one commercial web, replacing much of the old semi-subsistence farming. Factory towns like Lowell also drew young women off farms into wage work for cash.

b) Explain one way territorial expansion changed the United States in the period 1800-1848.

Model answer: The Louisiana Purchase of 1803 doubled the nation's land, and by the 1840s the country stretched toward the Pacific. This growth gave settlers new farmland and drew the nation into conflicts over Native lands and over whether slavery would spread west.

c) Explain one continuity in American life across the period 1800-1848.

Model answer: Slavery did not fade as the nation grew; it grew stronger in the South on the strength of cotton. Native peoples were also forced off their lands throughout the period as settlers moved west, before and after the Market Revolution.

The reasoning to practice: part (a) names a change from the passage, part (b) gives one effect of expansion, and part (c) names something that stayed the same across the whole period — change and continuity are different answers.

a) Identify one difference between the Federalist and Democratic-Republican visions of government.

Model answer: The election passed power peacefully from one party to another, the first such transfer in American history.
