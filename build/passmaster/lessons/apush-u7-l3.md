The 1920s looked like triumph: assembly lines, radios, cars, a consumer economy built on credit. This lesson answers a question the decade itself could not settle: who shared in the prosperity, and what happened when the credit ran out? The answer runs from the factory floor to the farm, and from the radio studio to the bank door.

## A new economy

The 1920s opened with a machine that changed how Americans lived. Henry Ford's moving **assembly line**, introduced at his Highland Park plant in 1913, carried each Model T chassis past workers who each added one part. The line cut the time to build a chassis from about twelve and a half hours to about one and a half hours. Ford's methods spread to every industry that made goods in quantity.

Cheaper goods meant more buyers. The price of a Model T touring car fell from 850 dollars in 1908 to 290 dollars by 1925, and by the end of the decade about 23 million cars were on American roads. Radios, refrigerators, and washing machines reached ordinary homes. Many of these goods were bought on the **installment plan**, paying a little each month, and by 1928 about 65 percent of cars were bought that way.

Look at the photograph: Model T chassis ride a conveyor line at Ford's Highland Park plant in 1913, each one waiting for its next part. Nothing in the picture is finished, and that is the point. The line never stopped, and the worker never chased the work. The factory had become a machine for making machines, and the price of a car had become a price a working family could pay.

![Model T chassis on a conveyor line at Ford's Highland Park plant, 1913](u7/ford-assembly-line-1913.jpg)
Figure 1: Model T chassis on the moving line at Ford's Highland Park plant, 1913. Public domain (Library of Congress).

## Radio, movies, and the airwaves

The car changed where Americans lived, and the radio changed what they heard. In July 1920, Frank Conrad broadcast music and talk from a station in the garage of his home in Wilkinsburg, Pennsylvania. That November, station KDKA in Pittsburgh broadcast election returns, the first licensed commercial broadcast in the country. By the end of the decade, radios stood in millions of homes, and families gathered to hear the same news, music, and sports at the same hour.

Look at the photograph: Conrad's station 8XK fills a garage with wires, dials, and a large horn, the work of one man in his spare time. Within a few years, radio had become a national business, with networks carrying programs across the country. The movies grew the same way, from storefront nickelodeons to Hollywood studios. In May 1927, Charles Lindbergh flew alone across the Atlantic, and radio carried the news of his landing to a country that waited together.

![Frank Conrad's radio station 8XK in his garage, July 1920](u7/frank-conrad-8xk-1920.jpg)
Figure 2: Frank Conrad's radio station 8XK, Wilkinsburg, Pennsylvania, July 1920. Public domain.

The new media also changed business. Advertising taught Americans to want the newest car, radio, or appliance, and companies used psychology to sell goods by appealing to status and desire. President Calvin Coolidge praised wealth as the product of industry and ambition, and businessmen were treated as heroes. The decade trusted private enterprise more than government, and the government returned the trust by cutting taxes and leaving business mostly alone.

## New freedoms and old fears

The new goods and media unsettled older certainties. Young women called **flappers** bobbed their hair, wore short dresses, smoked and drank in public, and dated without chaperones. Their style announced a break with Victorian rules about how women should look and behave. To many older Americans, the flapper was proof that the country was losing its morals. To the women themselves, she was proof that they could choose.

The **Harlem Renaissance** gave Black writers, musicians, and artists a new public voice. Langston Hughes and Zora Neale Hurston wrote about Black life, and Louis Armstrong and Duke Ellington carried jazz to a mainstream audience. Black culture reshaped American art even while segregation held firm and lynching continued in the South. The Renaissance showed that Black Americans would define their own culture, not wait for white approval.

**Fundamentalism** was the movement among Protestant Christians to defend the Bible's literal truth against modern science and liberal theology. Its followers saw the public schools as places where their children should learn the Bible's account of creation, not evolution. In 1925 Tennessee passed the Butler Act, which made it a crime for a teacher in a public school to teach that humans descended from lower animals. The American Civil Liberties Union offered to defend any teacher who broke the law.

The **Scopes trial** followed. John Scopes, a teacher in Dayton, Tennessee, accepted the offer, and his trial in July 1925 drew reporters from across the country. William Jennings Bryan argued for the prosecution, and Clarence Darrow for the defense. Scopes was convicted and fined, but the trial put the law itself on trial before the country. The argument over evolution and the Bible did not end in Dayton, and it has not ended since.

## The Klan, immigration, and the Red Scare

Fear of outsiders ran through the decade. The **Red Scare** of 1919 and 1920 followed the Russian Revolution and a wave of strikes and bombings at home. Attorney General A. Mitchell Palmer ordered raids that arrested thousands of suspected radicals and deported hundreds. The raids violated constitutional rights, and the panic faded, but the fear of foreign ideas stayed.

Congress turned the fear into law. The Emergency Quota Act of 1921 limited immigration from each country to 3 percent of that nationality's population in the United States in 1910. The **National Origins Act** of 1924 went further: it set each quota at 2 percent of the 1890 population, capped total immigration at 150,000 a year, and barred immigration from Asia. The laws aimed above all at southern and eastern Europe, and they paused mass immigration for forty years.

The reborn Ku Klux Klan gave the fear a mass movement. The Klan of the 1920s was not only Southern: it recruited across the country, claimed millions of members by 1925, and briefly controlled the politics of Indiana. It opposed Black Americans, immigrants, and Catholics, and it called its program "100 percent Americanism." Look at the photograph: robed Klansmen march in formation in Washington in August 1925, with the Capitol dome in the distance.

![Robed Ku Klux Klan members marching in formation in Washington, August 1925](u7/kkk-parade-washington-1925.jpg)
Figure 3: Ku Klux Klan parade, Washington, D.C., August 8, 1925. Public domain (Library of Congress).

**Prohibition** was the other great experiment in using law to fix morals. The 18th Amendment, ratified in 1919, banned the making and selling of alcohol. Speakeasies sold illegal liquor, and organized crime grew rich supplying it. Prohibition showed the limits of a law that millions of Americans refused to obey, and it was repealed by the 21st Amendment in 1933.

## From boom to bust

The prosperity had weak roots. Farmers had expanded production during the war and never cut back, so crop prices stayed low through the 1920s and rural banks failed. Factories turned out more goods than buyers could afford, because most families earned too little to keep buying. Wealth piled up at the top, and investors bought stocks with borrowed money, a practice called **margin buying**, putting as little as 10 percent down.

The stock market crash of October 1929 turned a weak economy into a collapse. Prices fell on October 24 and again on October 29, and panic selling wiped out fortunes built on borrowed money. The crash froze credit: lenders called in loans, rumors set off runs on banks, and banks failed. The **Great Depression** had begun, and it would last a decade.

The numbers tell the story. Unemployment stood at 3.2 percent in 1929 and climbed to 24.9 percent in 1933, when one worker in four had no job. Bank failures rose from 659 in 1929 to 4,000 in 1933, and depositors lost their savings. In the Great Plains, drought and years of over-farming stripped the soil, and dust storms ruined the farms. The **Dust Bowl** drove families west to California, where many became farm laborers for low wages.

President Herbert Hoover insisted at first that the economy was sound, and he refused direct federal relief to individuals, saying it would weaken self-reliance. In 1932 he backed the Reconstruction Finance Corporation, which lent federal money to banks and railroads. He also signed the Smoot-Hawley Tariff of 1930, which raised tariffs to record levels and deepened the worldwide collapse as other countries answered with tariffs of their own. By 1932, Hoovervilles, shantytowns named for the president, stood outside American cities.

## Key terms

- **Assembly line** — the moving line that carried each product past workers who each added one part, cutting the time and cost of building it.

- **Installment plan** — buying goods by paying a little each month, which let families take goods home before they could pay in full.

- **Flappers** — young women of the 1920s who claimed new personal freedoms in dress, behavior, and dating.

- **Harlem Renaissance** — the 1920s flowering of Black literature, music, and art centered in Harlem.

- **Fundamentalism** — the movement among Protestant Christians to defend the Bible's literal truth against modern science.

- **Scopes trial** — the 1925 Tennessee trial of John Scopes for teaching evolution, which put the Butler Act on trial before the country.

- **Red Scare** — the 1919-1920 panic over communism and anarchism, marked by the Palmer Raids and deportations.

- **National Origins Act** — the 1924 law, also called the Immigration Act, that set each national quota at 2 percent of the 1890 population and barred immigration from Asia.

- **Prohibition** — the ban on making and selling alcohol under the 18th Amendment, repealed in 1933.

- **Margin buying** — buying stocks with borrowed money, putting as little as 10 percent down.

- **Great Depression** — the 1929-1939 economic collapse of mass unemployment, bank failures, and farm ruin after the stock market crash.

- **Dust Bowl** — the drought and dust storms of the 1930s that ruined Great Plains farms and drove families west.
