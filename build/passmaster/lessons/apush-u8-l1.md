In 1945 the United States stood alone at the top of the world: its cities unbombed, its factories running, its army spread across two oceans. Yet within two years its leaders were warning of a new danger. This lesson answers how that danger took shape, how America answered it abroad with containment, and how the fear of communism changed life at home.

## A world split in two

The war ended with the United States stronger than any nation in history. Its factories had supplied the Allies, its cities had never been bombed, and it alone held the atomic bomb. The photograph shows what that bomb could do: a mushroom cloud rising over Nagasaki after the second atomic bombing, on August 9, 1945. One bomb had destroyed a city, and American leaders knew the weapon would shape every quarrel to come.

![Mushroom cloud over Nagasaki, August 9, 1945](u8/saq-set-26-q3.jpg)
Figure 1: The mushroom cloud over Nagasaki, August 9, 1945. Public domain (U.S. government photograph).

The winners tried to build a better peace than the one that followed the First World War. In 1945 they founded the United Nations, a place where nations could settle disputes together instead of acting alone. But the wartime alliance was already breaking. At Yalta, Stalin had promised free elections in Poland and across Eastern Europe. The elections never came. Soviet armies stayed, and Communist governments took power country by country.

In March 1946, Winston Churchill warned that an **iron curtain** had fallen across Europe, dividing a free West from a Communist-controlled East. The phrase caught on because it matched what Americans could see: Eastern Europe ruled from Moscow, Western Europe free but weak. Historians argue about why the Cold War began. Some blame Soviet expansion. Others blame American power and markets. A third view holds that two great powers with opposite systems were bound to clash. This lesson does not settle that argument. It shows what each side did next.

## Containment takes shape

**Containment** was the answer American leaders chose: hold firm against Communist expansion wherever it appeared, and wait for the Soviet system to change. The diplomat George Kennan supplied the idea in a long telegram from Moscow in February 1946; in an article signed "X" in 1947 he argued that Soviet leaders were driven by ideology and old Russian insecurity to press outward wherever they met no firm resistance. Patient pressure, he said, would work better than a quick war.

Look at the first page of the **Truman Doctrine** address. In March 1947, Truman told Congress that Greece needed aid against a Communist-led rebellion; Turkey needed support against Soviet pressure. Britain could no longer afford them. Truman asked for $400 million, and Congress agreed. That promise broke with the 1920s, when America had stayed out of Europe's quarrels and avoided lasting commitments abroad.

![Truman Doctrine address, first page, March 12, 1947](u8/barrons-2027-pt1-16.jpg)
Figure 2: Truman's address to Congress, March 12, 1947. Public domain (U.S. government document).

The **Marshall Plan** carried containment into economics. From 1948 to 1951, the United States sent about $13 billion to Western Europe: Britain took $3.3 billion, France $2.7 billion, Italy $1.5 billion, West Germany $1.4 billion, and the Netherlands $1.1 billion. Eastern Europe was offered aid too, but Moscow barred its satellites from taking it. Western Europe recovered, blunting Communist appeals.

In June 1948, the Soviet Union blocked West Berlin's roads after the West issued a new currency. Instead of an armed convoy, the Allies flew supplies in for fifteen months, in the **Berlin Airlift**, and the blockade failed. In April 1949, the United States joined Canada and ten European nations in **NATO**, its first peacetime alliance: an attack on one was an attack on all. Moscow answered in 1955 with the **Warsaw Pact**.

## Korea and the nuclear age

In June 1950, North Korea invaded the South across the 38th parallel, the line that had divided the peninsula since 1945. President Truman sent American troops under the flag of the United Nations, while the Soviet Union was boycotting the Security Council and could not veto the move. General Douglas MacArthur landed at Inchon, drove north, and reached the Chinese border. Then China entered the war in late 1950 and drove United Nations forces back south.

The war settled into a stalemate near the old border and ended in July 1953 with an armistice, not a peace treaty. Korea stayed divided. MacArthur wanted to widen the war into China, even with atomic weapons, and he said so in public. President Truman removed him from command in April 1951, insisting that civilian leaders, not generals, decide how far a war goes. MacArthur came home to a hero's welcome, and Truman's standing fell.

Under Eisenhower, the United States leaned harder on nuclear weapons. Secretary of State John Foster Dulles promised **massive retaliation**: any Communist aggression could meet an American nuclear reply, at places and with means of American choosing. The threat was meant to deter war cheaply, without large armies. But by the end of the decade, both sides held arsenals large enough to destroy each other many times over. The standoff had a name: mutually assured destruction.

The rivalry also moved into space. In October 1957, the Soviet Union launched Sputnik, the first artificial satellite, and Americans realized Soviet rockets could reach their cities. The United States created NASA in 1958 and poured money into science in schools. In May 1960, the Soviets shot down an American U-2 spy plane, and Eisenhower admitted the spying. Each side now watched the other from above, and neither trusted the other below.

## The Red Scare at home

Fear of communism changed life inside the United States. In 1947, President Truman set up a **loyalty program** that investigated millions of federal workers and dismissed those judged security risks. Some dismissed were gay employees, called risks because the government said their private lives invited blackmail. Truman acted to answer charges of softness on communism.

Congress hunted for Communists too. **HUAC**, the House Un-American Activities Committee, called writers and directors to testify about their politics. The Hollywood Ten refused to answer and went to jail. Studios refused to hire anyone accused of Communist ties, and the **blacklist** ruined careers.

Look at the poster. The word SABOTAGE stands over a collapsed bridge and a burning building, warning Americans of enemy agents striking factories. Whittaker Chambers produced documents he said came from Alger Hiss, and Hiss went to prison for perjury. Julius and Ethel Rosenberg were convicted of passing atomic secrets to the Soviet Union on March 29, 1951, and executed on June 19, 1953.

![Civil defense poster headed SABOTAGE, 1952](u8/pr25e-ch12-q02.jpg)
Figure 3: The SABOTAGE civil defense poster, 1952. Public domain (U.S. government poster).

Senator Joseph McCarthy gave the fear its name. At Wheeling, West Virginia, in February 1950, he claimed that 205 Communists worked in the State Department. The number kept changing. **McCarthyism** came to mean accusing people of communism without proof. In the televised Army-McCarthy hearings of 1954, the Army's lawyer, Joseph Welch, asked McCarthy whether he had any sense of decency left. The Senate censured McCarthy that December, and his power broke.

The scare also reached the law. Truman vetoed the McCarran Internal Security Act in 1950, warning that it threatened basic freedoms, but Congress passed it over his veto. The Smith Act was used to prosecute Communist leaders. In 1957, Yates narrowed the Smith Act cases.

## Containment changes shape

The Cold War did not stay frozen. In 1961, the Soviet Union built the Berlin Wall, a symbol of Communist rule, to stop East Germans from fleeing. In October 1962, American spy planes found Soviet missiles in Cuba. Kennedy ordered a naval quarantine and demanded their removal. For thirteen days the world stood near war, until Khrushchev withdrew the missiles and the United States agreed to remove its missiles from Turkey. The crisis ended in compromise, and the two sides set up a hotline and banned atmospheric nuclear tests.

Under Nixon, containment took a new form called detente. Nixon visited China in February 1972, opening relations with a Communist power the United States had refused to recognize for over twenty years. He played China and the Soviet Union against each other, and in the same year he signed the SALT I agreements, which limited the growth of nuclear arsenals without disarming either side. Detente treated the Soviet Union as a rival to manage, not an enemy to defeat.

President Carter shifted the emphasis again, judging governments by how they treated their people. His human rights policy pressed even friendly governments to ease repression. In 1978 he brokered the Camp David Accords, a peace agreement between Egypt and Israel. But in December 1979 the Soviet Union invaded Afghanistan, and detente ended. Carter answered by boycotting the Moscow Olympics and raising military spending. The Cold War that had begun with Truman's address was still running, in a harder form, thirty-three years later.

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0236 (Topic 8.1)

The stimulus is a paraphrase of President Truman's address to Congress, March 12, 1947.

> Paraphrased from President Harry S. Truman's address to Congress, March 12, 1947: The president told Congress that the world faced a grave moment and that American foreign policy and national security were at stake. Greece had asked urgently for financial and economic help, and American reports agreed that help was imperative if Greece was to survive as a free nation. Truman said the United States must support free peoples who were resisting subjugation, and he asked Congress to approve aid to Greece and Turkey.

Source: Paraphrased from President Harry S. Truman's address to Congress, March 12, 1947 (House Document No. 171), public domain (U.S. government document).

a. Identify the policy Truman announced in this address.

Model answer: Truman announced that the United States would support free peoples resisting subjugation, a policy that became known as containment. He asked Congress to approve aid to Greece and Turkey as the first step in carrying it out.

b. Explain why Truman said Greece needed American help in 1947.

Model answer: Greece had suffered invasion, occupation, and internal strife, and it asked urgently for financial and economic help to survive as a free nation. Britain, which had propped up Greece before, could no longer afford to do so, leaving the United States as the only power that could help.

c. Explain one way Truman's address marked a change from the foreign policy the United States followed in the 1920s.

Model answer: In the 1920s the United States kept its distance from Europe's quarrels and stayed out of lasting commitments abroad. Truman's address accepted a permanent American role in defending other nations, turning the country into the leader of the non-communist world.

How to think about it: part a asks for the policy, so name support for free peoples resisting subjugation, or containment. Part b asks why Greece needed help, so use the suffering Truman describes and Britain stepping back. Part c asks how the address changed foreign policy, so contrast lasting responsibility abroad with the 1920s habit of staying out.

### Worked item 2 — apush-saq-0237 (Topic 8.2)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage on containment from 1947 to 1980.

> After World War II, the United States and the Soviet Union emerged as the world's two superpowers, but their wartime alliance broke apart over the future of Europe. The United States answered Soviet expansion with containment: the Truman Doctrine promised aid to free peoples, the Marshall Plan rebuilt Western Europe, and NATO bound America to Europe's defense.

> Containment was tested in Berlin, where an airlift defeated a Soviet blockade, and in Korea, where American forces fought to restore the border between North and South. Over the next three decades, containment guided American policy through crises in Cuba and the Middle East, and it ended only when the Soviet Union itself collapsed.

Source: Original secondary passage written for this question; facts from the grounding books.

a. Identify one way the United States tried to contain communism after World War II.

Model answer: The United States offered military aid to Greece and Turkey under the Truman Doctrine, promising support to free peoples resisting Communist pressure. It also rebuilt Western Europe through the Marshall Plan, so that prosperity would keep war-weary voters from turning to communism.

b. Explain how the Berlin blockade or the Korean War tested containment.

Model answer: In Berlin, the Soviet Union closed the roads into West Berlin to force the West out, and the airlift answered by flying in supplies for nearly a year. In Korea, North Korea invaded the South, and American forces fought to restore the border at the 38th parallel. Both crises asked whether the United States would hold the line it had drawn.

c. Explain one way containment changed between 1947 and 1980.

Model answer: Early containment relied on aid and alliances in Europe, but later presidents changed its tools. Nixon pursued detente, negotiating arms limits and opening relations with China, while Carter answered the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan with sanctions and a military buildup. The goal of blocking expansion stayed, but the methods shifted from confrontation to negotiation and back.

How to think about it: part a asks for one way America contained communism, so name the Truman Doctrine, the Marshall Plan, NATO, or the Korean War. Part b asks how Berlin or Korea tested containment, so show the test and the answer. Part c asks how containment changed between 1947 and 1980, so trace it from Truman through massive retaliation to detente.

### Worked item 3 — apush-saq-0240 (Topic 8.3)

The stimulus is a paraphrase of the historian Richard Hofstadter's argument about the paranoid style in American politics.

> The historian Richard Hofstadter argued that American politics has often shown a paranoid style, marked by heated exaggeration, suspiciousness, and conspiratorial fantasy. He traced this style through movements from the Anti-Masonic Party of the 1820s to the Ku Klux Klan of the 1920s, and he saw it again in the anti-communist politics of the 1950s.

Source: Paraphrase of the historian Richard Hofstadter's argument about the paranoid style in American politics. Paraphrased, not quoted.

a. Identify one feature of the paranoid style that Hofstadter names.

Model answer: Hofstadter names heated exaggeration as one feature of the paranoid style, the habit of blowing a threat far beyond its size. He also names suspiciousness and conspiratorial fantasy, the habit of seeing hidden plots behind ordinary events.

b. Explain how McCarthyism showed the paranoid style.

Model answer: McCarthyism showed heated exaggeration when McCarthy claimed that 205 communists worked in the State Department without ever showing the list. It showed conspiratorial fantasy when his followers treated every setback in the Cold War as proof that hidden communists were at work inside the government.

c. Explain one way the Red Scare of the 1950s differed from the earlier movements Hofstadter names.

Model answer: The Red Scare of the 1950s was driven by the federal government itself, through loyalty boards, HUAC hearings, and Senate investigations, while the Anti-Masonic Party and the Ku Klux Klan were movements outside the government. It also rested on real espionage cases, like the Hiss case and the Rosenberg case, which gave the fear a basis that earlier panics lacked.

How to think about it: part a asks for one feature of the paranoid style, so take heated exaggeration, suspiciousness, or conspiratorial fantasy straight from Hofstadter. Part b asks how McCarthyism showed that style, so use the changing list and the unproved charges. Part c asks how the 1950s scare differed from the earlier movements, so name what made it different: the Cold War, the government machinery, or television.

## Key terms

- **Containment** — the policy of holding firm against Communist expansion wherever it appeared, rather than rolling communism back where it already ruled.

- **Truman Doctrine** — President Truman's 1947 promise that the United States would support free peoples resisting subjugation, beginning with aid to Greece and Turkey.

- **Marshall Plan** — the American program that sent about $13 billion in aid to rebuild Western Europe between 1948 and 1951.

- **NATO** — the North Atlantic Treaty Organization, the peacetime military alliance formed in April 1949, pledging that an attack on one member was an attack on all.

- **Warsaw Pact** — the military alliance the Soviet Union formed with its Eastern European satellites in 1955 in answer to NATO.

- **Iron curtain** — Churchill's phrase for the line dividing a free Western Europe from a Communist-controlled Eastern Europe.

- **Berlin Airlift** — the Allied operation that flew supplies into West Berlin for nearly fifteen months after the Soviet Union blocked its roads in 1948.

- **Massive retaliation** — the Eisenhower-era promise of a nuclear reply to Communist aggression, at places and with means of American choosing.

- **McCarthyism** — the practice of accusing people of communism without proof, named for Senator Joseph McCarthy.

- **HUAC** — the House Un-American Activities Committee, which investigated suspected Communists in government and in Hollywood.

- **Blacklist** — the studios' refusal to hire anyone accused of Communist ties, which ruined careers in the film industry.

- **Loyalty program** — President Truman's 1947 program that investigated federal workers and dismissed those judged security risks.
