Between 1865 and 1900 the United States became the world's leading industrial power. A few men controlled the railroads, the oil, and the steel, and millions of workers dug the coal, laid the track, and tended the furnaces. This lesson answers one question: how did big business get so big, and why did the workers who built it lose nearly every fight?

## How big business was built

After the Civil War, American businesses grew so large that the old ways of running them no longer worked. A single owner could not manage a railroad spanning a continent or a steel mill feeding a nation. The answer was the corporation, and then combinations larger still.

In the oil business, John D. Rockefeller joined many rival refineries under one board of trustees, forming the Standard Oil **Trust** in 1882. When trusts were attacked in the courts, businesses found a new form: the **holding company**, which owned enough stock in other companies to control them.

Look at the cartoon: a giant octopus labeled Standard Oil wraps its tentacles around the Capitol and a state capitol and reaches toward the White House while crushing small businessmen below. Puck published it in 1904, after the period, but it draws the fear that filled the Gilded Age: one company reaching into government itself. The cartoonist's point is that size had become power, and power had become political.

![A giant octopus labeled Standard Oil reaching for government buildings](u6/5s24-ch18-mcq-07.jpg)
Figure 1: Standard Oil as a giant octopus reaching for government buildings, Puck, 1904. Public domain.

Rockefeller practiced **horizontal integration**, buying out rival refineries until he controlled the market. Carnegie practiced **vertical integration**, owning every step from the iron mine to the finished rail. Both men cut prices and raised output, and both made fortunes that had no precedent in American history. The country got cheaper steel and cheaper oil, and it got a new question: who controlled the controllers?

## What bigness delivered, and what it cost

Big business delivered real gains. Prices fell, goods spread to every town, and a national market took shape. Large corporations also needed a new kind of worker: middle managers, clerks, and salesmen who ran the offices while owners ran the companies. The economy was no longer a nation of small shops and farms.

It also cost a great deal. Factory workers labored long hours for low wages in dangerous mills, and children worked beside them. The owners' case was that growth helped everyone in time. The workers' case was that the gains went to the top while the risks fell on them. Both cases had evidence, and the exam asks you to hold both and judge with facts, not slogans.

Congress answered the fear of monopoly with the **Sherman Antitrust Act** of 1890, which outlawed combinations in restraint of trade. The law looked strong on paper but proved weak in practice. In United States v. E. C. Knight in 1895, the Supreme Court ruled that manufacturing was not interstate commerce, so the government could not break up the sugar trust. The first federal attempt to limit big business had been narrowed by the courts, and the trusts went on growing.

## The Knights of Labor

Workers answered bigness with organization. The **Knights of Labor**, founded in 1869, welcomed skilled and unskilled workers, women, and Black workers, and campaigned for broad reforms such as the eight-hour day and an end to child labor. It was an industrial union, open to nearly everyone who worked, and it grew fast in the 1880s as workers looked for a voice bigger than a single craft.

The first great test of labor in this era came in 1877, and the Knights played no part in it. Railroad companies cut wages during the depression that followed the Panic of 1873, and railroad workers in Martinsburg, West Virginia, walked out. The strike spread along the rail lines to Pittsburgh and Chicago, and President Hayes sent federal troops, many recently withdrawn from enforcing Reconstruction in the South. The troops broke the strike, and the message was plain: the federal government would side with the railroads.

The Knights had played no part in that strike, but they kept growing anyway. They welcomed the unskilled, the very workers the older craft unions ignored, and they tied wages to a wider program of reform, including equal pay for women and an end to child labor. For a few years in the mid-1880s, they looked like the future of American labor. Then one evening in Chicago ended that future.

## Haymarket and the turn against labor

On May 4, 1886, workers gathered in Chicago's Haymarket Square to rally for the eight-hour day. A bomb exploded among the police who had come to disperse the meeting, killing police and protesters alike. No one proved who threw it. Look at the engraving: a speaker stands on a wagon at the left while police move through smoke in the center and men fall in the street. Harper's Weekly printed it eleven days later, and the image fixed the night in the public mind.

![A Harper’s Weekly engraving of the Haymarket Square bombing in 1886, showing a speaker on a wagon at left, police moving through smoke in the center, and men falling in the street](u6/pr25e-ch10-q05.jpg)
Figure 2: The Haymarket Square bombing, Chicago, May 4, 1886, Harper's Weekly, May 15, 1886. Public domain.

The **Haymarket affair** broke the Knights of Labor. Eight anarchists were tried for the bombing, and four were hanged, though the evidence against most of them was thin. The public, frightened by the bomb, linked unions with violence and foreign radicalism, and employers used the fear to blacklist union members and break strikes. The Knights, open to all and tied to reform, lost members fast. A movement that had welcomed everyone could not survive being blamed for one bomb.

The government learned a different lesson. After Haymarket, governors and presidents reached for troops and **injunctions**, court orders that could stop a strike, more quickly, and courts proved willing to help. Labor had lost the public argument, and the next unions to rise would learn from that loss by asking for less and organizing more narrowly.

## Craft unions and the big defeats

The **American Federation of Labor**, founded in 1886 under Samuel Gompers, took the narrower path. It organized only skilled workers by craft and sought higher wages and shorter hours, not revolution. Where the Knights had wanted to reform the whole economy, the AFL wanted a better bargain inside it. That modesty is why the AFL survived Haymarket while the Knights collapsed.

Even the AFL could not win the big strikes. In the **Homestead Strike** of 1892, Carnegie Steel locked out its steelworkers, and the company's manager hired Pinkerton guards to break the lockout. The guards and the workers fought on the riverbank, and the state militia finished the job. The union was broken, and Carnegie Steel kept its mills open on the company's terms.

The **Pullman Strike** of 1894 was larger still. George Pullman's company cut wages but not rents in its company town, and the American Railway Union, led by Eugene Debs, boycotted Pullman cars nationwide. The boycott halted the mail and interstate commerce, so President Cleveland sent federal troops to keep them moving. The government won an injunction, and Debs went to jail for defying it.

Look at the photograph: a long line of uniformed Illinois National Guard troops stands in formation before the Arcade Building in Pullman, Chicago, while a crowd watches. The troops are the point of the picture. Debs left prison a socialist, and he later helped found the Socialist Party of America in 1901, convinced that the courts and the army would always side with the owners.

## Key terms

- **Trust** — a combination of companies under one board of trustees, formed to kill competition and fix prices.

- **Holding company** — a company that owns enough stock in other companies to control them.

- **Horizontal integration** — buying out rival firms in the same business, as Rockefeller did in oil.

- **Vertical integration** — owning every step of production, from raw material to finished goods, as Carnegie did in steel.

- **Sherman Antitrust Act** — the 1890 federal law outlawing combinations in restraint of trade.

- **Knights of Labor** — the industrial union founded in 1869, open to the unskilled, women, and Black workers.

- **American Federation of Labor** — the 1886 federation of skilled craft unions led by Samuel Gompers.

- **Haymarket affair** — the 1886 Chicago bombing at a rally for the eight-hour day, which broke the Knights of Labor.

- **Homestead Strike** — the 1892 Carnegie Steel lockout, broken by Pinkerton guards and state militia.

- **Pullman Strike** — the 1894 railroad strike, broken by a federal injunction and troops, after which Debs turned to socialism.

- **Injunction** — a court order that can stop a strike, used by the federal government at Pullman.

## Closing check

After this lesson, you should be able to explain how trusts and holding companies let a few firms control whole industries.

You should be able to explain why the Sherman Antitrust Act proved weak in its first years.

You should be able to explain why the Knights of Labor collapsed after Haymarket while the AFL survived.

You should be able to explain why labor lost the big strikes at Homestead and Pullman, and what Debs concluded from Pullman.

a. Identify one way businesses combined into larger firms in the late nineteenth century.

b. Briefly explain how a holding company let one firm control other companies.

Model answer: A holding company bought enough stock in other companies to control them, so one firm could direct many companies without merging them into a single firm. After the Trust was dissolved in 1892, Standard Oil reorganized as Standard Oil of New Jersey in 1899 and used it to hold its oil companies together.

c. Briefly explain one reason the federal government passed the Sherman Antitrust Act in 1890.

Model answer: Reformers and the public feared that trusts and combinations had grown too powerful, controlling whole industries and influencing government. The Sherman Act answered that fear by declaring illegal every combination in restraint of trade among the states.

How to think about it: part a asks for one way businesses combined, so name the trust or the holding company. Part b asks how a holding company worked, so explain control through stock ownership. Part c asks why Congress passed the Sherman Act in 1890, so name public fear of monopoly power.

### Worked item 2 — apush-saq-0182 (Topic 6.7)

Original secondary passage, written for this question from the grounding books:

> The Knights of Labor and the American Federation of Labor both organized workers in the Gilded Age, but they organized different workers for different ends. The Knights, founded in 1869, welcomed skilled and unskilled workers, women, and Black workers, and campaigned for broad reforms such as the eight-hour day and an end to child labor. The American Federation of Labor, founded in 1886 under Samuel Gompers, organized only skilled workers by craft and sought higher wages and shorter hours. After the Haymarket bombing of 1886, the Knights lost members as the public linked them with violence, while the AFL kept growing into the twentieth century.

Source: Original secondary passage, written for this question from Barron’s Premium 2027 Chapter 8 (Topic 6.7), 5 Steps to a 5, 2024, Chapter 18, and Princeton Review, Chapter 10.

a. Identify one group of workers the Knights of Labor welcomed that the AFL did not organize.

Model answer: The Knights welcomed unskilled workers into their ranks. The AFL organized only skilled workers by craft, so unskilled workers were left outside it.

b. Briefly explain one reason the Knights of Labor lost members after 1886.

Model answer: After the Haymarket bombing of 1886, the public linked the Knights with violence and radicalism. Many Americans shied away from the labor movement, and the Knights’ membership fell sharply.

c. Briefly explain one way the AFL’s goals differed from the Knights’ goals.

Model answer: The AFL sought higher wages and shorter hours for its own skilled members, a narrow set of bread-and-butter gains. The Knights campaigned for broad social reforms, such as the eight-hour day, an end to child labor, and equal pay for equal work.

How to think about it: part a asks for a group the Knights welcomed, so name the unskilled, women, or Black workers. Part b asks why the Knights lost members after 1886, so name Haymarket and the fear it spread. Part c asks how the AFL's goals differed, so contrast broad reform with higher wages and shorter hours for skilled crafts.
