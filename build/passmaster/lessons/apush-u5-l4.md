In 1860 the nation elected a president the South would not accept, and within five months seven states had left the Union. This lesson follows the election, the secession, and the four-year war that followed, and how the war changed from a fight to save the Union into a fight to end slavery.

## The election that broke the parties

The Democratic Party split in 1860. Northern Democrats nominated Stephen Douglas on popular sovereignty. Southern Democrats walked out and nominated John C. Breckinridge, who stood for slavery in all the territories. A new Constitutional Union Party nominated John Bell and avoided the slavery question. The Republicans nominated Abraham Lincoln.

Lincoln promised not to interfere with slavery where it already existed, but he would block its expansion into the territories. He had made the case in 1858: "this government cannot endure, permanently, half slave and half free." The line comes from his House Divided speech, and it named the choice the nation faced.

Lincoln swept the Electoral College, carrying every Northern and Western state. The vote totals tell the same sectional story: Lincoln won 1,865,908 votes, under 40 percent, and 180 electors, all from the free states; Douglas 1,380,202 votes and 12 electors; Breckinridge 848,019 votes and 72 electors from the Deep South; Bell 590,901 votes and 39 electors from the Upper South. He won no Southern state and was not even on the ballot in much of the South.

Look at the map. Lincoln's red covers the North. He won California and Oregon statewide, but at county level the Pacific coast is mixed, with green, blue, and orange counties. Purple marks fusion tickets, and gray marks counties with no vote. Breckinridge's green covers the Deep South. Douglas's blue and Bell's yellow split the border and the upper South. The map shows a nation voting in sections.

## Secession and Fort Sumter

South Carolina led the **secession**, the withdrawal of a state from the Union, on December 20, 1860. Within six weeks Mississippi, Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, and Texas followed. In February 1861 their delegates met at Montgomery, Alabama, formed the Confederate States of America, and chose Jefferson Davis as president. In their declarations of secession, the states named slavery as their reason. Mississippi began, "Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery."

The upper South waited. Leaders there tried compromise once more. Senator John Crittenden of Kentucky proposed the **Crittenden Compromise**: protecting slavery where it already existed and extending the Missouri Compromise line to the Pacific. Republicans refused to surrender the free-soil position that had just won them the election, and the plan failed.

Congress tried once more with the Corwin Amendment, passed by the Senate on March 2, 1861. It would have barred Congress forever from interfering with slavery in the states. It came too late, and secession went on.

Lincoln, inaugurated March 4, 1861, said he would not start a war but would hold federal property. He also argued the Union was perpetual: no state could lawfully leave it. The test came at Fort Sumter, a federal fort in Charleston harbor. On April 12, 1861, Confederate guns opened fire, and the garrison surrendered on April 13, evacuating it the next day. Lincoln called for 75,000 volunteers, and men across the North answered; the Northern states united behind it. Virginia, Arkansas, North Carolina, and Tennessee seceded.

Four slave states stayed: Maryland, Delaware, Kentucky, and Missouri, the **border states**. West Virginia later broke away from Virginia and entered the Union as a separate state in 1863. Keeping them mattered. Maryland ringed Washington, and Kentucky guarded the Ohio River.

## Why the war lasted

Both sides expected a short war, over by Christmas 1861. Bull Run, on July 21, 1861, ended that hope: Confederate troops routed the Union army, which retreated to Washington in disorder.

The numbers favored the North. The historian James Ford Rhodes, writing in 1917, counted twenty-three states against eleven, and twenty-two million people against nine, of whom three and one-half million were enslaved. The 1860 census counted about 22 million free people in the North against under 6 million in the South, 22,000 miles of railroad against 9,000, and about nine-tenths of factory output against one-tenth. The South's advantages were defense, distance, and generals. It only had to hold its ground; the North had to conquer the whole South.

The war lasted because the task was so large: the Union had to conquer and occupy the South while holding a 3,500-mile blockade. New weapons made it worse. The rifled musket made close fighting deadlier and gave the defense the advantage, so battles became long-range firefights and less decisive, and neither army could win the war with one victory.

The war strained both home fronts. Lincoln suspended the writ of **habeas corpus**, the right to be brought before a judge, and jailed thousands of **Copperheads**, Northern Democrats who opposed the war, without trial. The South suffered worse: inflation, food shortages, and desertion.

In 1863 Congress passed a draft law, **conscription**. The $300 fee to buy out of service set off riots in New York City that July, in which more than 100 people were killed, many of them Black New Yorkers. The draft drew 776,829 names, yet only 46,347 men entered as draftees; most paid, hired substitutes, or were exempted, so its main effect was pushing men to volunteer.

## The plan and the turning tide

General Winfield Scott, the Union army’s elderly commander, alone predicted a long war. His **Anaconda Plan** — mocked by newspapers — was to blockade the Southern coast and seize the Mississippi, strangling the Confederacy. Look at the cartoon. J. B. Elliott’s "Scott’s Great Snake" of 1861 draws the blockade as a giant snake coiling around the South, its head at the Mississippi.

![Scott's Great Snake, J. B. Elliott, 1861](u5/5s24-ch15-mcq-06.jpg)
Figure 2: "Scott's Great Snake," 1861. The blockade coils around the South. Public domain.

The blockade took time, but it worked: the navy closed Southern ports, captured New Orleans in April 1862, and cut off cotton exports and manufactured imports. Britain and France never intervened for cotton; Europe found other supplies, and neither power would defend slavery.

In the East, Lee won at Second Bull Run, Fredericksburg, and Chancellorsville. In the West, Grant took Forts Henry and Donelson in February 1862 and fought at Shiloh in April. The turning point came in July 1863: Lee’s invasion failed at Gettysburg, and Grant took Vicksburg on July 4, giving the Union the whole Mississippi and splitting the Confederacy.

Alexander Gardner photographed dead Confederate soldiers by a fence at Antietam, Maryland, in September 1862. Antietam was the bloodiest single day of the war, and the Union’s narrow victory there gave Lincoln the moment he needed to issue the Emancipation Proclamation. About 360,000 Union soldiers and 258,000 Confederates died in the war, more than 500,000 in all. Disease killed far more than battle: about 250,000 Union soldiers died of disease against about 110,000 in battle, and the same pattern held for the Confederacy.

![Dead Confederate soldiers at Antietam, Maryland, September 1862](u5/pr25e-ch09-q11.jpg)

## Total war and victory

In 1864 Grant took command of all Union armies and devised a coordinated assault, pinning Lee’s army in Virginia, where the North could replace losses the South could not. Meanwhile Sherman drove toward Atlanta, the South’s railroad hub, and took it in September 1864, securing Lincoln’s reelection.

Then Sherman marched. In November 1864 he burned Atlanta’s militarily useful parts and marched across Georgia to Savannah, living off the land. His men tore up railroads, burned barns and mills, and freed enslaved people as they went. In early 1865 he turned north through the Carolinas, showing the Confederate army could no longer protect civilians in the Southern heartland.

This was **total war**: war aimed not only at armies but at the farms, railroads, and supplies that sustained them, meant to break the South’s will to fight. Desertions mounted as Confederate soldiers went home to protect their families.

About 180,000 Black men served. At first they were paid $10 a month, $3 deducted for clothing, against $13 for white soldiers. The 54th Massachusetts protested the unequal pay, and Frederick Douglass pressed the case, until Congress granted equal pay in June 1864.

The Union printed about $450 million in **greenbacks**, paper money backed only by faith in the government, under the Legal Tender Acts of 1862 and 1863. It levied the first federal income tax in 1861 and chartered national banks in 1863–1864.

In Virginia, Grant trapped Lee at Petersburg, and in April 1865 Lee surrendered at Appomattox Court House. The other Confederate armies soon laid down their arms. Five days later, on April 14, John Wilkes Booth assassinated Lincoln at Ford’s Theatre in Washington.

Congress passed the **Thirteenth Amendment** on January 31, 1865, and the states ratified it on December 6, abolishing slavery nationwide.

## From Union to emancipation

At the start Lincoln’s only goal was to restore the Union, and he resisted abolitionist appeals, fearing talk of freeing slaves would cost him the border states. But enslaved people forced the question, escaping to Union lines, giving information, building fortifications, and fighting. By summer 1862 he believed the time had come.

On September 22, 1862, he issued the preliminary **Emancipation Proclamation**, effective January 1, 1863. The printed proclamation, signed by Lincoln and Secretary of State Seward, declares that all persons held as slaves in the states in rebellion "are, and henceforward shall be free."

![Emancipation Proclamation broadside, January 1, 1863](u5/original-ctx-u5-06.jpg)
Figure 4: The Emancipation Proclamation broadside, January 1, 1863, signed by Lincoln and Seward. Public domain.

The proclamation freed no one where the Union could enforce it: not the border states, not the Union-held parts of the South. But it changed the war’s purpose: the Union now fought for freedom as well as reunion, and it opened the army to Black soldiers. It also ended any chance of European aid. He feared white Northerners and border-state Unionists would read emancipation as an invitation to slave rebellion, so he presented it as orderly and safe.

Lincoln gave the war meaning at Gettysburg. Four months after the battle, dedicating the cemetery, he said the nation had been "conceived in Liberty," dedicated to the idea that "all men are created equal," and called for "a new birth of freedom." The Address made the war a test of whether a nation built on equality could last.

In March 1865, Lincoln’s Second Inaugural looked past victory: "with malice toward none, with charity for all," he called the nation to bind up its wounds and care for soldiers, widows, and orphans — reconciliation, though many Northerners wanted punishment.

## Worked items

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0152 (Topic 5.7)

The stimulus is James Ford Rhodes’s account of the two sides at the start of the war, from his History of the Civil War (1917):

> “The array was now complete. Twenty-three States were pitted against eleven; twenty-two million people against nine, and of the nine, three and one-half million were slaves. Each side had peculiar advantages.

> But neither section understood the other. If the South had known that secession must result in war and that the foe would be a united North, it is doubtful if she would have proceeded to the last extremity. It is still more doubtful if the North would have fought, had she known that she must contend against a united Southern people. . . .

> But now the North confronted five and a half million earnest and brave people, supported by three and a half million servants, who grew the food and took care of the women and children at home while the men fought in the field.”

a. Identify Rhodes's claim about what each side misunderstood at the start of the war.

Model answer: Rhodes claims that neither section understood the other. The South did not know that secession would mean war against a united North, and the North did not know it would face a united Southern people. Each side expected the other to give way.

b. Explain ONE event or development from 1860 or 1861 that supports Rhodes's claim that the South misjudged the North.

Model answer: The Northern response to Fort Sumter supports Rhodes. After the bombardment of April 12, 1861, Lincoln's call for 75,000 volunteers was answered by men across the North, and the Northern states united behind the war. The South had expected the North to let it go in peace, but instead it faced a mobilized enemy.

c. Explain ONE event or development before the Civil War that shows one side's unwillingness to compromise.

Model answer: The failure of the Crittenden Compromise shows unwillingness to compromise. Republicans in Congress refused to accept federal protection for slavery south of the Missouri Compromise line, the price of the deal, because it surrendered the free-soil position that had won them the election. With that refusal, the last political path back to union closed.

How to think about it: part a asks for Rhodes’s claim, so restate what each side misunderstood. Part b asks for an event from 1860 or 1861 that supports the claim, so name the Northern response to Fort Sumter. Part c asks for an unwillingness to compromise, so name the compromise that failed and who refused it.

### Worked item 2 — apush-saq-0155 (Topic 5.8)

The stimulus is a paraphrase of historian James McPherson’s argument that Americans on both sides expected a short, glorious war in 1861, yet the conflict dragged on for four years. He writes that the South’s will to resist and the North’s need to conquer an entire society made anything swift impossible.

a. Briefly explain ONE reason the Civil War lasted longer than people in 1861 expected.

Model answer: One reason the war lasted so long was the size of the Confederacy. The Union had to conquer and occupy the whole South while holding a 3,500-mile blockade. That task took years of marching, sieging, and garrisoning, not the single battle Northern newspapers had predicted.

b. Briefly explain a SECOND reason the Civil War lasted longer than people in 1861 expected.

Model answer: A second reason was new weaponry. The rifled musket made close fighting far deadlier and gave the defense the advantage over the offense, so battles became longer-range firefights and less decisive, and neither army could win the war with one victory.

c. Briefly explain ONE important turning point of the Civil War.

Model answer: One turning point came in July 1863, when the Union won at Gettysburg and captured Vicksburg in the same week. Lee’s invasion of the North failed, and Grant’s victory gave the Union the whole Mississippi River and split the Confederacy in two. After that summer the Confederacy was in retreat.

How to think about it: parts a and b ask for two different reasons the war lasted, so name the size of the task first and the weapons second. Part c asks for one turning point, so name July 1863 and say what changed on both fronts.

### Worked item 3 — apush-saq-0159 (Topic 5.9)

The stimulus is Abraham Lincoln’s Emancipation Proclamation of January 1, 1863:

> “And I hereby enjoin upon the people so declared to be free to abstain from all violence, unless in necessary self-defence; and I recommend to them that, in all cases when allowed, they labor faithfully for reasonable wages.”

—Abraham Lincoln, Emancipation Proclamation, January 1, 1863

a. Identify and briefly explain ONE thing Lincoln asks freed people to do in this passage.

Model answer: Lincoln asks freed people to abstain from violence except in self-defence and to labor faithfully for reasonable wages. He is telling them to accept freedom peacefully and to work for pay rather than to rise against their former owners. The passage shows him urging order at the very moment he declares them free.

b. Briefly explain ONE reason Lincoln included this appeal to freed people in the proclamation.

Model answer: Lincoln included the appeal because he feared white Northerners and border-state Unionists would see emancipation as an invitation to slave rebellion. By urging freed people to work peacefully for wages, he presented emancipation as orderly and safe. The appeal also answered critics who claimed he meant to incite violence in the South.

c. Briefly explain ONE way the Emancipation Proclamation changed the Union’s war aims.

Model answer: The proclamation added ending slavery to the Union’s war aims, which had been only to restore the Union. It also opened the Union army to Black soldiers, so freed people could fight for their own freedom. From 1863 on, a Union victory meant freedom as well as reunion.

How to think about it: part a asks for one thing Lincoln asks, so quote the passage and say what it means. Part b asks why he included the appeal, so name the fear he was answering. Part c asks how the proclamation changed the war’s aims, so name what was added and who could now fight.

## Key terms

- **secession** — the withdrawal of a state from the Union.

- **border states** — the slave states that stayed in the Union: Maryland, Delaware, Kentucky, and Missouri.

- **Crittenden Compromise** — the 1860 proposal to protect slavery where it existed and extend the Missouri Compromise line to the Pacific.

- **Anaconda Plan** — Scott's strategy of blockading the Southern coast and seizing the Mississippi River.

- **total war** — war aimed at the farms, railroads, and supplies that sustain an army, meant to break the will to fight.

- **Emancipation Proclamation** — Lincoln's 1863 order freeing enslaved people in the states in rebellion.

- **Gettysburg Address** — Lincoln's 1863 speech dedicating the Gettysburg cemetery and calling for "a new birth of freedom."

- **habeas corpus** — the right to be brought before a judge, which Lincoln suspended during the war.

- **Copperheads** — Northern Democrats who opposed the war.

- **conscription** — a military draft, imposed by Congress in 1863.

- **greenbacks** — paper money printed by the Union under the Legal Tender Acts.

- **Thirteenth Amendment** — the 1865 amendment abolishing slavery nationwide.
