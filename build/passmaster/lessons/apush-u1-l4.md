Who did the work in Spain's American empire, and who decided their place in it? This lesson answers that question. The answer is a system built on forced labor that outlasted every reform, and a society ranked by ancestry that lasted for centuries.

## The encomienda and its limits

Spain's first labor system was the **encomienda**. The crown granted a colonist authority over a specified number of Native people. The colonist was obliged to protect them and convert them to Catholicism, and in exchange he was entitled to their labor, on plantations and in the mines. If that sounds like slavery, that is because it was close to it: the protection was often a fiction, and the labor was forced.

The system had a critic from inside. Bartolomé de Las Casas, a Dominican friar who had lived in the Americas, wrote A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies in 1542, charging Spaniards with cruelty toward Native peoples. Read his words below. He wrote to persuade the crown, so his numbers are contested, but his charges forced the crown to act. The **New Laws** of 1542 limited the encomienda and forbade new grants of Native labor.

> Finally, in one word, their Ambition and Avarice, than which the heart of Man never entertained greater, and the vast Wealth of those Regions; the Humility and Patience of the Inhabitants (which made their approach to these Lands more facil and easie) did much promote the business: Whom they so despicably contemned, that they treated them (I speak of things which I was an Eye Witness of, without the least fallacy) not as Beasts, which I cordially wished they would, but as the most abject dung and filth of the Earth.

Figure 1: Las Casas on Spanish cruelty, A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies (1542), English translation of 1689. Spelling as in the original. Public domain.

## Reform bent, it did not break

Reform did not end forced labor. The New Laws were resisted by colonists who depended on Native workers, and the crown backed away from enforcing them. The system bent and survived. Spain also used a second system, the **repartimiento**, a temporary draft of Native workers for wages, distinct from the encomienda. The compulsory draft began in the last quarter of the 16th century: the Andean mita took one in seven men for the Potosí mines, and New Spain's towns supplied four percent of their tributaries each week.

The crown's retreat shows the limit of reform from above. The men who profited from the encomienda sat on the councils that were supposed to enforce the New Laws, and the king in Madrid was an ocean away. Grants were limited on paper, and labor continued in practice, because the crown needed the workers as much as the colonists did: the silver mines and the sugar fields paid for the empire. Reform from Madrid could not beat interest on the ground.

## Las Casas and the debate over Native peoples

Las Casas's own story shows how hard reform was. He began as an encomendero, a holder of an encomienda, and in his early years he proposed replacing Native labor with African labor. He later recanted that proposal and spent the rest of his life arguing that Native peoples were fully human and should be treated as subjects of the crown, not as slaves. His change of mind is part of the record, and it matters: the man who denounced the cruelty had once proposed part of it.

The debate reached the crown itself. In 1550 and 1551, at Valladolid in Spain, Las Casas argued against Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda, a scholar who had never visited the Americas. Sepúlveda argued that Native peoples were by nature inferior and incapable of governing themselves, and were therefore **natural slaves**. The wars of conquest, he said, were **just wars**, fought to bring Christianity and civilized rule to people who could not rule themselves.

Las Casas answered in the language of natural law: Native peoples were rational and capable of self-rule, owed the same protection as any subject. Neither man won outright, and the conquest went on. But the debate itself is the point: Spain argued about the justice of its empire in public, before its own king, and both sides argued in natural law and Christian theology. The portrait below was engraved in 1791, about 225 years after Las Casas died, so it shows how later generations remembered him, not his face.

## Why the labor force changed

The encomienda depended on Native workers, and disease destroyed that workforce. Epidemic disease killed enormous numbers of Native people who had no immunity to European illnesses. The mines and plantations still needed workers, and the Native towns kept emptying. The demand for coerced labor never went away. What changed was who was forced to work.

Spain turned to enslaved Africans, brought in under the **asiento**, the crown's monopoly contract for supplying enslaved people to Spanish America. The first asiento was granted in August 1518 to Laurent de Gouvenot, for eight years and a maximum of 4,000 people. Gouvenot sold the license on to Genoese merchants for 25,000 ducats. The contract system meant the crown controlled the trade and took a cut of it. The people it moved had no say in any of it.

The shift changed slavery itself. In the older world, slavery had many forms, and it was often not for life. In Spanish America, slavery became lifelong and hereditary: children born to enslaved mothers were born slaves. That break from older slavery is the point to remember. The shift also brought large African populations whose descendants shaped colonial culture, from the food and music to the **Maroon** communities that follow in the next section.

## A society ranked by ancestry

Spanish America became a society ranked by ancestry. At the top stood the **peninsulares**, born in Spain, and the **criollos**, born in the Americas. Together they were only one or two percent of the population, yet they held the offices and the land. Below them came the **mestizos**, children of Spaniards and Native people, about four or five percent, and the **mulattos**, children of Spaniards and Africans. Native peoples and Africans stood at the bottom.

The ranking was not only custom; it was law. Spain divided its American subjects into two republics, the república de españoles, the republic of Spaniards, and the república de indios. Each had its own towns, its own officials, and its own obligations. The two-republics system kept Native communities separate and under Spanish rule, and made ancestry a legal fact, not only a social one.

The system was painted as well as lived. Spanish families in Mexico commissioned **casta** paintings, each panel naming a mixture and the child it produced: "De español e india, mestiza" means from a Spaniard and an Indian woman, a mestiza child. The paintings are evidence for the casta system as it looked in the 1700s, not for 1500 to 1607. Do not cite them for a period they postdate by a century.

The grid below makes the system's logic visible. Sixteen panels, each a family, each labeled with the parents' mixture and the child's name: mestizo, castizo, mulato, morisco, chino, salta atrás, and lobo. The names multiplied as the mixtures multiplied, and the grid turned ancestry into a chart. Read it as the elite's view of the world, not a neutral record.

## Resistance and survival

Enslaved people did not only endure the system; they escaped it and fought it. Across the Americas, people who fled slavery formed Maroon communities, settlements of escaped enslaved people hidden in mountains and forests. The Maroons preserved African traditions: herbs and healing, drumming and dancing, languages and religions blended with what their masters imposed. Religions such as voodoo blend Christianity with African belief, and enslaved people sang African songs in the fields as they worked.

The largest Maroon settlement was Palmares, in the hills of Brazil. It began in the early 1600s and grew to more than 30,000 people, a state within a state, with its own farms, crafts, and government. Portuguese forces attacked it for decades before finally conquering it in 1694. Palmares lasted almost a century, longer than many colonies, and it stands as proof that enslaved people built free societies inside a slave system.

Resistance took quieter forms too. Native peoples kept their languages and rituals inside the missions and the mines, and African peoples kept theirs inside slavery. The casta system tried to fix every person in a rank by birth. The people it ranked kept remaking their own cultures anyway, in the families, the fields, and the hidden settlements. That remaking is as much a part of this period as the laws that tried to stop it.

## Key terms

- **encomienda** — the grant of Native labor to a colonist, in exchange for protection and Christian instruction.

- **New Laws** — the 1542 laws that limited the encomienda and forbade new grants of Native labor.

- **repartimiento** — a temporary draft of Native workers for wages, distinct from the encomienda.

- **asiento** — the crown's monopoly contract for supplying enslaved people to Spanish America.

- **Maroon** — an enslaved person who escaped, or the community such people formed.

- **casta** — the system ranking colonial society by ancestry, pictured in the casta paintings.

- **peninsulares** — Spaniards born in Spain, at the top of the colonial hierarchy.

- **criollos** — Spaniards born in the Americas, below the peninsulares.

- **mestizos** — children of Spaniards and Native people.

- **mulattos** — children of Spaniards and Africans.

- **natural slaves** — Sepúlveda's claim that Native peoples were by nature inferior and unable to govern themselves.

- **just wars** — Sepúlveda's claim that the wars of conquest were fought to bring Christianity and civilized rule.
