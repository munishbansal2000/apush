In the early 1800s, Americans who felt their new nation had grown more interested in politics than in God began gathering in fields and groves to be converted all over again. This lesson follows that revival wave, the preachers who rode it, and the reformers, philosophers, and new communities it set in motion.

## The revivals come to the frontier

The **Second Great Awakening** was a wave of evangelical revivals that swept the United States in the early nineteenth century, strongest in the 1820s and 1830s. Its signature gathering was the **camp meeting**: days of outdoor preaching where thousands camped, listened, and came forward to convert. The most famous was Cane Ridge, Kentucky, in August 1801, where ten to twenty thousand people gathered around a log meetinghouse that seated about four hundred.

Look at the engraving below. A preacher stands on a raised platform at Eastham, Massachusetts, in 1852, arm lifted, speaking to a crowd on benches under the trees. The scene was still ordinary fifty years after Cane Ridge: the revival had become a lasting American form.

![Exhortation and preaching at the camp meeting at Eastham, 1852](u2/barrons-2027-pt2-49.jpg)
Figure 1: Exhortation and preaching at the camp meeting at Eastham, Massachusetts, 1852. Public domain.

The revival's great preacher was Charles Grandison Finney, whose Rochester revivals of 1830-1831 made him famous. Finney taught that any sinner could choose conversion, rejecting the old Puritan doctrine of predestination, which held that God had fixed each soul's fate in advance. The canal country of upstate New York, where his preaching burned hottest, came to be called the **burned-over district**. The same belief powered **perfectionism**, the idea that people could perfect themselves and then perfect society, which turned converts into reformers against drink, slavery, and vice.

## From the self to the community

Not everyone who caught the era's spiritual energy joined a revival. A small circle in Concord, Massachusetts, around Ralph Waldo Emerson and Henry David Thoreau, built **transcendentalism**, the belief that truth lives inside each person and is reached by intuition and nature rather than by churches or tradition. Emerson's "Self-Reliance" (1841) told readers to trust themselves, and his 1837 "American Scholar" address demanded intellectual independence from Europe. Thoreau lived the idea at Walden Pond from 1845 to 1847, and his 1849 essay on civil disobedience argued that conscience outranks law.

The same discontent with a market society that measured people by output also produced the **utopian community**, an experiment in communal living built around a guiding principle. Brook Farm, founded outside Boston in 1841 by the transcendentalist George Ripley, shared labor equally and valued leisure. New Harmony, founded in Indiana in 1825 by Robert Owen, was built on total equality. The Oneida Community, founded in New York in 1848 by John Humphrey Noyes, shared property and labor and practiced complex marriage.

New faiths grew in the same heat. Joseph Smith founded the Mormon church in upstate New York in 1830; hostility pushed the community from New York to Ohio, Missouri, and Illinois, where a mob killed Smith in 1844, and Brigham Young led most Mormons to Utah in 1847. And the ferment reached Native peoples too: after the defeat of the Iroquois Confederacy, the Seneca prophet Handsome Lake wove traditional Seneca beliefs together with Quaker ideas into the Longhouse Religion, denouncing alcohol and division and offering hope after dispossession.

## The reformers go to work

The revival's belief in perfecting people did not stay in church. In the 1830s and 1840s, converts carried it into crusades. The largest was temperance: by 1830 Americans drank more per person than ever before or since, and women watched wages vanish in taverns and drunken husbands come home violent. The American Temperance Society, founded in 1826, claimed 1.5 million members. Temperance worked by voluntary pledges and moral persuasion to change private behavior, not by law.

Look at the engraving below. The Boott Cotton Mills at Lowell rise beside the river. The mills were the new world reformers were answering: young farm women earning wages in crowded towns. Reformers built institutions to give it a conscience — Dorothea Dix's asylums for the mentally ill, Horace Mann's free public schools, which Catholic families feared would teach Protestant doctrine, and penitentiaries meant to reform, not merely punish.

![Boott Cotton Mills at Lowell, Massachusetts](u4/original-misc-us-culture-03.jpg)
Figure 2: The Boott Cotton Mills at Lowell, Massachusetts.

William Lloyd Garrison's Liberator, begun in 1831, demanded **immediate emancipation** — freedom at once, no payment to owners — breaking with gradual emancipation and the plan to send free Black Americans to Liberia. Colonization drew antislavery sympathizers hoping Liberia offered escape from racism, and white supremacists wanting free Black people gone. Black abolitionists led too: Frederick Douglass's Narrative (1845) told it from inside.

The fight taught its own lesson: women barred from speaking against slavery, like Sarah Grimké — raised in a slaveholding Charleston family, she knew slavery firsthand — began demanding equality for themselves, a demand the Seneca Falls Convention put in the **Declaration of Sentiments**, modeled on the Declaration of Independence, in 1848. Margaret Fuller carried the argument further in *Woman in the Nineteenth Century* (1845), claiming women's equality and self-development.

## Black Americans answer slavery

While white reformers debated slavery, Black Americans led the fight against it themselves. Enslaved people rose against their masters — Gabriel planned a rising in Richmond in 1800, and Nat Turner led a rebellion in Southampton County, Virginia, in 1831. Both risings were put down, their leaders hanged, and Southern states answered with stricter laws. Yet the risings forced the nation to see that enslaved people would not accept bondage quietly.

Free Black Americans built institutions of their own. Richard Allen founded the **African Methodist Episcopal Church** in Philadelphia in 1816, the first independent Black denomination, born after Black worshippers were pulled from prayer in a white-led Philadelphia church. In 1827, John Brown Russwurm and Samuel Cornish began Freedom's Journal, the first Black-owned newspaper, declaring that Black Americans would plead their own cause. Delegates met at Mother Bethel Church in 1830 for the first **National Negro Convention**, organizing to defend free Black rights.

Black writers carried the argument further. David Walker, a free Black Bostonian, published his Appeal in 1829, calling Black Americans to wake up and claim their rights at home rather than leave for Africa. Henry Highland Garnet told a convention in 1843 that enslaved people should rise and strike for freedom.

And Frederick Douglass, who escaped slavery in 1838, told his story in the Narrative of 1845 — and posed for the portrait below, a free Black man presenting himself as dignified and commanding. He later founded the North Star, an abolitionist newspaper, in 1847.

![Frederick Douglass, daguerreotype portrait by Samuel J. Miller, 1847–1852](u4/frederick-douglass-miller-1847-52.jpg)
Figure 3: Frederick Douglass, photographed by Samuel J. Miller between 1847 and 1852. Public domain.

## Cotton, slavery, and the Southern way of life

The South took a different road, and the reason lay in its fields. Whitney's cotton gin (1793) made short-staple cotton quick to clean, and planters spread cotton westward. The crop boomed: 73,000 bales in 1800, more than 2 million by 1850.

Cotton needed hands all year, so the enslaved population grew with it: 700,000 in 1790, about 2 million in 1830. Importation was banned in 1808; the growth came from natural increase — children born into slavery — and the domestic slave trade moving enslaved people south and west. By 1850 nearly a third of Southerners were Black, a majority in Mississippi and South Carolina. The South stayed farm country, and its wealth rested on cotton, grown by enslaved labor and sold abroad.

Most white Southerners owned no slaves: in 1840 about one in three white families in the slave states held any. Yet slavery defined the region: it set the economy, cotton leading the nation's exports, and the politics, fights over new land becoming fights over slavery.

And it set the racial order: every white person above every Black person, owner or not. That shared rank, and the hope of owning slaves one day, bound them to the system.

The defense of slavery changed. Older Southerners had called slavery a necessary evil. After Nat Turner's rebellion in 1831, that apology gave way to open praise. In 1837 John C. Calhoun told the Senate slavery was a **positive good**, good for enslaved people and owners alike, and the foundation of free Southern institutions.

Other defenders compared Northern factory hands, fired when business was slow, with enslaved people their owners fed and housed. The argument traveled west: settlers in Texas brought slavery onto cotton land despite Mexico's ban, and the fight over their rebellion and annexation showed slavery shaping national politics.

## The period in one chain

Step back: what caused all of this? The market revolution is the first link: the population more than tripled between 1800 and 1840, and railroad mileage grew from 23 miles to more than 2,800. Canals, steamboats, railroads tied the regions into one market — and pulled them apart, the North on wage labor, the South on enslaved labor.

Look at the print below: New York harbor celebrates the Erie Canal's opening in 1825, sailing ships and steamboats. The canal tied western farms to New York instead of New Orleans, turning the West's trade toward the Northeast.

![New York harbor celebrates the Erie Canal's opening, 1825](u4/original-misc-us-culture-02.png)
Figure 4: New York harbor celebrates the Erie Canal's opening, 1825. Public domain.

Tocqueville, visiting in the 1830s, wrote that the white and Black races "do not amalgamate" — two communities living side by side, he warned, would one day collide.

Andrew Jackson said the same in his Farewell Address of 1837: "At every hazard, and by every sacrifice, this Union must be preserved." He warned that if selfishness replaced public spirit, Congress would become "a scramble for personal and sectional advantages." Sam Houston's Texan forces won independence at San Jacinto in 1836; Texas annexation in the 1840s, and the fight over slavery's spread, proved his fear right.

The chain ran through reform: the Awakening's belief in perfecting society led converts into abolition, and Angelina Grimké told Southern women in 1836 to read, pray, speak, and act — laws protecting slavery "ought to be no barrier in the way of your duty."

John Quincy Adams called the Missouri question, in his diary in January 1820, "a title-page to a great tragic volume." That is **sectionalism**: loyalty to a region and its economic interests over the nation, until the regions could no longer share one.

## Key terms (Topics 4.10–4.14)

- **Second Great Awakening** — The evangelical revival wave of the early nineteenth century, peaking in the 1820s and 1830s, that stressed personal conversion and inspired reform movements.

- **Camp meeting** — A large outdoor revival gathering lasting several days, where crowds camped, listened to preachers, and came forward to convert.

- **Burned-over district** — The canal country of upstate New York, named for the intensity of revival preaching that swept it in the 1820s and 1830s.

- **Perfectionism** — The belief that people could perfect themselves through right living, and then work to perfect society as well.

- **Transcendentalism** — The belief, centered on Emerson and Thoreau, that truth lives inside each person and is reached by intuition and nature.

- **Utopian community** — An experiment in communal living, usually rural, structured around a guiding principle such as shared labor or equality.

- **Immediate emancipation** — The demand, led by William Lloyd Garrison from 1831, that slavery end at once, with no payment to slave owners.

- **Declaration of Sentiments** — The statement of women's equality issued by the Seneca Falls Convention in 1848, modeled on the Declaration of Independence.

- **African Methodist Episcopal (AME) Church** — The independent Black denomination founded in Philadelphia in 1816 by Richard Allen and other Black Methodist ministers, after Black worshippers were pulled from prayer in white-led churches.

- **National Negro Convention** — National meetings of free Black delegates, beginning in Philadelphia in 1830, organized to defend Black rights and plan common action.

- **Positive good** — The defense of slavery, stated by John C. Calhoun in 1837, that slavery was not a necessary evil but a good for enslaved people and owners alike.

- **Sectionalism** — Loyalty to one's region and its economic interests over national policy.
