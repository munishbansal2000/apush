Why did the Cold War end in 1989, without a war between the superpowers? This lesson answers that question. The Soviet Union failed from within, and American pressure raised the price of competing until Moscow could no longer pay it.

## From détente to confrontation

In the 1970s, President Richard Nixon tried a policy called **détente**, a French word for easing tensions. The United States and the Soviet Union signed arms-control agreements and traded with each other, accepting that neither side could destroy the other. Détente meant coexistence: the two superpowers would compete, but within limits, and avoid a war neither could win.

The easing ended in December 1979, when Soviet troops invaded Afghanistan. President Jimmy Carter answered with a grain embargo and a boycott of the 1980 Olympic Games in Moscow, but the invasion went on. The Soviet move made many Americans doubt that coexistence could work, and it helped elect a president who promised a harder line.

Ronald Reagan took office in 1981 convinced that détente had let the Soviet Union grow stronger while America grew weaker. In 1982 he told the British Parliament that freedom and democracy would leave Marxism-Leninism behind, a system he called destined for the ash heap of history. In 1983 he called the Soviet Union an **evil empire**, a term that made clear he saw the Cold War as a moral contest, not only a contest of armies.

Reagan also launched a military buildup, including research on a space-based missile shield called the **Strategic Defense Initiative**. The shield, announced in 1983, was never built, but it forced Moscow to think about competing in space, where it could not afford to follow. Containment had meant holding the line against communism. Reagan wanted to roll it back.

## The Reagan Doctrine and its costs

Reagan's foreign policy had a second part: the **Reagan Doctrine**, the policy of supporting anti-communist forces around the world, even when those forces were undemocratic. The idea was to put pressure on Moscow everywhere at once, so the Soviet Union would have to defend its allies in many places at the same time.

The policy scored a quick success in Grenada, a small Caribbean island, where American troops landed in 1983 and removed a pro-communist government. It worked more slowly in the Philippines, where the United States had long backed President Ferdinand Marcos. When Marcos stole an election in 1986, Washington withdrew its support, and Corazon Aquino took power after a popular uprising.

Nicaragua brought the doctrine's greatest cost. Reagan backed the Contras, rebels fighting the left-wing Sandinista government, but Congress passed the Boland Amendment in 1982 cutting off aid to them. Officials in Reagan's administration then secretly sold arms to Iran and used the money to fund the Contras. The scheme, exposed in 1986, became the Iran-Contra affair.

Fourteen people were charged over the affair, and eleven were convicted. Two convictions were overturned on appeal, and President George H. W. Bush pardoned six of those charged in December 1992. The deeper question was constitutional: by funding the Contras in secret, the administration had denied Congress its power of the purse, the power to decide what the government spends. Reagan said he had no direct knowledge of the diversion.

## Gorbachev's reforms

In 1985, Mikhail Gorbachev became the leader of the Soviet Union. He inherited an economy that had barely grown for years, a country that could not afford both a huge military and support for its allies in Eastern Europe. Gorbachev believed the system could be saved, but only if it changed.

His two reforms were **glasnost**, openness in public life, and **perestroika**, restructuring of the economy to allow elements of capitalism. Glasnost let newspapers print criticism and citizens speak openly for the first time in decades. Perestroika let some businesses operate outside state control. Both reforms were meant to save communism. Instead, they let long-buried discontent surface.

The disaster at the Chernobyl nuclear plant in 1986 pushed Gorbachev further toward openness. Soviet officials first hid the accident, and the secrecy made the damage worse. Gorbachev concluded that the costs of secrecy were too high, and he pressed for more honest reporting. But openness could not fix an economy built on central planning, and shortages grew worse.

Gorbachev's most important choice came in Eastern Europe. When protests rose in 1989, he refused to send Soviet troops to crush them, breaking with the invasions of 1956 in Hungary and 1968 in Czechoslovakia. Once the threat of Soviet force was gone, governments that had ruled by fear had nothing left to hold them up.

## 1989: the year the wall fell

The change swept Eastern Europe in a single year. In Poland, the **Solidarity** movement forced the Communists from power. In Hungary, the Communist government gave way to a non-Communist regime. In Czechoslovakia, the Velvet Revolution replaced the Communist government without bloodshed. In Romania, the Ceausescu regime was overthrown in December, and in Bulgaria the Communist government was replaced.

The symbol of the whole change was the **Berlin Wall**, the concrete barrier that had divided Berlin since 1961. In November 1989, East Germany opened its borders, and crowds climbed onto the Wall and began tearing it down. Look at the photograph: a West German man stands on a ladder at Potsdamer Platz, chipping at the graffiti-covered Wall with a hammer and chisel while a crowd watches.

Within a year, Germany reunified in October 1990, ending a division that had lasted since 1945. The Soviet Union itself dissolved in December 1991. The two superpowers had never fought a direct war, and now one of them was gone. The Cold War ended not with a battle, but with the collapse of a system that could no longer hold itself together.

Reagan and Gorbachev had already shown that negotiation could work. The **INF Treaty** of 1987 eliminated an entire class of intermediate-range missiles, the first agreement to remove weapons rather than only limit them. It proved that the two sides could agree to disarm, and it set the stage for the arms reductions that followed the Cold War's end.

## The sole superpower

After 1991, the United States stood alone as the world's **sole superpower**, the only country able to act anywhere. The question was what to do with that power. Some argued America should use it to stop suffering abroad. Others argued it should stay out of other countries' quarrels.

The first test came in August 1990, when Iraq invaded Kuwait. President George H. W. Bush built a coalition of 34 nations and won United Nations backing. American-led forces drove Iraq out of Kuwait by February 1991, in a war remembered as the **Persian Gulf War**. Look at the photograph: troops in desert camouflage march across the sand during the war.

![American troops in the desert during the Persian Gulf War](u9/pr25e-ch13-q07.jpg)

Bush halted the advance at the Iraqi border and left Saddam Hussein in power. His mandate from the United Nations was limited to freeing Kuwait, not overthrowing Iraq's government. The decision kept the coalition together, but it left the question of Iraq unsettled, and American troops stayed in the region for years.

The limits of American power showed in Somalia in 1993, when American soldiers were killed in the capital, Mogadishu, during a mission to feed a starving population. The mission ended, and American leaders grew reluctant to commit troops where the mission was unclear. In Bosnia, the **Dayton Agreement** of 1995 ended a brutal war, and NATO enforced the peace.

The Cold War's end had not ended conflict. It had changed the question. America no longer faced one great enemy, but many smaller ones, and each new crisis raised the same question: when should the world's only superpower act, and when should it stay out?

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0278 (Topic 9.3)

The stimulus is a paraphrase of President Ronald Reagan's address at the Brandenburg Gate, Berlin, June 12, 1987: Speaking beside the Berlin Wall, Reagan challenged Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev to prove his commitment to openness by removing the Wall. Reagan described the Wall as a scar across the city and a test of whether Moscow truly sought freedom for Eastern Europe.

a. Briefly describe the historical situation in which Reagan gave this address.

Model answer: Reagan spoke in 1987, two years into Gorbachev's reforms, when Soviet-American relations were thawing after the harsh confrontation of his first term. The Berlin Wall still divided the city, and Gorbachev's talk of openness had raised hopes that the division of Europe might finally end.

b. Briefly explain ONE reason Reagan chose to challenge Gorbachev publicly at the Wall.

Model answer: Reagan wanted to test whether Gorbachev's reforms were real or only words, and a public challenge made the test visible to the whole world. He also wanted to rally Western support by framing the Wall as a moral symbol, not just a border.

c. Briefly explain ONE way the address reflected a change in Reagan's approach to the Soviet Union.

Model answer: In his first term Reagan had denounced the Soviet Union as an evil empire and refused serious negotiation. By 1987 he was speaking directly to Gorbachev as a partner, treating the Soviet leader as someone who could be persuaded rather than only confronted.

How to think about it: part a asks for the situation, so set the scene — Gorbachev's reforms, the thaw, the Wall still standing. Part b asks why Reagan challenged Gorbachev in public, so name his purpose: testing whether the reforms were real. Part c asks what changed, so compare the two terms — denunciation gave way to direct engagement.

### Worked item 3 — apush-saq-0280 (Topic 9.3)

The stimulus is an original summary based on the grounding books: Historians describe the Cold War's end as the meeting of two forces: a Soviet system that could no longer pay for its empire, and American pressure that raised the price of competing. The debate is over which force mattered more: the rot within the Soviet Union, or the push from Washington.

a. Briefly explain ONE internal Soviet development that contributed to the end of the Cold War.

Model answer: The Soviet economy had stagnated for years and could no longer afford both a huge military and support for its Eastern European satellites. Gorbachev's reforms then loosened the Party's grip, letting public discontent turn into open protest.

b. Briefly explain ONE American action that pressured the Soviet Union.

Model answer: Reagan's military buildup, including the Strategic Defense Initiative, forced Moscow to compete in an arms race it could not afford. The buildup raised the cost of rivalry just as the Soviet economy was failing.

c. Briefly explain ONE reason historians disagree about which cause mattered more.

Model answer: The two causes happened at the same time, so it is hard to separate their effects: Soviet weakness and American pressure fed each other. Historians also weigh the evidence differently, since some stress Gorbachev's choices while others stress the arms race.

How to think about it: part a asks for a Soviet cause, so stay inside the Soviet Union — a failing economy and reforms that loosened control. Part b asks for an American cause, so name an action from Washington — the buildup and the missile shield. Part c asks why historians disagree, so point to the overlap: the two causes happened together and fed each other.

## Key terms

- **détente** — the easing of tensions between the United States and the Soviet Union in the 1970s.

- **evil empire** — Reagan's term for the Soviet Union, casting the Cold War as a moral contest.

- **Strategic Defense Initiative** — Reagan's 1983 plan for a space-based missile shield, never built.

- **Reagan Doctrine** — the policy of supporting anti-communist forces abroad, even undemocratic ones.

- **glasnost** — Gorbachev's policy of openness in Soviet public life.

- **perestroika** — Gorbachev's restructuring of the Soviet economy to allow elements of capitalism.

- **INF Treaty** — the 1987 agreement that eliminated an entire class of intermediate-range missiles.

- **Berlin Wall** — the concrete barrier that divided Berlin from 1961 until November 1989.

- **Solidarity** — the Polish workers' movement that forced the Communists from power in 1989.

- **Persian Gulf War** — the 1991 war in which a 34-nation coalition drove Iraq out of Kuwait.

- **Dayton Agreement** — the 1995 agreement that ended the war in Bosnia, enforced by NATO troops.

- **sole superpower** — the United States' position after 1991 as the world's only superpower.
