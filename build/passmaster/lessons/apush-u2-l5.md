What happens when ordinary people start judging authority for themselves — in church, in court, and in the colony itself? This lesson answers that question. The answer is that the revivals, the trials, and the arguments of the 1700s taught colonists a habit they would not forget: to ask why they should obey.

## A sleeping church wakes

By the 1730s, many colonial churches were quiet. Ministers preached formal sermons, fewer people joined the church, and membership shrank from one generation to the next. The faith that had built New England seemed to be running down — and then the **Great Awakening** came.

Jonathan Edwards lit the fire. On July 8, 1741, at Enfield, Connecticut, he preached "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God," telling his hearers that they hung over the pit of hell like a spider over a fire, held only by God's hand. The sermon was read in a level voice, and the congregation wept. The words did the work.

George Whitefield brought the fire outdoors. The English **itinerant preacher** toured the colonies from 1739, preaching in fields to crowds in the thousands, his voice carrying across open ground. Benjamin Franklin tested the claim by measuring: standing in a semicircle, he reckoned more than 30,000 people could hear Whitefield at once. The number was a guess, but the method was the point — Franklin tested a claim by measuring it.

## New Lights, Old Lights

The revivals split the churches. **New Lights** welcomed the emotional preaching and the conversions it produced; **Old Lights** defended the settled ministers and the old order. The fight was about who could say what true religion was — a minister trained and ordained, or a hearer who had felt **conversion** in the heart.

The shift mattered beyond the church. Revivals taught ordinary colonists to judge religious authority for themselves — to trust a conversion they felt over a sermon they were told to accept. Once learned, that habit of judging authority for oneself was hard to confine to the church.

The churches had been loosening for years. The **Halfway Covenant** of 1662 let the children of church members be baptized even if their parents had never experienced conversion — a compromise that kept families inside a shrinking church. The revivals answered the shrinking with fire: new members came by feeling, not by birth.

Historians disagree about whether the Awakening helped cause the Revolution. The revivals taught defiance of authority, yet the fires had died decades before 1776, and many patriot leaders were never awakened. A habit learned in church may or may not have survived into politics — and the evidence pulls both ways.

The revivals were not the only challenge to authority. The **Enlightenment** taught colonists to trust reason and natural law over custom and clergy — a cooler challenge than the revivals' fire, but aimed at the same settled authorities. The two movements pulled in opposite directions, and both left the colonies less willing to obey without asking why.

## Salem: when fear ruled

Fifty years before the revivals, New England showed what religious fear could do. In Salem, in 1692, more than 100 people were accused of **witchcraft** and 20 were executed. The trials grew from a Puritan worldview in which God and Satan fought daily over every soul — and a neighbor's strange behavior could be the devil's work.

Look at the painting. Howard Pyle's Arresting a Witch, painted in 1883, shows an older woman in a shawl being led away by armed men along a snowy path. Pyle painted it nearly two centuries after Salem. It is an imagining — useful for how Americans remembered the trials, not for what an arrest looked like in 1692.

![Arresting a Witch, Howard Pyle, 1883](u2/5s24-ch03-mcq-34.jpg)
Figure 2: Howard Pyle's Arresting a Witch, 1883. An older woman led away by armed men — painted two centuries after Salem. Public domain.

The trials ended when the accusations reached too high. They touched prominent families and the governor's wife, and Governor Phipps dissolved the Court of Oyer and Terminer in October 1692. One petition that survives is Mary Easty's, written in September 1692, pleading for the lives of the accused. The colony later apologized for the executions.

Salem shows the limits of Puritan authority in both directions. The authority could turn fear into deadly punishment — twenty people hanged on accusations. And the authority could be stopped — when the panic threatened the colony's own leaders, the court was dissolved and the trials ended.

## The law of belief

The colonies also fought over belief in the courts. In 1735, John Peter Zenger was tried for **seditious libel** after his newspaper attacked the royal governor of New York. His lawyer, Andrew Hamilton, told the jury that the case was the cause of liberty. The jury acquitted Zenger — but no law changed, and truth was still no defense against libel. The acquittal was a victory for a jury, not for the law.

![The trial of John Peter Zenger, image published 1877–1896](u2/5s24-exam2-mcq-13.jpg)
Figure 3: The trial of John Peter Zenger, image published 1877–1896. A crowded courtroom imagining of the 1735 trial. Public domain.

Pennsylvania answered the question of belief differently. Penn's **Charter of Privileges** of 1701 gave the colony toleration and a representative government, and the township names on Holme's map — Welsh, German, Radnor, Haverford — show who came: settlers from across Europe, drawn by the promise that their faith would not be punished.

John Locke gave later Americans the argument for **toleration** — but not Penn or Williams. Locke wrote his Letter Concerning Toleration in 1685, and it was published in 1689. Penn's Frame of Government of 1682 was already written, and Williams had founded Providence in 1636 and written the Bloudy Tenent in 1644. Locke influenced later eighteenth-century advocates of toleration, never the men who came before him.

Maryland's Act of 1649 shows the same limit from the other side. It protected Christians who professed belief in Jesus Christ — and it left Jews and people of other faiths with no protection at all, while anyone who denied the Trinity could be put to death. Toleration in the colonies was often narrow, and the narrowness is part of the story.

## Strangers in a crowded land

The colonies were filling with strangers. Germans poured into Pennsylvania, and Benjamin Franklin complained in 1751 that they would "Germanize us instead of our Anglifying them." The complaint shows how fast the mixture was changing the colonies — and how English the English still expected them to stay.

Not all newcomers came free. **Redemptioners** paid for their passage after arriving, unlike indentured servants, who signed a contract before sailing. Both were bound labor, and both filled the colonies with people who owed years of work for the crossing.

The Puritan "calling" — the idea that work itself was God's service — shaped habits that outlived the religion. Prices and wages were controlled, thrift was a virtue, and the discipline of work stayed even after the faith that taught it faded. Historians Grob and Beck made that case in 1963: the habits outlived the religion.

Roger Williams had said it first: the care of souls could not belong to the civil magistrate, because the magistrate's power was only outward force. And the Mayflower Compact of 1620 still echoed in the revivals — a covenant of people governing themselves by agreement, the same spirit that would later judge kings as well as ministers.

## One empire, argued over

By 1754 the regions were distinct: the Chesapeake grew tobacco, about 40 percent enslaved; the Lower South grew rice, about 70 percent; New England held only a small share; the Middle Colonies grew wheat. France had sent about 60,000 settlers and built its empire on fur and Native partners.

The Caribbean took a large share of the Atlantic slave trade; the mainland took only about 6 percent, and the Caribbean's enslaved population grew by importation while the mainland's grew by natural increase. Champlain's Habitation of 1613 is a small fur-trade post; the Castello Plan of 1660 is a commercial town.

King Philip's War and Bacon's Rebellion shared a cause — English expansion onto Native land — but ended differently. The war began with Wampanoag attacks on Swansea on June 24, 1675, after the trial and execution of three Wampanoag men. The labor timeline — 1619, 1640, 1662, 1676, 1705 — shows slavery developing law by law. The 1662 partus rule is the best evidence that slavery came first and race ideas followed.

The Navigation Act of 1660 said colonial trade must travel on English or Irish-owned ships with English crews — and its first section admits ships "of the built of" the plantations, so building place is in the act.

London could not enforce the Acts across an ocean, so smuggling was common and the laws went laxly enforced for decades — salutary neglect. Historians still argue whether mercantilism oppressed or benefited the colonies: the laws restricted trade, but the empire protected colonial shipping and bought colonial crops.

## Worked items

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0059 (Topic 2.7)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage: the revivals of the 1730s and 1740s did more than fill churches. They taught ordinary colonists to judge religious authority for themselves — to trust a conversion they felt over a sermon they were told to accept. Once learned, that habit of judging authority for oneself was hard to confine to the church.

a. Identify one way the Great Awakening challenged established religious authority.

Model answer: The Awakening challenged established authority when itinerant preachers drew crowds away from settled ministers and told hearers to trust their own conversion experience over the minister's word. Ordinary colonists began judging sermons and ministers for themselves.

b. Explain how the Awakening gave ordinary colonists experience in judging authority for themselves.

Model answer: Revival preaching put the test of true religion inside the hearer: a conversion felt in the heart mattered more than a minister's learning or a church's approval. Colonists who judged a preacher by their own experience were practicing independent judgment.

c. Explain one reason historians disagree about whether the Awakening helped cause the Revolution.

Model answer: Historians disagree because the evidence pulls both ways: the revivals taught defiance of authority, yet the fires had died decades before 1776 and many patriot leaders were never awakened. A habit learned in church may or may not have survived into politics.

How to think about it: part a asks for a challenge, so name the itinerant preachers and the judgment they invited. Part b asks how the experience trained judgment, so put the test inside the hearer. Part c asks for a reason to disagree, so name the time gap or the unawakened leaders.

### Worked item 2 — apush-saq-0063 (Topic 2.7)

The stimulus is John Locke's own words, from A Letter Concerning Toleration — written in 1685, published in 1689:

> "The care of souls cannot belong to the civil magistrate, because his power consists only in outward force; but true and saving religion consists in the inward persuasion of the mind, without which nothing can be acceptable to God."

a. Identify Locke's claim about the civil magistrate's power.

Model answer: Locke claims the magistrate's power consists only in outward force — fines, prisons, and punishments. It cannot reach the inward persuasion of the mind, so the care of souls cannot belong to the magistrate.

b. Explain why Locke argues the magistrate cannot care for souls.

Model answer: True religion consists in the inward persuasion of the mind, which no outward force can produce. A magistrate can compel outward conformity, but he cannot compel belief, so his power is the wrong tool for souls.

c. Explain one way Locke's ideas influenced colonial thinking about religious freedom.

Model answer: Locke's argument shaped later 18th-century advocates of toleration, who used it to defend dissenters against established churches. That claim — that government cannot compel belief — became a foundation for religious freedom in America.

How to think about it: part a asks for Locke's claim, so name the limit of the magistrate's power. Part b asks why, so contrast outward force with inward persuasion. Part c asks for an influence, so name the later advocates — and remember the dates, so the influence runs forward, never back to Penn or Williams.

### Worked item 3 — apush-saq-0065 (Topic 2.8)

The stimulus is the Maryland Toleration Act's own words, passed April 21, 1649:

> "...that no person or persons whatsoever within this Province, or the Islands, ports, harbours, creeks or havens thereunto belonging, professing to believe in Jesus Christ, shall from henceforth be any ways troubled, molested, or discountenanced, for or in respect of his or her religion, nor in the free exercise thereof within this Province..."

a. Identify the protection the Act gives.

Model answer: The Act protects anyone in Maryland who professes to believe in Jesus Christ from being troubled, molested, or discountenanced for their religion. It guarantees them the free exercise of their religion within the province.

b. Explain why Maryland passed this Act.

Model answer: Maryland was founded by Lord Baltimore as a refuge for Catholics, and Catholics and Protestants lived side by side there. The Act was meant to keep the peace between them by promising that no Christian would be punished for their faith.

c. Explain one limit of the Act's protection.

Model answer: The Act protected only Christians — anyone who denied the Trinity or blasphemed God could be put to death under the same Act. Jews and people of other faiths had no protection at all.

How to think about it: part a asks for the protection, so quote the Act's own words — troubled, molested, discountenanced. Part b asks why, so name the Catholic refuge and the mixed colony. Part c asks for a limit, so name who is left out and what the Act itself threatens.

## Key terms

- **Great Awakening** — the religious revivals of the 1730s and 1740s, led by Edwards and Whitefield, that split the churches.

- **New Light** — a supporter of the revivals, who welcomed emotional preaching and conversion experience.

- **Old Light** — a defender of the settled ministers and the old order against the revivals.

- **Halfway Covenant** — the 1662 rule letting the children of church members be baptized even if their parents had never experienced conversion.

- **itinerant preacher** — a preacher who traveled from place to place, such as Whitefield, drawing crowds away from settled ministers.

- **conversion** — the personal experience of new birth in faith, which the revivals made the test of true religion.

- **witchcraft** — dealings with the devil, the charge that sent twenty people to execution in Salem in 1692.

- **seditious libel** — printed criticism of the government, the charge against John Peter Zenger in 1735.

- **Charter of Privileges** — Penn's 1701 grant giving Pennsylvania toleration and a representative government.

- **redemptioner** — a migrant who paid for passage after arriving, unlike an indentured servant, who signed before sailing.

- **toleration** — allowing religious dissent without punishment, as in Penn's Pennsylvania or Locke's Letter.

- **Enlightenment** — the movement that taught colonists to trust reason and natural law over custom and clergy.
