The Depression broke the old idea that the government should leave the economy alone. This lesson answers the question of what took its place: how Franklin Roosevelt rebuilt the federal government in four years, where his New Deal stopped short, and why the country that rebuilt itself at home still tried to stay out of the world's wars.

## The election of 1932

By 1932 the Depression had worn the country down. Unemployment stood above 20 percent, banks kept failing, and the Bonus Army, thousands of World War I veterans who had marched on Washington to demand early payment of bonuses due in 1945, had been driven out of the capital by troops under Douglas MacArthur. President Hoover, blamed for a collapse he had not caused and could not stop, ran for reelection against Franklin D. Roosevelt, the governor of New York.

Roosevelt promised a **New Deal** for the American people, though he did not say in detail what it would be. Voters heard a promise of action where Hoover had offered patience, and they gave Roosevelt 472 electoral votes to Hoover's 59, out of 531. The election was a mandate for experiment. Roosevelt took office on March 4, 1933, telling the country that the only thing it had to fear was fear itself.

Roosevelt's first act was to close the banks. On March 6 he declared a bank holiday, and Congress passed the Emergency Banking Act three days later. When the banks reopened, depositors who had lined up to withdraw their money mostly left it in place. Roosevelt explained the plan in the first of his fireside chats, radio talks that made the president a voice in American living rooms. Confidence, he showed, was a policy tool.

## The first hundred days

The first hundred days of Roosevelt's presidency produced a flood of laws. The Civilian Conservation Corps put young men to work planting trees and building parks. The **AAA**, the Agricultural Adjustment Administration, paid farmers to grow less so that crop prices would rise. The National Industrial Recovery Act set codes for industry, fixing prices and wages. Each program aimed at one part of the economy, and together they put Washington into farming, industry, and work.

Banking reform came first in importance. The Glass-Steagall Act of 1933 separated commercial banks from investment banks, and it created the **FDIC**, the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, which insured individual bank deposits. A bank failure would no longer wipe out a family's savings. The runs on banks that had spread panic in 1933 faded, because depositors now trusted that their money was safe.

The **TVA**, the Tennessee Valley Authority, showed the new reach of the federal government. Congress created it in 1933 to develop the Tennessee River Valley through dams and electricity. Look at the photograph: Norris Dam under construction on the Clinch River in 1936, its concrete face rising above the water while workers cross narrow walkways. The dam made power, controlled floods, and brought work to a poor region that had little of any of these.

## The Second New Deal

By 1935 the First New Deal was running into trouble. The Supreme Court struck down the National Industrial Recovery Act in Schechter v. United States in 1935, and the AAA in United States v. Butler in 1936. Unemployment still stood near 20 percent. Critics on the left, above all Senator Huey Long of Louisiana, demanded more. Long's Share Our Wealth plan promised to cap fortunes and guarantee every family a homestead and a minimum income, and millions joined his clubs.

Roosevelt answered with the Second New Deal of 1935, which shifted from reviving sectors of the economy toward direct help for working people. The **WPA**, the Works Progress Administration, gave jobs to the unemployed, building schools, roads, and airports. The **Wagner Act** protected workers' right to join unions and bargain collectively. The **Social Security Act** created old-age pensions and unemployment insurance, the core of the American welfare state.

Look at the poster "Work Pays America," made for the Works Progress Administration in the late 1930s. A farmer with a rake and a worker with a hammer shake hands over a stalk of wheat, under the word "Prosperity." The poster says the New Deal's case in one picture: work, not charity, would rebuild the country, and the government would provide the work. The WPA employed more than eight million people before it closed in 1943.

## Limits and opposition

The New Deal changed the government, but it did not end the Depression. Unemployment fell from 24.9 percent in 1933 to 14.3 percent in 1937, then rose again to 19.0 percent in 1938 after Roosevelt cut spending. The Depression would only end when wartime spending put the whole country to work. The New Deal's achievement was different: it changed what Americans expected the government to do, and those expectations stayed.

The New Deal also left much undone. Farm and domestic workers, who were disproportionately Black and Latino, were left out of Social Security and the Wagner Act's protections. Roosevelt, needing Southern Democrats in Congress, did not push an anti-lynching bill. Black Americans still backed him: the New Deal's jobs and relief reached them, and Eleanor Roosevelt spoke for civil rights. But the racial order of the South stayed mostly intact.

Roosevelt's biggest defeat came from his own overreach. After the Court struck down the NIRA and the AAA, he proposed in 1937 to add a justice to the Supreme Court for every justice over seventy, a **court-packing plan** that would have let him name six new justices. Even many Democrats called it an attack on the Court's independence, and Congress refused. The Court then began upholding New Deal laws anyway, but Roosevelt had spent political capital he never got back.

## A world drifting toward war

Abroad, the 1920s and 1930s showed the limits of leadership without commitment. The **Washington Naval Conference** of 1921-1922 produced the Five-Power Treaty, setting battleship tonnage ratios of 5:5:3 for the United States, Britain, and Japan, with France and Italy lower. The **Kellogg-Briand Pact** of 1928, signed by more than sixty nations, renounced war as national policy. Neither had any way to enforce its promises.

Americans stayed out of the League itself. Weary of war and afraid membership would drag the country into foreign wars, the Senate had refused to ratify the Treaty of Versailles in 1919.

The promises failed fast. Japan invaded Manchuria in 1931 without consequence. Italy conquered Ethiopia in 1935. Germany rearmed, took the Rhineland in 1936, and annexed Austria in March 1938. The League of Nations protested and did nothing. Treaties without enforcement could not bind a power determined to break them.

The United States pulled back from its hemisphere first. The **Good Neighbor Policy**, announced by Roosevelt in 1933, renounced armed intervention in Latin America. At Montevideo that year, the United States signed a convention against intervention, and the occupations of Haiti and Nicaragua were wound down. The policy mixed principle and economics: the Depression made foreign adventures expensive, and Washington wanted Latin American markets and cooperation, not resentment.

Congress then tried to keep the country out of war. The **Neutrality Acts** of 1935 to 1937 banned arms sales and loans to belligerents, and added cash-and-carry, letting belligerents buy non-military goods for cash, carried in their own ships. The laws treated all sides alike, but impartiality favors the strong, and the laws did nothing to stop aggressors. Roosevelt chipped away at them, repealing the arms embargo in 1939 and winning Lend-Lease in March 1941, which sent American arms to Britain on loan.

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0222 (Topic 7.10)

The stimulus is a paraphrase of Senator Huey Long's Share Our Wealth radio address, February 1934.

> Paraphrase of Senator Huey Long, Share Our Wealth radio address, February 1934: Long charged that a tiny elite hoarded the nation's wealth while millions suffered in the Depression. He proposed capping personal fortunes, guaranteeing every family a homestead and a minimum yearly income, shortening the workweek, and providing free college and old-age pensions, all paid for by steep taxes on the rich.

Source: Paraphrase of Senator Huey Long, Share Our Wealth radio address, February 1934. Post-1929 source, paraphrased, not quoted.

a. Identify ONE proposal in Long's Share Our Wealth plan.

Model answer: Long proposed capping personal fortunes so that no one could hoard vast wealth. The money taken from large fortunes would fund guarantees for ordinary families.

b. Explain why Long's movement threatened Roosevelt's position.

Model answer: Long built a national following of millions through Share Our Wealth clubs, and he talked of running against Roosevelt in 1936. His following sat to Roosevelt's left, so it could pull Democratic voters away from the president.

c. Explain ONE effect of pressure from Long and other critics on the left on the New Deal.

Model answer: Pressure from the left pushed Roosevelt toward the Second New Deal of 1935. The Social Security Act answered the demand for old-age pensions by putting government pensions into law.

How to think about it: part a asks for one proposal in Long's plan, so name the cap on fortunes or a guarantee for families. Part b asks why Long threatened Roosevelt, so count his following and his talk of running in 1936. Part c asks for one effect on the New Deal, so connect the pressure from the left to the Second New Deal.

### Worked item 3 — apush-saq-0224 (Topic 7.11)

The stimulus is original secondary text on American internationalism without entanglement in the 1920s.

> In the 1920s the United States practiced what historians call internationalism without entanglement. It hosted the Washington Naval Conference, signed the Kellogg-Briand Pact with more than sixty other nations, and took part in disarmament talks, all without joining the League of Nations or accepting any binding promise to defend another country.

Source: Original secondary text, written for this question from Barron's Premium 2027, Chapter 9, Topic 7.11, and Princeton Review, Chapter 11.

a. Identify ONE example of American internationalism without entanglement in the 1920s.

Model answer: The Washington Naval Conference of 1921-1922 is one example. The United States hosted the conference and signed the Five-Power Treaty, which limited battleship tonnage. It did all this without joining the League of Nations.

b. Explain ONE reason the United States pursued disarmament and peace pacts in the 1920s without joining the League of Nations.

Model answer: Americans were weary of war and feared that League membership would drag the country into foreign wars. The Senate had already refused to ratify the Treaty of Versailles, so disarmament talks offered a way to limit war without the binding commitments the League required.

c. Explain ONE limit of the disarmament and peace efforts of the 1920s.

Model answer: The treaties had no enforcement mechanism, so a nation could break them without consequence. Japan resented its lower battleship ratio and renounced the naval limits in the mid-1930s, and its invasion of Manchuria in 1931 showed the Kellogg-Briand Pact could not stop aggression.

How to think about it: part a asks for one example, so name the Washington Naval Conference or the Kellogg-Briand Pact. Part b asks why the United States chose this path, so name war weariness and the Senate's refusal of the League. Part c asks for one limit, so name the missing enforcement mechanism.

## Key terms

- **New Deal** — Franklin Roosevelt's program of relief, recovery, and reform that vastly expanded the federal government's economic role.

- **AAA** — the Agricultural Adjustment Administration, which paid farmers to grow less so crop prices would rise.

- **FDIC** — the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, created in 1933 to insure individual bank deposits.

- **Social Security Act** — the 1935 law that created old-age pensions and unemployment insurance.

- **Wagner Act** — the 1935 law that protected workers' right to join unions and bargain collectively.

- **WPA** — the Works Progress Administration, which gave jobs to the unemployed building schools, roads, and airports.

- **TVA** — the Tennessee Valley Authority, created in 1933 to develop the Tennessee River Valley through dams and electricity.

- **Court-packing plan** — Roosevelt's 1937 proposal to add a Supreme Court justice for every justice over seventy.

- **Good Neighbor Policy** — Roosevelt's 1933 policy renouncing armed intervention in Latin America.

- **Neutrality Acts** — the 1935-1937 laws that banned arms sales and loans to belligerents to keep the United States out of war.

- **Kellogg-Briand Pact** — the 1928 pact in which more than sixty nations renounced war as an instrument of national policy.

- **Washington Naval Conference** — the 1921-1922 conference that set battleship tonnage ratios for the major naval powers.
