Between 1890 and 1945, the United States went from a continental republic to the world's richest and most powerful nation. This lesson answers the question of how the Second World War finished that change: how the country mobilized, how it fought, and what kind of peace it built when the fighting stopped.

## From Pearl Harbor to total war

The road to war ran through Asia. Japan, short on oil and steel, invaded China in 1937 and moved into French Indochina in 1940. The United States answered with embargoes, cutting off scrap metal in 1940 and oil in 1941. Japan faced a choice: give up its conquests or seize the oil of Southeast Asia. On December 7, 1941, Japanese planes attacked the American naval base at Pearl Harbor, Hawaii, killing 2,403 Americans and wounding 1,178. Congress declared war the next day.

The country that had argued about staying out of war now argued about how to win it. Germany and Italy declared war on the United States three days later, and the war became a two-front fight. The United States, Britain, and the Soviet Union agreed to defeat Germany first, while holding Japan in the Pacific. The plan asked the country to build an army, a navy, and an air force almost from scratch, and to do it fast.

**Mobilization** is the organizing of a whole society for war, and the United States mobilized on a scale it had never tried. The War Production Board decided what factories would build, and in what order. The Office of Price Administration set prices and rationed goods, from gasoline to sugar. Taxes rose, and the government borrowed on a scale that dwarfed the New Deal. Unemployment, still near 15 percent in 1940, nearly vanished.

## The arsenal of democracy

Roosevelt had called the United States the **arsenal of democracy**, and the factories proved him right. Plants that had made cars and refrigerators turned to tanks and planes. American factories built 6,086 military aircraft in 1940 and 96,318 in 1944. American shipyards launched Liberty ships in days, and the country outbuilt every other power in the war. Production, more than any single battle, decided the war.

The war opened factory doors to women and Black workers who had been shut out before. About six million women entered the workforce, and the number of women in paid work rose from about 12 million in 1940 to more than 19 million in 1945. Black workers moved north and west to war plants in the **Second Great Migration**, and A. Philip Randolph's threatened march on Washington won Executive Order 8802 in 1941, which banned discrimination in defense industries.

Black Americans pressed the **Double V** campaign: victory over fascism abroad and victory over racism at home. The Pittsburgh Courier gave the campaign its name. Black soldiers served in segregated units, and Black workers won new jobs, but the war did not end segregation. The Double V showed that Black Americans would fight for the country and demand its promises at the same time.

Mobilization also took rights away. After Pearl Harbor, fear and prejudice fell on Japanese Americans. **Executive Order 9066**, signed on February 19, 1942, gave military commanders power to exclude people from West Coast military areas. About 120,000 people of Japanese descent, about two-thirds of them American citizens, were forced from their homes into **internment** in relocation centers in the interior West. In **Korematsu v. United States** in 1944, the Supreme Court upheld the removal. The camps stand as the war's betrayal of its own ideals.

## The war in the Pacific and Europe

In the Pacific, the United States recovered from Pearl Harbor faster than Japan expected. The Battle of Midway in June 1942 turned the war: Japan lost four aircraft carriers and more than 200 planes, while the United States lost one carrier. From Midway on, Japan fought on the defensive, unable to replace its losses. American forces then island-hopped across the Pacific, taking one island at a time toward Japan.

In Europe, the Soviet Union bore the heaviest fighting. German armies drove deep into Russia in 1941, and the Soviet victory at Stalingrad in early 1943 turned the eastern front. On June 6, 1944, **D-Day**, Allied forces landed in Normandy and opened the second front Stalin had long demanded. Paris was liberated in August, and by the spring of 1945 Allied armies were closing on Berlin from both sides.

The war's end came with a new weapon. The **Manhattan Project**, based at Los Alamos, New Mexico, built the first atomic bombs. The first test, at Trinity near Alamogordo, came on July 16, 1945. On August 6, an American plane dropped an atomic bomb on Hiroshima, and on August 9 a second bomb fell on Nagasaki. Japan surrendered, and the formal surrender was signed on September 2, 1945. Germany had already surrendered: its surrender was signed on May 7 and took effect on May 8, V-E Day.

The war's cost was enormous. More than 400,000 Americans died in the war, and tens of millions died worldwide, including about six million Jews murdered in the Holocaust. The United States emerged the world's richest and most powerful nation, its factories intact while Europe and Asia lay in ruins. The war had settled the argument of 1890: the United States was a global power, and it would stay one.

## Yalta, Potsdam, and the United Nations

The victors planned the peace before the war ended. At the **Yalta Conference** in February 1945, Roosevelt, Churchill, and Stalin agreed to divide Germany into occupation zones, promised free elections in the nations freed from German rule, and agreed to create the United Nations. Look at the photograph: Churchill, Roosevelt, and Stalin sit together on the patio of the Livadia Palace at Yalta on February 9, 1945, with their advisers standing behind them.

![Churchill, Roosevelt, and Stalin seated together at Yalta, February 1945](u7/yalta-big-three-1945.jpg)
Figure 1: Churchill, Roosevelt, and Stalin at Yalta, February 9, 1945. Public domain (U.S. Army Signal Corps).

Within months, the promises frayed. Soviet-backed governments took power in Eastern Europe, and the free elections never came. Roosevelt died in April 1945, and Harry Truman took his place. At the **Potsdam Conference** in July and August 1945, Truman and Stalin — joined by Britain's Clement Attlee, who replaced Winston Churchill midway through the conference after Churchill's party lost the British election — disagreed over Germany's future, with the Soviets taking heavy reparations from their zone while the Western Allies wanted Germany rebuilt enough to feed itself.

![Attlee, Truman, and Stalin seated together at Potsdam, July 1945](u7/potsdam-big-three-1945.jpg)
Figure 2: Attlee, Truman, and Stalin at Potsdam, July 1945. Public domain (U.S. Army Signal Corps).

The **United Nations** was the one plan that held. Its Charter was signed in San Francisco on June 26, 1945, and came into force on October 24, 1945. The United States, which had refused to join the League of Nations after World War I, became a founding member, with a permanent seat and a veto on the Security Council. The lesson of the 1930s was that staying out of world affairs had not kept the country safe, and this time the Senate agreed.

## Comparing the period

The period that began in 1890 ended with a government the founders of the period would hardly have recognized. In 1890 Washington collected tariffs and regulated railroads in limited ways. By 1945 it managed the economy, ran a safety net, and led alliances around the world. The change did not happen all at once: Progressives made the government a referee, the New Deal made it a provider, and the war made it a manager of everything.

The two wars show the pattern. In World War I, the government sold bonds, directed production, and jailed critics under the Espionage Act. In World War II, it did all of that on a larger scale, and it also moved whole populations: women into factories, Black workers into northern cities, and Japanese Americans into camps. Both wars expanded federal power and narrowed freedom, but the second war changed work, migration, and women's lives far more deeply.

One continuity ran through the whole period: fear of outsiders. The Red Scare, the immigration quotas of the 1920s, and wartime suspicion of immigrants and Japanese Americans each named a different enemy, but each used the same logic, that the nation was safer if it shut people out. The targets changed. The fear did not, and neither did the argument over whether a country built by immigrants should close its doors.

> Historians note that mobilization for World War II changed the American economy more in four years than the New Deal had in eight. Factories that had made cars and refrigerators turned to tanks and planes, and the federal government decided what they would build, in what order, and at what price. Unemployment, still near 15 percent in 1940, nearly vanished. At the same time, the war opened factory doors to women and Black workers who had been shut out before, even as the government forced Japanese Americans from their homes.

Source: Original secondary passage written for this question, summarizing the account in Barron's Premium 2027, Chapter 9, Topic 7.12, and 5 Steps to a 5 2024, Chapter 24.

a. Identify ONE way mobilization changed the American economy.

Model answer: Mobilization turned civilian factories to war production, so plants that had made cars and refrigerators began building tanks and planes. The federal government decided what the factories built and in what order, taking a direct hand in production.

b. Explain ONE way mobilization created a new opportunity for a group of Americans.

Model answer: The war opened factory jobs to women and Black workers who had been shut out of industrial work before. About six million women entered the workforce, and Black workers moved north and west to war plants that finally hired them.

c. Explain ONE way mobilization limited the rights of a group of Americans.

Model answer: The government forced about 120,000 Japanese Americans from their West Coast homes into relocation centers. Most lost their homes and property, and the Supreme Court upheld the removal in Korematsu v. United States in 1944.

How to think about it: part a asks for one economic change, so name the turn from civilian goods to war production. Part b asks for one new opportunity, so name the factory jobs that opened to women or Black workers. Part c asks for one limit on rights, so name the removal of Japanese Americans.

How to think about it: part a asks how Midway changed the Pacific war, so name Japan's move to the defensive. Part b asks why the United States could replace its losses, so name American industrial size. Part c asks for one other turning point, so name D-Day and what it opened.

## Key terms

- **Mobilization** — the organizing of a whole society for war.

- **Arsenal of democracy** — Roosevelt's name for the United States as the supplier of arms and goods for the fight against fascism.

- **Executive Order 9066** — the February 1942 order that gave military commanders power to exclude people from West Coast military areas.

- **Internment** — the forced removal of about 120,000 people of Japanese descent into relocation centers during World War II.

- **Korematsu v. United States** — the 1944 Supreme Court decision that upheld the removal of Japanese Americans.

- **Double V** — the campaign for victory over fascism abroad and victory over racism at home.

- **Second Great Migration** — the movement of Black workers north and west to war plants during World War II.

- **D-Day** — the Allied landings in Normandy on June 6, 1944, which opened the second front in Western Europe.

- **Manhattan Project** — the wartime program, based at Los Alamos, that built the first atomic bombs.

- **Yalta Conference** — the February 1945 meeting where Roosevelt, Churchill, and Stalin planned the postwar world.

- **Potsdam Conference** — the July-August 1945 meeting where Truman, Stalin, and Clement Attlee — who replaced Winston Churchill midway through — disagreed over Germany's future.

- **United Nations** — the international organization founded in 1945, with the United States as a founding member.
