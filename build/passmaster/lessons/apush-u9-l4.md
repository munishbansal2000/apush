How did immigration remake the American population after 1965, and why did it become such a sharp argument? This lesson answers that question. A law meant to keep immigration small changed who came, a bargain meant to settle the issue failed, and the argument over who belongs has run ever since.

## The 1965 law and its surprise

For most of the twentieth century, American immigration law ran on **national-origins quotas**, limits that favored immigrants from northern and western Europe and kept the numbers from Asia, Africa, and Latin America small. The quotas, set in the 1920s, were built on the idea that some newcomers fit in more easily than others.

The **Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965** ended the quotas. In their place, it set annual limits and gave priority to **family reunification**, letting new citizens sponsor their relatives, and to skilled workers. Its authors expected the flow to stay mostly European. They were wrong.

Look at the chart: the **foreign-born** share of the population rose from 4.7 percent in 1970 to 13.6 percent in 2020.

![Foreign-born share of the population, 1970–2020](pt01/fig6-foreign-born-1970-2020.jpg)
Figure 1: The foreign-born share of the population rose from 4.7 percent in 1970 to 13.6 percent in 2020. Chart: U.S. Census Bureau.

The numbers tell the story: the foreign-born population rose from about 10 million in 1970 to 31 million in 2000, and just over half of the foreign-born had been born in Latin America. Immigration, once a small share of population growth, now accounted for about a third of it. The new arrivals changed the country's languages, foods, and faiths, and they changed its politics too.

## The 1986 bargain

By the 1980s, a new problem had grown: **unauthorized immigration**, people living in the United States without legal status. Congress answered with the **Immigration Reform and Control Act** of 1986, also called the Simpson-Mazzoli Act after its sponsors in the Senate and the House. It was a bargain: legal status for longtime residents, and penalties for employers who hired workers without legal papers.

President Ronald Reagan signed the law on November 6, 1986, in the Roosevelt Room of the White House, with Vice President George H. W. Bush standing behind him. Look at the photograph: Reagan sits at the desk, signing, while Vice President Bush stands behind him.

![President Reagan signs the Immigration Reform and Control Act, November 6, 1986](u9/reagan-signs-irca-1986.jpg)
Figure 2: President Reagan signs the Immigration Reform and Control Act on November 6, 1986, with Vice President Bush behind him. Public domain (U.S. federal government work).

Reagan called the law the most far-reaching reform of immigration law since 1952, and he warned that it would work only if its enforcement provisions were carried out. About 2.7 million people gained legal status — about 1.6 million residents since before 1982, and about 1.1 million farmworkers under a separate program for seasonal agricultural work. The bargain was meant to settle the issue once and for all.

It did not. Employer penalties were rarely enforced, and jobs remained available. The unauthorized population grew from about 3 million in 1986 to more than 12 million by 2007. The law had settled the status of millions, but it had not stopped the flow that made the law necessary.

## The border hardens

As unauthorized crossings rose in the 1990s and 2000s, Congress and presidents pushed to harden the border. Fences went up first in busy urban areas like San Diego and Tijuana, to push crossings into harder terrain. The idea was that a harder crossing would mean fewer crossings.

Look at the photograph: a tall metal fence runs across the beach near Tijuana and out into the Pacific Ocean, with people gathered on the sand on both sides. The fence was photographed in October 2006, from the Mexican side.

![A metal fence at the United States-Mexico border near Tijuana, 2006](u9/border-fence-playas-tijuana-2006.jpg)
Figure 3: A metal fence runs across the beach near Tijuana and out into the Pacific Ocean, October 2006. Public domain (released by the author).

The numbers suggest the fences changed the flow. **Apprehensions**, the count of people stopped at the southwest border, fell from over 1.6 million in 2000 to about 304,000 in 2017, though they rose again by 2019. But apprehensions count stops, not people: some crossers are never caught, and some are caught more than once. A fall in apprehensions does not prove a fall in crossings.

Crossings also changed. In 2000, most of those stopped were single Mexican adults looking for work. By 2019, more than half of those arriving were Central American families seeking asylum, protection for people fleeing danger at home. And because the fences pushed crossings into remote deserts, the journey grew more dangerous, even as the number of stops fell.

## A changing electorate

Immigration changed who votes. As **Hispanic** and Asian populations grew, both parties began competing for their votes in national elections. The Hispanic share of the population nearly tripled between 1980 and 2020, and by the 2000 census Hispanics had outnumbered African Americans as the largest minority group.

The growth also shifted political power. House seats and Electoral College votes moved toward the South and West, where many newcomers settled, and toward the **Sun Belt**, the warm-weather states whose growth came from immigration and from Americans moving south and west for jobs and retirement.

The census of 2020 showed the shift in seats: Texas gained two House seats, and North Carolina, Florida, Montana, Oregon, and Colorado each gained one. California lost a seat for the first time. Every seat gained in the South and West was a seat lost somewhere else, and immigration was part of why the map moved.

The politics of immigration turned sharpest in California in 1994, when voters passed **Proposition 187** by 59 to 41 percent. The measure barred people without legal status from public schools and public health care. Supporters said unauthorized immigration strained the state’s schools and services, and that denying services would discourage new arrivals and cut public costs. A federal judge later ruled most of it unconstitutional, in 1997, but the campaign had already mobilized Latino voters toward the Democratic Party.

## Deadlock and executive action

No broad reform law has passed since 1986. Congress has debated immigration for decades, but the two parties have not agreed on a package that pairs enforcement with a path to legal status. Each side has feared that a compromise would cost it more than a stalemate.

In the gap left by Congress, presidents have acted on their own. The **DREAM Act**, a bill that would have given a path to citizenship to young people brought to the United States as children, failed in Congress. In 2012, President Barack Obama acted by executive action, creating **DACA**, Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals. It offered protection from deportation and work permits, but no path to citizenship, and a later president could undo it.

The argument kept running into elections. In 2016, Donald Trump's campaign promised a wall along the southern border and a harder line on immigration, and the promise helped carry him to the White House. The wall became a symbol: to supporters it meant control, to critics it meant closing the door.

The pattern is an old one. Each new wave of arrivals has raised the same question: whether newcomers fit in, and on what terms. The answer has never been settled once and for all. It has been argued out, wave by wave, in Congress, in the courts, and at the border itself.

## Key terms

- **Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965** — the law that ended the national-origins quota system.

- **national-origins quotas** — limits that favored immigrants from northern and western Europe, set in the 1920s.

- **family reunification** — priority in immigration law for relatives of citizens and residents.

- **Immigration Reform and Control Act** — the 1986 law that paired legalization with employer penalties.

- **unauthorized immigration** — living in the United States without legal status.

- **DACA** — Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals, the 2012 executive action protecting young people brought as children from deportation.

- **DREAM Act** — the failed bill that would have given a path to citizenship to young people brought as children.

- **Proposition 187** — the 1994 California measure barring people without legal status from public schools and health care.

- **Sun Belt** — the warm-weather states of the South and West that gained population and power.

- **apprehensions** — the count of people stopped at the border.

- **foreign-born** — people living in the United States who were born in another country.

- **Hispanic** — people of Spanish-speaking origin, the largest minority group by the 2000 census.
