What happened when Europeans, Native peoples, and Africans lived side by side in the Americas? This lesson answers that question. The answer is a world remade by missions and revolts, by silver that made Spain poorer, and by a disease that decided conquests before the battles were fought.

## Two ways to meet a continent

Spain and France met Native peoples in very different ways, and the difference came from what each wanted. Spain wanted silver and souls, and it built an empire of mines, **missions**, and coerced labor. France wanted furs, and the **fur trade** depended on Native hunters who knew the land. Cooperation paid better than conquest, so the French traded, allied, and intermarried, while the Spanish conquered, converted, and commanded.

The French model began early. French fishermen and traders worked the Atlantic coast in the 1500s, and Jacques Cartier explored the St. Lawrence in the 1530s. Few French settlers came, and most were single men, some of whom married Native women. France did not found a permanent settlement until Quebec in 1608. French Jesuit priests arrived in 1611 to convert Native peoples to Catholicism, but they were more likely to spread **smallpox** than to win many converts.

The English were different again. They sent whole families, not single traders, and they came to farm, not to trade furs or dig silver. English intermarriage with Native peoples and Africans was rare, so no new mixed peoples emerged in their colonies, and social classes stayed rigid. The Spanish, by contrast, were mostly men at first: Spanish men far outnumbered Spanish women, and the children of Spanish fathers and Native mothers, the **mestizos**, became a large part of colonial society.

Do not mistake the differences for a difference in aim. All three crowns wanted wealth, and all three took Native land. The French were gentler because their business needed Native partners, not because their aims were kinder. When a question asks how Spanish and French interactions differed, name the economics first, then the behavior: silver and coercion on one side, fur trade and alliance on the other, and the same hunger for wealth underneath.

## Missions, revolt, and terror

Spain held its northern frontier with missions, not armies. In the 1500s, Spain built four missions in Florida near St. Augustine, and Franciscan missionaries worked to bring nearby Native peoples into the mission system, teaching Christianity and Spanish ways. Missions let the crown hold Florida with few soldiers, since priests and converts did the work of control. The Guale people lived near one of these missions, and the mission pressed them to live under Spanish priests and follow Christian teaching.

The Guale answered with revolt. In 1597, as missionaries pressed the Guale into the mission system, **Juanillo's Revolt** broke out, and several missionaries were killed. The revolt shows that Native peoples resisted Spanish control violently when it threatened their autonomy. It also shows the limit of the mission system: priests could teach, but they could not hold a people who refused to be held.

Farther west, the Spanish used terror instead of teaching. In 1598, Juan de Oñate led settlers into New Mexico, and his nephew was among fifteen Spaniards killed in an attack on the **Acoma** pueblo. The retaliation came in January 1599: more than eight hundred Acoma people were killed, as many as eighty men had a foot cut off, and about five hundred were enslaved. The Spanish were few, and they used visible cruelty to make a small force look large. Terror was the policy, not an accident.

Do not carry this story past 1607. The Pueblo Revolt of 1680, when the Pueblo peoples drove the Spanish out of New Mexico for twelve years, belongs to a later period. It is not evidence for this one. The Acoma attack is the Period 1 evidence for Spanish terror as control, and it is enough.

## A new people, and a new picture

Contact made new peoples. The mestizos, children of Spanish fathers and Native mothers, grew into a large part of colonial society, about four or five percent by the later counts. They were neither Spanish nor Indian in the law's eyes, and they lived between the two republics. Their existence shows what the casta system tried to deny: that the peoples of the Americas were mixing, whatever the law said.

The Tlaxcalans left a picture of the meeting from their side. The **Lienzo de Tlaxcala** is a pictorial history made by Tlaxcalan artists in the 1550s, and the copy below was drawn in 1773. It shows Tlaxcalan lords and Spaniards together at the foot of a cross, with Cortés among them. The Tlaxcalans were the allies who helped Cortés take Tenochtitlan, and their picture remembers the conquest as a joint act, not a Spanish one alone.

## Two pictures of the conquest

Cortés's own letter tells the same meeting from the other side. In his Second Letter to Charles V, dated October 30, 1520, he described casting the Aztec idols down the temple steps and setting up images of the Virgin and the saints. Montezuma remonstrated, warning that the people would rise against him if his gods were ill-treated. Cortés answered that the idols were the work of human hands, and that there was but one God. Read the passage for its purpose: it was written to win the king's support.

> I precipitated [the idols] from their pedestals, and cast them down the steps of the temple, purifying the chapels in which they had stood, as they were all polluted with human blood, shed in the sacrifices. In the places of these I put images of our Lady and the Saints, which excited not a little feeling in Muteczuma and the inhabitants, who at first remonstrated, declaring that if my proceedings were known throughout the country, the people would rise against me; for they believed that their idols bestowed on them all temporal good, and if they permitted them to be ill-treated, they would be angry and withhold their gifts.

Figure 2: Hernando Cortés on casting down the Aztec idols, Second Letter to Charles V, 30 October 1520. Public domain.

The two pictures do not cancel each other out. The Lienzo shows Native allies who chose a side; the letter shows a conqueror who justified his war as a mission. Both are true at once, and the exam rewards the student who can hold both: the conquest was a Native alliance and a Spanish imposition, in the same act.

## The silver paradox

American silver flowed into Spain through the 1500s, yet the crown grew poorer. Taxes rose more than fivefold to pay for armies and wars, and the crown borrowed heavily from European bankers. At the same time, prices rose across Europe as the supply of silver money grew. The silver arrived in Seville and left again, spent on soldiers, ships, and interest.

The table below shows the population of Hispaniola, the island Columbus first settled: over one million people in 1492, about five hundred by the 1540s. Across the Americas, between 50 and 90 percent of Native peoples died between 1500 and 1650. The wealth of the empire was built on a population that was disappearing.

| Year | Native population of Hispaniola |
|---|---|
| 1492 | Over 1,000,000 |
| 1540s | About 500 |

Table 1: The Native population of Hispaniola, 1492 to the 1540s.

Historians argue about how far to push the argument. The silver funded an arms race, as Spain's large army forced its rivals to multiply their own forces, and it set off a long wave of inflation across Western Europe. Some historians go further and call the silver the most lasting effect of the conquest on Europe. That claim belongs to the historian who made it, not to the numbers in the table.

Las Casas's account of Spanish cruelty, written in 1542, was translated into English, Dutch, and German and printed by Spain's Protestant rivals with violent engravings. The image of Spain as uniquely cruel that grew from those printings is called the **Black Legend**, a term first used by a Spanish historian in 1914. The Black Legend is not a lie and not the whole truth: Spanish cruelty was real, and so was the printing campaign that sold it.

## Disease decided the conquest

The Europeans had steel and horses, and those mattered, but the decisive factor, by a wide margin, was disease. Native peoples had never been exposed to European microbes and had never developed immunities to them. Smallpox reached Tenochtitlan in 1520, before the siege, and killed defenders who had no resistance, including the emperor Cuitláhuac.

The image below comes from Book 12 of the **Florentine Codex**, compiled around 1585 by Bernardino de Sahagún with Nahua scholars. Five panels show the sick: one lies covered in spots while another speaks over him, and four more lie still, wrapped in blankets. The drawing is a Nahua record of a Nahua catastrophe, made with a Spanish friar's help. It is the closest thing we have to the view from inside the epidemic.

![Smallpox victims in Tenochtitlan](u1/5s24-exam1-mcq-36.jpg)
Figure 3: Smallpox victims in Tenochtitlan. Florentine Codex, Book 12, about 1585. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

Disease led, but it did not work alone. War, slavery, and broken food systems helped the dying along. Steel, horses, and Native allies mattered too: without the Tlaxcalans, Cortés had no army, and without steel and horses, his small force could not have fought. When a question asks whether disease was the most significant cause of Spanish success, lead with disease, then name the others. A one-cause answer is half an answer.

The consequences ran both ways, and they are still running. Europe got new foods that fed more people, and silver that paid for armies. The Americas got wheat, sugar, and the horse, and paid in people: whole towns emptied, food systems broke, and societies lost their independence. The exchange made the modern world, and its costs were not shared equally. That uneven ledger is the consequence to carry out of Period 1.

## Worked items

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0025 (Topic 1.6)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage: in the 1500s, Spain built four missions in Florida near St. Augustine, and Franciscan missionaries worked to bring nearby Native peoples into the mission system, teaching Christianity and Spanish ways. The Guale people lived near one of these missions, and in 1597, as missionaries pressed the Guale into the mission system, a revolt known as Juanillo's Revolt broke out, and several missionaries were killed.

a. Identify one reason Spain built missions in Florida.

Model answer: Spain built missions to bring Native peoples into the mission system, teaching Christianity and Spanish ways. Missions let the Crown hold Florida with few soldiers, since priests and converts did the work of control.

b. Explain one way the mission system changed life for the Guale people.

Model answer: The mission system pressed the Guale to live under Spanish priests and follow Christian teaching. It replaced their own religious practices and put their daily life under Spanish control.

c. Explain one Native response to the mission system in Florida.

Model answer: The Guale answered mission pressure with Juanillo's Revolt in 1597, killing several missionaries. The revolt shows that Native peoples resisted Spanish control violently when it threatened their autonomy.

How to think about it: part a asks for one reason, so name the mission's work: teaching Christianity and Spanish ways while holding Florida with few soldiers. Part b asks for a change, so say what the mission replaced and what it put under Spanish control. Part c asks for a response, so name the revolt, the year, and what it shows about resistance.

### Worked item 2 — apush-saq-0031 (Topic 1.7)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage: American silver flowed into Spain through the 1500s, yet the crown grew poorer rather than richer. Taxes rose more than fivefold to pay for armies and wars, and the crown borrowed heavily from European bankers. At the same time, prices rose across Europe as the supply of silver money grew.

a. Identify one effect of American silver on Spain's economy.

Model answer: American silver drove prices up in Spain and across Europe. More silver money chased the same goods, so prices rose.

b. Explain why the silver did not make Spain richer.

Model answer: The crown spent the silver on armies, wars, and interest payments rather than investing it in farms or workshops. A tiny landowning class saw no reason to modernize, so the wealth passed through Spain instead of building its economy.

c. Explain one effect of American silver on Europe beyond Spain.

Model answer: Silver set off a long wave of inflation across Western Europe, raising prices everywhere the money circulated. It also funded an arms race, as Spain's large army forced its rivals to multiply their own forces.

How to think about it: part a asks for one effect, so name the price rise and say why more money meant higher prices. Part b asks why the silver did not enrich Spain, so follow the money: armies, wars, and interest, not farms or workshops. Part c asks for an effect beyond Spain, so name the inflation across Western Europe and the arms race it funded.

### Worked item 3 — apush-saq-0032 (Topic 1.7)

The stimulus is a short passage from Christopher Columbus's Journal of the First Voyage, 12 October 1492, as copied by Bartolomé de Las Casas: "They do not bear arms, and do not know them, for I showed them a sword, they took it by the edge and cut themselves out of ignorance. They have no iron. Their spears are made of cane. They would make fine servants. With fifty men we could subjugate them all and make them do whatever we want."

a. Identify Columbus's attitude toward the Taíno people he met.

Model answer: Columbus saw the Taíno as gentle, unarmed people who would make fine servants. He believed a small force of fifty men could subjugate them all.

b. Explain what the passage shows about Spanish aims in the Americas.

Model answer: The passage shows that from the first landing, the Spanish aimed to rule Native peoples and put them to work. Columbus judged the Taíno by their usefulness as subjects and servants, not as equals.

c. Explain one limitation of this journal entry as evidence about the voyage.

Model answer: The entry survives only in a copy made by Las Casas decades later, so the words may not be exactly Columbus's. Columbus was also writing to justify his voyage to the crown, so he had reason to stress how easily the new lands could be ruled.

How to think about it: part a asks for an attitude, so take it from his own words: gentle, unarmed, fine servants, fifty men to subjugate them. Part b asks what the passage shows about Spanish aims, so read the judgment underneath: rule and put to work, from the first landing. Part c asks for a limit, so name both: the copy by Las Casas, and the purpose of justifying the voyage to the crown.

## Key terms

- **missions** — Spanish religious settlements that taught Christianity and Spanish ways while holding territory with few soldiers.

- **Juanillo's Revolt** — the Guale revolt of 1597 against the Florida mission system, in which several missionaries were killed.

- **Acoma** — the pueblo attacked by the Spanish in 1599, after fifteen Spaniards were killed there in 1598.

- **mestizos** — children of Spaniards and Native people.

- **Lienzo de Tlaxcala** — the Tlaxcalan pictorial history of the conquest, made in the 1550s and copied in 1773.

- **Black Legend** — the image of Spain as uniquely cruel, spread by its Protestant rivals.

- **Florentine Codex** — the history of central Mexico compiled by Bernardino de Sahagún with Nahua scholars in the 1500s.

- **smallpox** — the Old World disease that killed Native peoples who had no immunity to it.

- **fur trade** — the French trade in furs, which depended on Native hunters and rewarded cooperation over conquest.
