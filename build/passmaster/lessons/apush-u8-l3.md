By 1960 the United States had spent fifteen years containing communism, and the next fifteen would test that policy harder than the first. The tests came in Vietnam, where a war grew beyond control, and at home, where President Johnson tried to build a Great Society while fighting that war. This lesson answers how America became a world power, why Vietnam broke its confidence, and what the Great Society built.

## America takes up world power

The United States became a world power by taking on duties it had always refused. Before the Second World War, it had kept out of peacetime alliances and let Europe settle its own wars. After 1945, it did the opposite: it promised aid to Greece and Turkey, sent about $13 billion to rebuild Western Europe through the **Marshall Plan**, and joined **NATO**, its first peacetime alliance. Power now meant permanent responsibility.

Korea showed the limits of that power. The photograph shows American Marines in the snow during the winter fighting, resting beside a jeep on a snowy road. The war was fought under the United Nations flag, and President Truman called it a police action, not a war. When General MacArthur pressed to widen the fighting into China, Truman removed him from command in April 1951. The war ended in July 1953 with an armistice, not a peace treaty, and the two Koreas remain divided.

![Marines resting in snow beside a jeep](u8/pr25e-ch12-q06.jpg)
Figure 1: American Marines in the snow during the Korean War. Public domain (U.S. Marine Corps photograph).

Korea taught three lessons that stayed with American leaders. First, the United States would fight to hold the line, but it would not risk a wider war with China or the Soviet Union. Second, presidents could send troops without asking Congress for a declaration of war. Third, a limited war could still cost more than fifty thousand American lives and end in a stalemate. Each of these lessons would matter again in Vietnam.

## Eisenhower's Cold War

President Eisenhower wanted a cheaper Cold War. His New Look policy cut the size of the army and relied instead on nuclear weapons. Secretary of State John Foster Dulles called the approach **massive retaliation**: any Communist aggression could meet an American nuclear reply. Dulles also practiced **brinkmanship**, pushing a crisis to the edge of war to force the other side to back down. The threat was meant to deter war, but it meant every crisis could become a nuclear one.

Where armies were too costly, Eisenhower used the Central Intelligence Agency. In 1953 the CIA helped overthrow Prime Minister Mossadegh of Iran, who had taken control of Iran's oil. In 1954 it helped overthrow President Arbenz of Guatemala, whose land reform threatened the United Fruit Company. Both operations succeeded at first, but both planted resentment that outlived them.

The limits of the new policy showed in 1956, when Britain, France, and Israel attacked Egypt over the Suez Canal. Eisenhower refused to back his own allies and forced them to withdraw, because he would not defend an old-style colonial war. The next year he announced the Eisenhower Doctrine, promising American help to any Middle Eastern country threatened by communism. In July 1958 he sent Marines to Lebanon to back its pro-Western government. The Cold War now reached every region of the world.

Then the rivalry moved upward. In October 1957 the Soviet Union launched Sputnik, and the United States answered by creating NASA in 1958 and a military research agency, ARPA, the same year. In May 1960 the Soviets shot down an American U-2 spy plane, and Eisenhower admitted the spying. When he left office in January 1961, he warned of the military-industrial complex, and of a world where both sides could destroy each other many times over.

## Vietnam: from advisers to war

Vietnam began as a French war. France had ruled Vietnam as a colony, and after 1945 it fought to keep it against Ho Chi Minh's independence movement. In 1954 the French lost at Dien Bien Phu, and the Geneva Accords divided Vietnam at the 17th parallel, with Ho's Communists in the north and a non-Communist government in the south. The accords promised elections to reunite the country, but the elections never happened.

President Eisenhower explained the stakes with the **domino theory**: if one country fell to communism, its neighbors would fall after it, like a row of dominoes. He sent advisers and aid to Ngo Dinh Diem, the South Vietnamese leader, and President Kennedy sent more advisers still. By 1963 the United States had about sixteen thousand advisers in Vietnam, and Diem's government was losing ground to Communist guerrillas in the south.

In November 1963, Diem was killed in a coup that American officials had encouraged, and three weeks later Kennedy was assassinated in Dallas. President Johnson inherited the war and, after reported attacks on American ships in the Gulf of Tonkin in August 1964, asked Congress for the **Gulf of Tonkin Resolution**, which gave him broad power to use force. In 1965 he sent combat troops, and by the end of that year about 184,000 American troops were in Vietnam.

The war had no front lines. Communist forces fought as guerrillas, hiding among villagers and striking where American troops were weakest. American soldiers could win every battle and still lose the countryside. The draft fell hardest on working-class and poor young men, who could not claim the student deferments that kept many college men out. In 1971, the 26th Amendment lowered the voting age to eighteen, so that young men old enough to fight were old enough to vote.

## Tet and the turn against the war

In January 1968, Communist forces struck cities across South Vietnam in the **Tet Offensive**, attacking during the lunar new year holiday. The photograph shows Cholon, the Chinese district of Saigon, after the fighting: burned-out buildings and civilians picking through the rubble. American and South Vietnamese forces won the battles, but the scale of the attack shocked Americans at home, who had been told the enemy was beaten.

![Burned-out buildings and civilians in Cholon, 1968](u8/5s24-ch27-mcq-02.jpg)
Figure 2: Cholon, Saigon, after the Tet Offensive, 1968. Public domain (U.S. Army photograph).

Tet turned American opinion against the war. In March 1968, President Johnson announced he would not seek reelection, and peace talks began in Paris. News of the My Lai massacre, where American soldiers killed hundreds of unarmed villagers in March 1968, deepened the anger. The antiwar movement grew on campuses and in the streets, and in 1971 the Pentagon Papers showed that the government had misled the public about the war for years.

President Nixon promised to end the war, and his plan was **Vietnamization**: training South Vietnamese forces to fight while American troops came home. But he also widened the war, bombing Cambodia in 1970, and protests erupted. At Kent State University on May 4, 1970, National Guardsmen killed four students during a protest. The Paris Accords of January 1973 ended American combat, and in April 1975 Saigon fell to the North. The war was over, and the United States had lost.

Congress moved to limit presidential war-making. The **War Powers Resolution**, passed over Nixon's veto in November 1973, required the president to report to Congress within forty-eight hours of sending troops into combat and to withdraw them after sixty days unless Congress approved. It was the first serious attempt to reclaim the war power Congress had let slip since Korea.

## The Great Society

While the war grew, Johnson promised a second front at home. At the University of Michigan in May 1964, he called for a **Great Society**, a country without poverty, racial injustice, or decaying cities. He asked Congress for a war on poverty, and it answered with the Economic Opportunity Act, which created the Job Corps, VISTA, and Head Start preschools for poor children. Johnson's 1964 landslide, with 61.1 percent of the popular vote, gave the Democrats large majorities in Congress, so stalled bills could finally pass.

The **Great Society** reached into health care and schools. In July 1965, Johnson signed **Medicare** and **Medicaid** into law, with former President Truman at his side as the first Medicare beneficiary. By July 1, 1966, more than 19 million older Americans had enrolled in Medicare. The Elementary and Secondary Education Act sent federal money to poor school districts for the first time.

![Johnson signing Medicare with Truman beside him, 1965](u8/pr25e-test1-q52.jpg)
Figure 3: Johnson signs Medicare into law, July 30, 1965. Public domain (U.S. government photograph).

The Great Society also changed who could come to America. The Immigration Act of 1965 ended the national-origins quotas that had favored northern Europe since the 1920s, setting caps of 170,000 immigrants a year from the Eastern Hemisphere and 120,000 from the Western Hemisphere. The authors expected little change, but it opened the country to Asia, Africa, and Latin America, and the population grew more diverse.

Poverty fell while the money flowed: the poverty rate dropped from 22.4 percent in 1959 to 11.1 percent in 1973. But it had limits. Vietnam drained money and attention, and many programs promised more than they delivered. Conservatives argued that federal programs made the poor dependent, while liberals argued that funding was never enough.

> After World War II, much of Western Europe lay in ruins, with factories idle, farms broken, and people short of food and fuel. American leaders feared that hunger and unemployment would push voters toward Communist parties, especially in France and Italy. The Marshall Plan, passed by Congress in 1948, sent about 13 billion dollars of American food, fuel, machinery, and loans to sixteen Western European countries over four years. The aid helped factories reopen and trade revive, and the countries that received it stayed outside the Soviet orbit.

a. Identify one aim of the Marshall Plan stated or implied in the passage.

Model answer: The passage says the Marshall Plan aimed to rebuild Western Europe's economies. It sent food, fuel, machinery, and loans so factories could reopen and trade could revive.

b. Explain one reason American leaders believed economic aid would help contain communism.

Model answer: The Marshall Plan made the United States the economic anchor of Western Europe. The countries that took American aid stayed outside the Soviet orbit and tied their recovery to American support.

How to think about it: part a asks what the Marshall Plan did, so name the aid to rebuild Western Europe. Part b asks why the United States offered it, so use the fear that poverty would feed communism. Part c asks how the plan served containment, so connect recovery to resistance.

## Key terms

- **Marshall Plan** — the American program that sent about $13 billion in aid to rebuild Western Europe between 1948 and 1951.

- **NATO** — the North Atlantic Treaty Organization, the peacetime military alliance formed in April 1949, pledging that an attack on one member was an attack on all.

- **Domino theory** — the belief that if one country fell to communism, its neighbors would fall after it, like a row of dominoes.

- **Massive retaliation** — the Eisenhower-era promise of a nuclear reply to Communist aggression, at places and with means of American choosing.

- **Brinkmanship** — the practice of pushing a crisis to the edge of war to force the other side to back down.

- **Gulf of Tonkin Resolution** — the 1964 congressional resolution that gave President Johnson broad power to use force in Vietnam.

- **Tet Offensive** — the Communist attacks on cities across South Vietnam in January 1968, a military defeat and a political victory.

- **Vietnamization** — President Nixon's plan to train South Vietnamese forces to fight while American troops came home.

- **War Powers Resolution** — the 1973 law, passed over Nixon's veto, that limited how long a president could send troops into combat without Congress.

- **Great Society** — President Johnson's program to end poverty and racial injustice, carried out through the War on Poverty, Medicare, Medicaid, and federal aid to schools.

- **Medicare** — the 1965 federal health insurance program for Americans aged sixty-five and older.

- **Medicaid** — the 1965 federal-state program that paid health care costs for poor Americans.
