Slavery was over, but nobody agreed on what freedom should mean. This lesson follows the fight over Reconstruction, why it failed, and what survived it.

## Three plans for reunion

The war ended slavery, but nobody agreed on what freedom should mean. The question was whether the South would be rebuilt on the old terms or the new ones, and who would decide: the president or Congress. Three plans competed.

Emancipation came in two steps. Lincoln’s Emancipation Proclamation of January 1, 1863, freed enslaved people only in Confederate-held territory, as a war measure. The Thirteenth Amendment, ratified in 1865, abolished slavery everywhere in the United States, permanently.

Lincoln's plan was quick and lenient. Under his Ten Percent Plan of 1863, a state could return when 10 percent of its voters swore loyalty and accepted emancipation. He wanted the South back fast, with little punishment.

Congress wanted more. The **Wade-Davis Bill** of 1864 demanded a majority oath and barred Confederate leaders from office, and Lincoln pocket-vetoed it. The fight over who set the terms began before Lincoln died.

Andrew Johnson, who became president after Lincoln's assassination, kept the lenient course. He pardoned most former Confederates, accepted new Southern governments, and let them write **Black Codes**, laws that restricted freedpeople's movement, work, and rights, binding them to the land by vagrancy laws and labor contracts.

The Black Codes showed that freedom without protection meant little. Freedpeople had left slavery but could not own land, vote, or testify against white men in court. Congress, returning in December 1865, refused to seat the Southern delegations. **Reconstruction**, the rebuilding of the South and the nation after the war, would now be Congress's work.

The nation had changed since 1844. Then it held 26 states, slavery legal in 13, and nearly 3 million enslaved. By 1877, 38 states, the Thirteenth Amendment had abolished slavery, and transcontinental railroad since 1869 and telegraph since 1861 joined the coasts.

## Congress takes charge

The Radical Republicans, led in the House by Thaddeus Stevens and in the Senate by Charles Sumner, pushed Congress to act. They created the Freedmen’s Bureau in March 1865 to give freedpeople food, clothing, and shelter, open schools, and help them sign labor contracts. It was the first large federal agency for ordinary people starting new lives.

Johnson vetoed the Bureau's extension and the Civil Rights Act of 1866, and Congress overrode both vetoes, the first time Congress had overridden a presidential veto on major legislation. The Civil Rights Act declared freedpeople citizens, and the **Fourteenth Amendment**, ratified in 1868, wrote citizenship and equal protection into the Constitution.

The Reconstruction Acts of 1867 divided the South into five military districts, required new state constitutions with Black suffrage, and made ratification of the Fourteenth Amendment the price of readmission. Johnson fought them, and in 1868 the House impeached him for violating the Tenure of Office Act. Look at the engraving. Davis’s "The Senate as a court of impeachment" shows the trial in the Senate chamber.

The Senate fell one vote short, and Johnson finished his term. But the amendment stood, and in 1870 the **Fifteenth Amendment** was ratified, barring states from denying the vote because of race. Look at the lithograph. "The Fifteenth Amendment. Celebrated May 19th, 1870" shows a parade and portraits of Grant, Colfax, and Lincoln, with vignettes of a Black schoolroom, a wedding, voting, a church, and Black legislators.

## Freedom without land

The vote and the amendments did not come with land. "Forty acres and a mule" was a promise General Sherman had made in 1865, but Johnson returned most confiscated land to its former owners, and Congress never gave freedpeople farms. Freedom came without economic independence.

Most freedpeople became **sharecroppers**, farming white-owned land for a share of the crop and borrowing seed and supplies from the landowner. Debt tied them to the land, and the crop lien system kept most freed families poor. Some bought land, opened shops, and built schools, but most stayed on the farms they had worked as slaves.

Black Southerners used the opening to vote, hold office, and build institutions. About 2,000 Black men held office during Reconstruction, including 16 in Congress, and Hiram Revels of Mississippi took a seat in the Senate in 1870. Look at the portrait. Blanche Bruce, photographed by Mathew Brady's studio, served in the Senate from 1875 to 1881, the only Black senator to serve a full term in the nineteenth century.

![Blanche K. Bruce, portrait, Brady-Handy collection](u5/5s24-ch16-mcq-06.jpg)
Figure 3: Blanche K. Bruce, senator from Mississippi, 1875–1881. Public domain.

The institutions mattered most. Black churches became centers of community life and politics. Black schools, including Howard and Fisk Universities, trained teachers and leaders. Southern whites who cooperated with Reconstruction were called scalawags, and Northern newcomers carpetbaggers, by their enemies, but both helped run the new governments.

Lincoln had named that promise at Gettysburg. His address of November 19, 1863, re-dedicated the nation to the Declaration’s promise that ‘all men are created equal’ — the idea the founders had left unapplied to enslaved people.

## Why Reconstruction failed

White Southerners fought back with terror. The **Ku Klux Klan**, founded in 1866, beat and killed Black voters and white Republicans. In Colfax, Louisiana, in 1873, a white militia murdered more than 100 Black men, and the Supreme Court in United States v. Cruikshank (1876) ruled that private violence was a state matter, beyond federal reach. The Enforcement Acts of 1870–71 had let federal authorities prosecute Klan violence until Cruikshank narrowed that power.

Look at the cartoon. Thomas Nast’s Harper’s Weekly cartoon of 1874, "The Union as it was / The lost cause, worse than slavery," shows a White League man and a Klansman flanking a cowering Black family, with a lynched man and a burning schoolhouse behind them. Nast, a longtime supporter of Reconstruction, was now showing what it had become.

![The Union as it was / The lost cause, worse than slavery, Thomas Nast, 1874](u5/5s24-exam2-mcq-17.jpg)
Figure 4: Nast's cartoon, October 24, 1874. A White League man and a Klansman menace a Black family. Public domain.

The Court narrowed the amendments. In the Slaughter-House Cases (1873) it read the Fourteenth Amendment to cover only federal rights; in United States v. Reese (1876) it limited the Fifteenth. With the amendments narrowed, neither Congress nor the courts could reach the violence and laws undoing Black rights.

The Amnesty Act of May 22, 1872, removed the Fourteenth Amendment’s officeholding disabilities from most former Confederates, restoring the old Southern leadership to political life.

The North tired. The Panic of 1873 brought a depression, and Northern voters turned to hard times. **Redeemers**, white Democrats who promised to redeem the South from Republican rule, retook state after state, sometimes by fraud and violence, sometimes by election. By 1877 only Louisiana, South Carolina, and Florida still had Republican governments.

## 1876 and the Compromise of 1877

The election of 1876 was the closest in American history by electoral votes, decided by a single vote, 185 to 184. The Democrat Samuel Tilden won the popular vote, but twenty electoral votes were disputed, including Florida, Louisiana, and South Carolina. Tilden 4,288,546, Hayes 4,034,311; 185 electoral votes were needed to win. A fifteen-member Electoral Commission awarded all twenty to the Republican, Rutherford B. Hayes, 8 to 7.

The **Compromise of 1877** settled it. Democrats accepted Hayes as president, and in return the last federal troops left the South. The South’s remaining Republican governments collapsed. Reconstruction was over.

Like the Compromise of 1850, the bargain settled a crisis by trading concessions — but it pointed the other way. In 1850 the trade kept the Union together for another decade. In 1877 the trade ended Reconstruction and left Black Southerners to the Redeemers.

In 1850 California entered as a free state, the Mexican Cession territories were left to decide slavery for themselves, and a stricter Fugitive Slave Act returned runaways South — leaving slavery in place where it already existed. But the bargain left open whether slavery could spread into the territories, and within four years the Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854) reopened that question, letting settlers decide it — and the result was Bleeding Kansas.

The Redeemers wrote new constitutions and new laws. Poll taxes and literacy tests took the vote from Black men; grandfather clauses joined them in the 1890s, starting in Louisiana in 1898. **Jim Crow laws** separated Black and white people in trains, schools, and public places. The promises of the amendments survived on paper, unenforced.

In 1879 thousands of Exodusters left the South for Kansas, fleeing violence and laws that stripped their rights.

## What endured

Reconstruction failed to protect Black rights, but it did not leave nothing behind. The Thirteenth, Fourteenth, and Fifteenth Amendments stayed in the Constitution, and a century later the civil rights movement used them to win. That is the first thing that endured.

The second was the institutions Black Southerners built. Black churches became centers of community life and politics. Black schools, including Howard and Fisk Universities, trained teachers and leaders who carried the struggle forward. Those institutions survived the Redeemers and outlived Jim Crow.

The law moved against Black rights too. Congress passed the Civil Rights Act of 1875, guaranteeing equal treatment in public accommodations, transportation, and juries. The Supreme Court struck it down in the Civil Rights Cases of 1883, holding the Fourteenth Amendment did not reach private discrimination. In Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) the Court upheld segregation itself, ruling separate facilities could still be equal under the law.

Booker T. Washington, in Up From Slavery (1901), urged economic self-help through vocational training at Tuskegee. W. E. B. Du Bois, in The Souls of Black Folk (1903), called the color-line the problem of the twentieth century: the racial struggle behind the war would define the century.

Historians still argue over why Reconstruction failed. The Dunning school, writing in the early twentieth century, called Reconstruction a tragic mistake, a Northern overreach that punished the South. The revisionists, led by Eric Foner, answer that Reconstruction failed because white violence went unpunished, the North retreated, and the revolution stopped halfway: freedom without land or lasting protection.

The Lost Cause myth shaped the memory. After the war, Southern writers cast the Confederacy as a noble defense of states’ rights and home, and slavery as a kindly institution. The myth hid the war’s cause and Reconstruction’s promise for generations.

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0161 (Topic 5.10)

The stimulus is an original secondary passage on the plans for Reconstruction:

> Reconstruction was the nation’s second founding in miniature. The war had ended slavery, but nobody agreed on what freedom should mean. President Lincoln wanted the Southern states back quickly, asking only a small oath of loyalty. President Johnson kept Lincoln’s lenient course, pardoning former Confederates and accepting new Southern governments. Congress answered with a sterner program: the Freedmen’s Bureau, the Civil Rights Act, the Fourteenth Amendment, and military rule for the South. Black Southerners used the opening to vote, hold office, and build schools and churches. Yet by 1877, federal troops had left the South, and the promises of Reconstruction were already fading.

a. Identify ONE way the federal government tried to help formerly enslaved people during Reconstruction.

Model answer: One way was the Freedmen’s Bureau, created in 1865 to give freedpeople food, clothing, and shelter. The Bureau also opened schools and helped freedpeople sign labor contracts. It was the first large federal agency built to help ordinary people start new lives.

b. Explain ONE way Congress and President Johnson disagreed about Reconstruction.

Model answer: Johnson wanted a quick, lenient reunion, pardoning former Confederates and accepting their new state governments. Congress wanted stronger guarantees for freedpeople, so it passed the Civil Rights Act and the Fourteenth Amendment over his vetoes. The fight was over who would set the terms of reunion, the president or Congress.

c. Explain ONE limit of Reconstruction’s help to formerly enslaved people.

Model answer: One limit was that freedom came without land, so most freedpeople had to work as sharecroppers on white-owned farms. Sharecropping left them in debt to the landowner for seed and supplies, which tied them to the land. Legal freedom without economic independence kept most freed families poor.

How to think about it: part a asks for one way the government tried to help, so name the Bureau and what it did. Part b asks how Congress and Johnson disagreed, so name what each side wanted. Part c asks for one limit, so name the missing land and what it meant.

### Worked item 2 — apush-saq-0164 (Topic 5.11)

The stimulus is a paraphrase of the Barron’s Chapter 7, Topic 5.11, and Princeton Review Chapter 9 accounts: Reconstruction failed because several forces acted together. An economic depression after the Panic of 1873 turned Northern voters toward hard times. The Supreme Court narrowed the new amendments in the Slaughter-House Cases, Cruikshank, and Reese. White Southerners used violence and “Redeemer” governments to retake power, while Northern voters grew tired of the effort. By 1877 the troops were gone, but the amendments, Black churches, and Black schools remained.

a. Identify ONE cause of the failure of Reconstruction named in the passage.

Model answer: One cause named in the passage is the economic depression that followed the Panic of 1873. The depression turned Northern voters toward their own hard times and away from Reconstruction. Once Northern voters stopped caring, Congress lost the will to enforce the new amendments.

b. Explain ONE way the Supreme Court contributed to the failure of Reconstruction.

Model answer: The Court narrowed the new amendments so they protected freedpeople less. In the Slaughter-House Cases it read the Fourteenth Amendment to cover only federal rights, and in Cruikshank it ruled that private violence was a state matter. With the amendments narrowed, neither Congress nor the courts could reach the violence and laws that undid Black rights.

c. Explain ONE way Reconstruction’s legacy endured after 1877 despite its failure.

Model answer: One legacy that endured was the set of Black institutions built during Reconstruction, especially churches and schools. Black churches became centers of community life and politics, and Black schools, including Howard and Fisk Universities, trained teachers and leaders. These institutions survived the Redeemers and carried the struggle forward into the next century.

How to think about it: part a asks for one cause named in the passage, so pick the depression and say what it did. Part b asks how the Court contributed, so name the narrowing cases. Part c asks for one legacy that endured, so name the institutions that survived.

### Worked item 3 — apush-saq-0166 (Topic 5.12)

The stimulus is an original secondary-style passage on the two compromises:

> Historians often pair the Compromise of 1850 with the Compromise of 1877. Each ended a crisis that threatened to break the Union or block a presidency, and each did so by trading something one side wanted for something the other side wanted. But the two bargains pointed in opposite directions: the first kept the Union together for another decade, while the second brought Reconstruction to an end.

a. Identify ONE thing the Compromise of 1850 and the Compromise of 1877 had in common.

Model answer: Both compromises settled an urgent crisis by trading concessions between the sides. In 1850 each side gave something up to hold the Union together, and in 1877 each side gave something up to settle the disputed election. Neither bargain solved the deeper conflict underneath the crisis.

b. Explain ONE way the Compromise of 1877 differed from the Compromise of 1850 in its effect on Black Americans.

Model answer: The Compromise of 1850 left slavery in place and postponed the question of its spread, but the Compromise of 1877 took away the federal troops that protected Black voters and officeholders in the South. Once the troops left, Redeemer governments and terror groups could strip Black Southerners of the rights the amendments had promised. The 1877 bargain did not postpone Black rights; it abandoned their protection.

c. Explain ONE reason the Compromise of 1850 did not settle the sectional conflict over slavery.

Model answer: The Compromise of 1850 settled the fight over the Mexican Cession lands, but it left open whether slavery could spread into the territories. Within four years the Kansas-Nebraska Act reopened that very question by letting settlers decide it, and the result was Bleeding Kansas. A bargain that leaves the central question open cannot hold once that question returns.

How to think about it: part a asks for one thing the two bargains had in common, so name the trade of concessions. Part b asks how they differed for Black Americans, so name what each one did to their rights. Part c asks why 1850 did not settle the conflict, so name the question it left open and what reopened it.

## Key terms

- **Reconstruction** — the rebuilding of the South and the nation after the Civil War.

- **Wade-Davis Bill** — the 1864 congressional plan demanding a majority oath and barring Confederate leaders from office.

- **Freedmen's Bureau** — the federal agency created in 1865 to aid freedpeople with food, shelter, schools, and labor contracts.

- **Black Codes** — Southern laws that restricted freedpeople's movement, work, and rights after the war.

- **sharecropping** — farming white-owned land for a share of the crop, borrowing seed and supplies from the landowner.

- **Radical Republicans** — the congressional faction that pushed for strong guarantees for freedpeople.

- **Fourteenth Amendment** — the 1868 amendment making freedpeople citizens and guaranteeing equal protection.

- **Fifteenth Amendment** — the 1870 amendment barring states from denying the vote because of race.

- **Ku Klux Klan** — the secret society, founded in 1866, that used violence against Black voters and white Republicans.

- **Redeemers** — white Democrats who retook the South and promised to redeem it from Republican rule.

- **Compromise of 1877** — the bargain that settled the 1876 election and ended Reconstruction.

- **Jim Crow laws** — Southern laws separating Black and white people in trains, schools, and public places.
