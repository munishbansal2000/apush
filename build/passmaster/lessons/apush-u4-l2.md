What holds a country together when its regions want different things? After the **War of 1812**, the United States looked united: one party ruled, and the years were called the **Era of Good Feelings**. Under that calm, the North, the South, and the West were building different economies, and each one wanted national policy to serve its own work. Abroad, the young republic fought a second war with Britain, warned Europe off the Americas, and pushed toward the Pacific. This lesson follows both stories, at home and abroad.

## One party, many regions

The calm was real, and it was thin. The Federalist Party had opposed the War of 1812, and when the war ended in stalemate — and Jackson's victory at New Orleans made it feel like a triumph — the party looked disloyal and faded away. James Monroe easily won the presidency in 1816, and again in 1820. For a time the Democratic-Republicans stood alone in national politics, and the country called the years from about 1815 to 1825 the Era of Good Feelings.

Under the one-party surface, the regions were pulling apart. In the North, mills and workshops were turning farm daughters into wage workers. In the South, the cotton gin had made short-staple cotton profitable, and enslaved laborers grew the fiber that British mills wanted. In the West, family farms grew grain and livestock for eastern markets. The market revolution tied these regions into one economy, and it deepened the divide between free labor and slave labor at the same time.

Loyalty to region over nation — **sectionalism** — was becoming the country's hardest political fact.

The regions even showed their differences in how they argued. New England Federalists met at Hartford in December 1814, while the war was still being fought, to demand constitutional limits on war and trade. Their Hartford Convention proposed amendments, not secession, but the timing ruined the party: peace and Jackson's victory at New Orleans arrived just after the convention adjourned, and the Federalists never recovered.

## Clay's American System
Henry Clay of Kentucky offered a plan to bind the regions together, and he called it the **American System**. It had three parts: a protective tariff to shield American manufacturing, a **Second Bank of the United States** to steady the currency and lend credit, and **internal improvements**, roads and canals paid for by the federal government, to link the regions. Congress chartered the Second Bank and passed a protective tariff in 1816.

The plan was not new. Alexander Hamilton had proposed most of it in the 1790s — a national bank, tariffs, and aid to manufacturing — and Clay restated it for a country now building factories. But Clay added one part Hamilton had never proposed: internal improvements, roads and canals paid for by the federal government. The opposition was continuous too: Jefferson had fought Hamilton's program, and the South now fought Clay's.

Look at the canal in the picture below. The Erie Canal joined the Great Lakes to New York City when it opened in 1825, and it made western grain cheap to ship east. But New York State built it, not Congress.

![View on the Erie Canal, published 1832](u4/pr25e-ch08-q10.jpg)
Figure 1: View on the Erie Canal, published 1832. A canal boat on the waterway that tied the Midwest to New York City. Public domain.

The third part of Clay's plan lagged behind the other two. Presidents doubted the Constitution allowed the federal government to build roads and canals, and Madison vetoed the Bonus Bill for internal improvements in 1817. So the great canal of the age was a state project, not a federal one.

## The Missouri crisis

The first great test came from the West. Missouri applied for statehood as a slave state in 1818, when free and slave states stood eleven to eleven. The North answered with the Tallmadge Amendment, which would have barred new enslaved people from Missouri and freed enslaved children at twenty-five. The House passed it; the Senate rejected it.

The bargain that ended the fight is the **Missouri Compromise** of 1820. Missouri entered as a slave state and Maine as a free state, keeping the Senate at twelve and twelve. The Compromise also drew a line across the rest of the Louisiana territory at 36°30′ north latitude: slavery barred north of it, allowed south of it.

Thomas Jefferson wrote to Congressman John Holmes on April 22, 1820:

> "but this momentous question, like a fire bell in the night, awakened and filled me with terror. I considered it at once as the knell of the Union. it is hushed indeed for the moment. but this is a reprieve only, not a final sentence."

Jefferson feared the line itself: a geographical line drawn over a moral question would never be obliterated, and every new irritation would mark it deeper. The Compromise bought quiet, not peace.

Look at the map below. Abolitionists made it in the 1850s, and it still draws the line across the West, with the territories beyond it labeled as lands whose destiny is yet to be decided.

![Abolitionist wall map showing the Missouri Compromise line, 1850s](u5/5s24-ch14-mcq-01.jpg)

## War with Britain, again

Missouri was not the first split: the war that ended in 1815 had divided the regions first. Britain and France were at war, and Britain stopped American ships, seized cargoes, and pressed some 6,000 sailors into its navy between 1803 and 1812. In 1807 the British warship Leopard fired on the American frigate Chesapeake, killing three. Jefferson answered with the Embargo Act, banning American trade abroad — and hurting American merchants more than Britain.

The **War of 1812** came when the "War Hawks" in Congress — young westerners and southerners led by Henry Clay — demanded it. The vote split along sectional lines: the South and West for war, New England and some Middle Atlantic states against. The war itself was a near-run thing.

American forces burned York, now Toronto, and won the Battle of the Thames in 1813, where the Shawnee leader Tecumseh was killed. Tecumseh had built a Native confederacy to resist American expansion, and allied it with Britain, which could supply muskets and powder and stood as a great-power ally against the Americans.

But British troops seized Washington in August 1814 and burned the Capitol and the President's House.

Look at the map below: the war's three theaters — Canada, where American invasions failed; the Chesapeake, where British ships raided the coast; and New Orleans, where Jackson won his victory in early 1815. The **Treaty of Ghent**, signed in late 1814, gave back all seized territory and said nothing about impressment or the seizures that had caused the war. New England Federalists, who had met at Hartford in December 1814 to protest the war, never recovered from the charge of disloyalty.

## A doctrine and a continent

The war left America more confident abroad, and in 1823 President James Monroe announced the policy that bears his name: the **Monroe Doctrine** warned Europe against new colonization in the Americas and against interfering with the Latin American nations that had won independence from Spain — while promising not to interfere with colonies Europe already held.

The warning was bold and unenforceable: America had no navy to back it, and Europe respected it mainly because the British navy wanted Latin American trade kept open.

The push began with the Louisiana Purchase (1803): Jefferson bought a vast French territory for $15 million, setting aside his constitutional doubts for the practical gain. It doubled the nation's land and won New Orleans, which controlled trade down the Mississippi to the Gulf. But maps of the new territory left out the Native peoples who already lived there.

Closer to home, America pushed outward. The **Adams-Onís Treaty** of 1819 gave the United States Florida, after Andrew Jackson's invasion in the First Seminole War showed Spain it could not hold the territory, and settled the boundary with Spanish Texas.

A generation later, the push reached the Pacific Northwest: settlers poured into Oregon, politicians demanded all territory to 54°40′, and in 1846 President Polk settled for the 49th parallel. The Webster-Ashburton Treaty of 1842 had already settled Maine's boundary and the Caroline incident, both sides admitting wrongdoing.

Jefferson refused Tripoli's demand for higher tribute in 1801, and the Barbary Wars that followed ended American tribute payments in 1815. In China, merchants bought tea, porcelain, and silk, but the two sides saw trade differently: Americans treated trade as a right, Chinese officials as a favor the emperor granted for tribute. The Treaty of Wanghia (1844) gave the United States the same China trading privileges Britain held.

## Tariffs, nullification, and silencing the debate

The tariff carried the sectional fight into the 1820s. Average duties on imports climbed from about 25 percent in 1816 to about 33 percent in 1824 and about 43 percent in 1828. Each rise helped Northern manufacturers and raised what Southern planters paid for imported goods. The Tariff of 1828, the highest of the three, was called the Tariff of Abominations in the South.

South Carolina answered with **nullification**. In 1828 John C. Calhoun argued in the South Carolina Exposition and Protest that the states had formed the Constitution as a compact and could declare an unauthorized federal act void. In 1832 the state nullified the tariffs of 1828 and 1832. Jackson answered with the Force Bill and a threat of troops, and Congress passed the Compromise Tariff of 1833. The crisis ended the same way the Missouri crisis had: by bargain, not by settlement.

The Panic of 1819, when the Second Bank called in its loans, had already shown how a national institution could ruin western borrowers. But the slavery debate, not the Bank, is what Congress tried hardest to silence.

By the 1830s abolitionists sent petitions to the House in large numbers, and William Lloyd Garrison's Liberator, first published in 1831, demanded immediate emancipation rather than a gradual plan. Southern members answered with the **gag rule**: first adopted in 1836, it ordered every petition about slavery laid on the table without being read or debated. The rule stayed in force until 1844.

John Quincy Adams, then a member of the House from Massachusetts, fought the rule. The gag backfired: it outraged Northerners who had not cared about abolition and drew them into the movement. A bargain or a ban could quiet a sectional fight for a season, but the regions kept returning to the same questions.

## Key terms

- **American System** — Henry Clay's plan of a protective tariff, a national bank, and federally funded internal improvements.

- **Missouri Compromise** — The 1820 bargain admitting Missouri as a slave state and Maine as a free state, with slavery barred north of 36°30′ in the Louisiana territory.

- **Era of Good Feelings** — The years about 1815 to 1825, when the Democratic-Republicans stood alone in national politics.

- **Internal improvements** — Federally funded roads, canals, and other transport works meant to link the regions.

- **Second Bank of the United States** — The national bank chartered in 1816 to steady the currency and lend credit.

- **Nullification** — The claim that a state may declare a federal law void within its borders.

- **Gag rule** — The House rule, 1836 to 1844, tabling every antislavery petition without reading or debate.

- **Sectionalism** — Loyalty to one's region and its economic interests over national policy.

- **War of 1812** — The 1812–1815 war with Britain over trade, impressment, and expansion, ending in stalemate at the Treaty of Ghent.

- **Treaty of Ghent** — The 1814 treaty ending the War of 1812, restoring seized territory and settling none of the war's causes.

- **Monroe Doctrine** — President Monroe's 1823 warning against new European colonization or intervention in the Americas.

- **Adams-Onís Treaty** — The 1819 treaty by which Spain gave Florida to the United States and settled the boundary with Texas.

## Worked items

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0106

A secondary text paraphrases Barron's on Topic 4.3: regional interests often beat national interests, the North industrialized while slavery grew on cotton in the South, and the regions grew more linked and more divided at once.

a) Briefly explain ONE way the growth of industry in the North shaped national politics in the period 1816-1840.

Model answer: Northern manufacturers wanted protection from British goods, so they pressed Congress for tariffs like those of 1816, 1824, and 1828. The tariff became a national fight because it helped Northern mills while raising prices for everyone else.

b) Briefly explain a SECOND way regional economic interests shaped national politics in the same period.

Model answer: Southern planters depended on exporting cotton and importing manufactured goods, so they opposed protective tariffs and backed the claim that a state could judge federal law. Their opposition led to South Carolina’s nullification of the tariff in 1832.

c) Briefly explain ONE way the federal government tried to link the regions, and why that effort had limited success.

Model answer: Clay’s American System promised roads, canals, tariffs, and a national bank to bind the regions together. It had limited success because the tariff and the Bank split the sections, and federal road-building stalled on constitutional doubts, so New York built the Erie Canal itself.

The reasoning to practice: each part names one regional interest and ties it to one political outcome, so the two examples in parts (a) and (b) must come from different regions.

### Worked item 2 — apush-saq-0109

President James Monroe spoke to Congress on December 2, 1823:

> “With the existing colonies or dependencies of any European power we have not interfered and shall not interfere. But with the Governments who have declared their independence and maintain it, and whose independence we have, on great consideration and on just principles, acknowledged, we could not view any interposition for the purpose of oppressing them, or controlling in any other manner their destiny, by any European power in any other light than as the manifestation of an unfriendly disposition toward the United States.”

a) Identify ONE principle about existing European colonies that Monroe states in the excerpt.

Model answer: Monroe states that the United States has not interfered with existing European colonies and will not interfere with them. The Doctrine warned against new colonization and intervention, not against colonies that already existed.

b) Briefly explain ONE reason Monroe issued this warning in 1823.

Model answer: Latin American nations had won independence from Spain, and Monroe feared the Holy Alliance of Russia, Prussia, and Austria would help Spain take them back. He also opposed Russia's claim to the Pacific Northwest above the 51st parallel.

c) Briefly explain ONE limit of the Monroe Doctrine as a statement of American power in 1823.

Model answer: The United States had no navy strong enough to enforce the Doctrine against Europe. It was respected mainly because the British navy, which also wanted Latin American trade open, kept other European powers out.

The reasoning to practice: part (a) reads the Doctrine's own words, part (b) gives the reason for the warning, and part (c) tests the Doctrine against the navy that would have enforced it.
