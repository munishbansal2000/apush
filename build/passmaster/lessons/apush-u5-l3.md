By the 1850s the North and the South had become two different societies, and every attempt to settle their argument over slavery made it worse. This lesson follows the argument from the cities and the reform meetings to Kansas, the Senate floor, and the Supreme Court.

## Two different countries

By the 1850s the North and South were moving in different directions. The North built factories, railroads, and cities on free labor. The South grew cotton on slave labor, and enslaved people made up to 75 percent of the population in parts of the Deep South. The sections traded with each other, but their economies pulled them apart.

The North also grew by immigration. Large numbers of Irish and German immigrants arrived in the 1840s and 1850s, many fleeing famine in Ireland. They crowded into cities like New York, took low-paying jobs, and kept their languages. The Irish were overwhelmingly Catholic; the Germans were mixed, about half Protestant and the rest Catholic.

Many native-born Americans resented the newcomers. **Nativism**, hostility to immigrants, found political form in the **Know-Nothing Party**, formally the American Party, which tried to limit immigrants' rights and keep them from voting. Look at the print. "Uncle Sam's Youngest Son, Citizen Know Nothing," published in 1854, shows a young man framed by the American flag, as if the newest American were the truest one. The party won elections in several states before splitting over slavery.

![Uncle Sam's Youngest Son, Citizen Know Nothing, Sarony and Co., 1854](u5/5s24-ch14-mcq-05.jpg)
Figure 1: "Uncle Sam's Youngest Son, Citizen Know Nothing," 1854. A young man framed by the flag. Public domain.

The sections also differed in what they believed about work. The North's free-labor idea said that a person who worked hard could rise and own property. The South's slave system said that labor belonged to an owner, and that a small class of planters should rule. Those two ideas could not both govern the same territories.

## Reformers and the women's question

The 1840s and 1850s were also a reforming age. Abolitionists demanded an end to slavery at once. Some wrote and spoke. Others helped fugitives reach the North on the Underground Railroad, a network of safe houses guided by conductors like Harriet Tubman. A small minority, John Brown among them, chose violence.

The reform impulse spread to women's rights. In July 1848, Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott called a convention at Seneca Falls, New York. Both were veterans of abolitionism who had been barred from the World Anti-Slavery Convention in London in 1840 because they were women. Their **Declaration of Sentiments** rewrote the Declaration of Independence with women included.

> "He has taken from her all right in property, even to the wages she earns. He has compelled her to submit to laws, in the formation of which she had no voice."

The convention's demand for **women's suffrage**, the right of women to vote, was its most controversial. Even among reformers, the vote seemed a step too far in 1848, and the suffrage resolution — the only one that did not pass unanimously — passed only after Frederick Douglass spoke in its favor. The convention showed how abolitionism's language of natural rights could be carried into a new fight.

## The South answers back

White Southerners defended slavery with growing confidence. In the revolutionary era many had called it a necessary evil. By the 1850s John C. Calhoun and writers like George Fitzhugh called it a **positive good**, claiming that slavery cared for workers better than Northern wage labor did.

The defense went with a defense of states' rights: each state could judge federal law for itself, and the federal government had no right to interfere with slavery. Ministers cited the Bible, pointing out that the Hebrews owned slaves and that Jesus never condemned slavery. Plantation churches taught enslaved people to obey their masters.

Southern culture carried the message too. Minstrel shows, in which white performers in blackface caricatured Black Americans as lazy and happy-go-lucky, were popular across the country, not only in the South. By the 1850s the caricatures grew crueler, a conscious answer to abolitionist agitation.

Enslaved people resisted in their own ways. Some rebelled outright, like Nat Turner, whose 1831 revolt killed about 55 white people and was crushed with great violence. Most resisted quietly, slowing work, breaking tools, and building families and communities that protected their dignity.

In August 1857 the Ohio Life Insurance and Trust Company failed, and the panic spread fast. Banks stopped paying out gold, railroad stocks fell, and more than 5,000 American businesses failed within a year. The North's factories and railroads suffered most, while the South's cotton trade stayed strong. Southerners pointed to the panic as proof that their slave economy was sounder than Northern free labor.

## Kansas-Nebraska and Bleeding Kansas

In 1854 Stephen A. Douglas wanted a transcontinental railroad from Chicago, and the land it would cross had to be organized. The Kansas-Nebraska Act split the northern Louisiana Purchase into two territories and let settlers decide slavery by popular sovereignty. The price was repealing the Missouri Compromise line, which had banned slavery there since 1820.

Northerners saw betrayal. The act reopened a question they thought settled and drove settlers of both sides into Kansas to decide the vote. When the territorial election came on March 30, 1855, thousands of proslavery Missourians crossed the border to vote. The February census had counted only 2,905 legal voters, yet 6,307 ballots were cast — only about 1,410 of them legal. Two rival governments followed: the antislavery Topeka Constitution and the proslavery **Lecompton Constitution**.

Violence followed. In May 1856 a proslavery posse sacked Lawrence. Days later John Brown led a band that killed five proslavery settlers at Pottawatomie Creek. More than 200 people died, and newspapers called it **Bleeding Kansas**.

Look at the map. Reynolds's Political Map of the United States, published in New York in 1856, was a Republican campaign map. Frémont and Dayton look down from the top corners, the free states are pink, the slave states dark gray, and the open territories green, with Kansas pale in the middle. The map argues in color that free labor builds a stronger society.

The map also draws the Missouri Compromise line across the middle. Its tables compare the sections from the census of 1850 by population, wealth, manufactures, and railroads, and in nearly every column the free states lead.

## Violence in the Senate and the Court

In May 1856 Senator Charles Sumner of Massachusetts gave a speech, "The Crime Against Kansas," attacking slavery and singling out Senator Andrew Butler of South Carolina. Two days later Butler's nephew, Representative Preston Brooks, entered the Senate chamber and beat Sumner with a heavy cane until the cane broke. Sumner was left badly injured and could not return to his seat for years.

Look at the cartoon. John L. Magee's Southern Chivalry, published in 1856, shows Brooks raising his cane over Sumner, who clutches a paper marked "Kansas." The title mocks the South's claim to chivalry: argument versus clubs. Northerners saw the beating as proof of Southern barbarity. Southerners sent Brooks new canes and made him a hero.

![Southern Chivalry: Preston Brooks beats Charles Sumner, John L. Magee, 1856](u5/southern-chivalry-magee-1856.jpg)
Figure 3: Magee's Southern Chivalry, 1856. Brooks beats Sumner, who holds a paper marked "Kansas." Public domain.

A year later the Supreme Court spoke. Dred Scott, an enslaved man whose owner had taken him to live in Illinois and Wisconsin Territory, sued for his freedom. In the **Dred Scott decision** of 1857, Chief Justice Roger Taney ruled that Scott was still a slave, that Black people could not be citizens, and that Congress could not ban slavery in the territories. The decision struck down the Missouri Compromise.

The decision told Northerners that slavery could spread anywhere by law, and it told Southerners the Court stood with them. It also destroyed the Republican goal of keeping slavery out of the territories by law. The middle ground was gone.

## A new party and a raid

The Kansas-Nebraska Act killed the Whig Party, split between proslavery Cotton Whigs and antislavery Conscience Whigs. In 1854 former Whigs, Free-Soilers, and antislavery Democrats formed the **Republican Party**, built on the **free-labor** idea: hard work should let a person rise, and slavery's spread threatened that promise. The party did not demand abolition, only that slavery stay where it was.

The new party ran John C. Frémont for president in 1856 under the slogan "Free soil, free labor, free men, Frémont." Democrat James Buchanan won, carrying the South, but the Republicans had arrived as a major party in two years. Buchanan took 45.3 percent and 174 electoral votes; Frémont took 33.1 percent and 114; the Know-Nothing Millard Fillmore took 21.5 percent and 8.

On June 16, 1858, Lincoln accepted the Republican Senate nomination at Springfield with the ‘House Divided’ speech. ‘A house divided against itself cannot stand,’ he said. ‘This government cannot endure, permanently half slave and half free. It will become all one thing, or all the other.’ The speech said popular sovereignty could not settle the slavery question.

In 1858 Abraham Lincoln challenged Stephen Douglas for his Senate seat in seven debates. At Freeport, Lincoln asked how Douglas could defend popular sovereignty after Dred Scott. Douglas answered with the **Freeport Doctrine**: a territory could keep slavery out by refusing laws to protect it. Douglas kept his seat but lost the South; Lincoln became a national figure.

In October 1859 John Brown attacked the federal arsenal at Harpers Ferry, Virginia, hoping to arm enslaved people for a revolt. Federal troops under Colonel Robert E. Lee captured him; he was hanged for treason. Northerners who praised him as a martyr terrified the South, which believed the North meant to destroy slavery by force.

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0146 (Topic 5.5)

The stimulus is the Declaration of Sentiments, from the Seneca Falls Convention of July 1848:

> “He has taken from her all right in property, even to the wages she earns. He has compelled her to submit to laws, in the formation of which she had no voice.”

a. Identify one grievance the Declaration of Sentiments raises about married women’s legal rights.

Model answer: One grievance is that a married woman lost all right to property, even to the wages she earned. Under the law, her husband controlled what she owned and earned, leaving her no property of her own.

b. Explain how the Seneca Falls Convention grew out of the abolitionist movement.

Model answer: Its organizers, Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott, were veterans of abolitionism who had been barred from the World Anti-Slavery Convention in London in 1840 because they were women. That exclusion pushed them to hold their own meeting, carrying abolitionism’s language of natural rights into the fight for women’s equality.

c. Explain one reason the convention’s demand for women’s suffrage was its most controversial.

Model answer: Even among reformers, the vote seemed a step too far in 1848, so the suffrage resolution was the only one that faced serious opposition. It passed only after Frederick Douglass spoke in its favor, showing that the ballot marked the edge of what reformers would accept.

How to think about it: part a asks for one grievance, so take it straight from the quoted lines. Part b asks how the convention grew from abolitionism, so name the organizers and the London meeting that excluded them. Part c asks why suffrage was the most controversial demand, so name who objected and who saved it.

### Worked item 2 — apush-saq-0149 (Topic 5.6)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage:

> By the late 1850s, every attempt to settle the slavery question had left the sections angrier than before. The Missouri Compromise had drawn a line, the Compromise of 1850 had patched the Union together, and the Kansas-Nebraska Act had let the settlers vote — yet each settlement became a new quarrel. In Kansas, settlers fought with rifles as well as ballots, and in Washington, a senator was beaten at his desk for a speech about Kansas.

a. Identify one development in the period 1854–1859 that deepened sectional hostility between the North and the South.

Model answer: One development was “Bleeding Kansas,” where proslavery and antislavery settlers fought for control of the territory after a rigged election. The violence showed Northerners that slavery would spread by fraud and force, not by fair votes.

b. Explain how the Kansas-Nebraska Act deepened sectional hostility.

Model answer: The act repealed the Missouri Compromise line and let settlers vote on slavery, which reopened a question Northerners thought settled. It also drove settlers of both sides into Kansas to decide the vote, turning the territory into a battleground.

c. Explain one reason the Dred Scott decision deepened sectional hostility.

Model answer: The Court ruled that Congress could not bar slavery from the territories, which told Northerners that slavery could spread anywhere by law. It also ruled that Black people could not be citizens, which convinced many Northerners that the Court favored the slaveholders.

How to think about it: part a asks for one development, so name an event from the passage and say what it showed. Part b asks how the act deepened hostility, so name what it repealed and what it set in motion. Part c asks for one reason the decision deepened hostility, so name a ruling and what it told Northerners.

## Key terms

- **nativism** — hostility to immigrants, which in the 1850s targeted Irish and German Catholics.

- **Know-Nothing Party** — the nativist American Party of the 1850s, which tried to limit immigrants' rights and voting.

- **Declaration of Sentiments** — the 1848 Seneca Falls statement of women's grievances, modeled on the Declaration of Independence.

- **women's suffrage** — the right of women to vote, the Seneca Falls convention's most controversial demand.

- **positive good** — the Southern claim, voiced by Calhoun and Fitzhugh, that slavery benefited workers and society.

- **Lecompton Constitution** — the proslavery constitution written for Kansas after a rigged 1855 election.

- **Bleeding Kansas** — the name for the violence between proslavery and antislavery settlers in Kansas in the mid-1850s.

- **Dred Scott decision** — the Supreme Court's 1857 ruling that Black people could not be citizens and Congress could not ban slavery in the territories.

- **Republican Party** — the party formed in 1854 to keep slavery out of the territories.

- **free-labor** — the idea that hard work should let a person rise and own property.

- **Freeport Doctrine** — Douglas's 1858 claim that a territory could keep slavery out by refusing to pass laws protecting it.
