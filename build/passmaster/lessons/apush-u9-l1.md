Why did American politics turn to the right after 1980 — and why did the turn last for decades? This lesson answers that question. The answer is that a new conservative movement changed what Americans expected from their government, and its ideas kept shaping politics long after Ronald Reagan left office.

## A country ready to turn

By 1980, many Americans had lost faith in their government. Prices rose fast while jobs disappeared, a mix called **stagflation**, and the old playbook for managing the economy seemed to fail. American hostages were still held in Tehran, and President Jimmy Carter seemed unable to free them. Voters were ready for a leader who promised a new direction.

Ronald Reagan won the election of 1980 by promising optimism and smaller government. He won many traditionally Democratic blue-collar voters who had grown alienated from their party's liberal positions on abortion, taxes, and the role of government. His victory broke the New Deal coalition that had dominated national politics for nearly fifty years.

The period that followed had three great changes, and this lesson covers the first. A conservative turn reshaped politics after 1980. Then the Cold War ended, leaving the United States the world's only superpower. Then globalization tied the American economy to the world, while new technology and the attacks of September 11 remade American life.

The conservative turn was not one idea but a movement. It drew together people who wanted lower taxes, people who wanted a stronger stand against communism, and people who wanted a return to traditional values. The next section looks at how those groups found each other.

## The New Right

The **New Right** was the name for the conservative movement that rose in the 1970s and won power in 1980. It had roots in Barry Goldwater's 1964 campaign, but it grew into something larger: a coalition of suburban activists, evangelical Christians, and free-market economists who reshaped American politics from the grassroots up.

The movement had three wings that sometimes worked together and sometimes pulled apart. Cold War conservatives wanted to confront communism abroad. Pro-business conservatives wanted lower taxes and fewer rules on business. The religious and cultural wing, the largest at the grassroots, wanted a return to traditional positions on family, abortion, and school prayer.

The **Moral Majority**, founded by Jerry Falwell in 1979, turned evangelical Christians into a political force. Its voters helped elect Reagan in 1980 and pushed the Republican Party toward traditional positions on family life. Religion, once kept apart from party politics, now helped decide elections.

Reagan also changed who sat on the bench. In 1981 he appointed **Sandra Day O'Connor**, the first woman to serve on the Supreme Court. In 1984 the Democrats answered with their own first: Walter Mondale chose **Geraldine Ferraro** as his running mate, the first woman on a major party's national ticket. Both parties now knew that women voters were watching.

## Reaganomics and its limits

**Reaganomics** was Reagan's economic program: tax cuts, **deregulation**, and domestic spending cuts, built on **supply-side economics**. The theory held that cutting taxes on businesses would spur investment, and investment would grow the whole economy. In 1981 Congress passed a large tax cut, and Reagan rolled back rules on banking, industry, and energy.

The results were mixed. Growth returned and inflation fell, but the tax cuts plus a sharp rise in military spending left the government spending far more than it collected. The national debt more than doubled between 1980 and 1986. Critics said the wealthy gained most: the 1986 tax reform cut the top rate from 50 percent to 28 percent.

Reagan's hardest line came against organized labor. In August 1981 the air-traffic controllers' union, **PATCO**, went on strike — the **PATCO strike**. Reagan gave the strikers 48 hours to return, then fired more than 11,000 who stayed out. Look at the photograph: Reagan is speaking in the Rose Garden days after the strike began, setting his terms in public.

The firing broke the strike and the union, and it signaled a sharp break from the New Deal era, when the federal government had backed organized labor. Employers took the message, and union membership kept falling for decades. Yet Reagan could not shrink the government as a whole: Social Security and Medicare were too popular to cut, and voters would not give them up.

## Pressure and negotiation

Abroad, Reagan began as a confrontational Cold War president. He called the Soviet Union an evil empire and launched a military buildup, including research on a space-based missile shield called the Strategic Defense Initiative. He also backed anti-communist forces abroad, even undemocratic ones, a policy known as the **Reagan Doctrine**.

The hard line had costs. In Nicaragua, Reagan backed the Contras against the left-wing Sandinista government. When Congress cut off aid to the Contras, officials in his administration secretly sold arms to Iran and used the money to fund them. The **Iran-Contra affair**, exposed in 1986, raised a constitutional question: the secret funding had denied Congress its control over spending.

But Reagan also knew when to talk. In June 1987 he stood at the Brandenburg Gate in Berlin, with the Wall behind him, and challenged the Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev to prove his reforms were real by removing it. Look at the photograph: the Wall, covered in graffiti, rises behind the platform, with a sign warning that you are leaving West Berlin.

![President Reagan at the Brandenburg Gate, June 1987](u9/pr25e-ch13-q06.jpg)
Figure 2: President Reagan speaks at the Brandenburg Gate in West Berlin in June 1987, with the Berlin Wall behind him. Public domain (U.S. federal government work).

The speech paired pressure with an open door: Reagan pressed Gorbachev in public while keeping the way open to negotiate. Just over two years later the Wall fell, and about four and a half years later the Soviet Union was gone. The lesson covers that ending in full in the next lesson.

## The turn outlasts Reagan

Reagan left office in 1989, but the turn he led kept going. His vice president, George H. W. Bush, won the presidency in 1988 after promising he would not raise taxes. When the deficit ballooned, Bush signed a bipartisan budget deal in 1990 that raised revenue, breaking his pledge. Many conservatives decided he could not be trusted on taxes, and he lost in 1992.

The 1992 election showed how divided the country had become. Bill Clinton won, but the independent Ross Perot took 18.9 percent of the popular vote and no electoral votes. Landslide victories were giving way to narrow, closely divided elections, a pattern that would hold for decades.

Clinton governed as a centrist. He embraced the North American Free Trade Agreement, which removed trade barriers among the United States, Canada, and Mexico, breaking with organized labor. Look at the photograph: Clinton signs the agreement in December 1993, with former presidents Ford, Carter, and Bush standing behind him. Free trade, the photograph shows, had support across party lines.

![President Clinton signs NAFTA, December 8, 1993](u9/original-ctx-u9-04.jpg)
Figure 3: President Clinton signs the North American Free Trade Agreement on December 8, 1993, with three former presidents behind him. Public domain (U.S. federal government work).

In 1994 Republicans won control of both houses of Congress behind the **Contract with America**, a platform promising smaller government and **welfare reform**. Clinton answered by moving further to the center. In 1996 he signed the welfare reform law, ending the federal guarantee of welfare aid and adding work requirements, and he declared that the era of big government was over.

## The limits of the turn

The conservative turn changed the terms of debate, but it never shrank the government as a whole. Republican leaders kept promising to end big government, yet the federal government kept growing. Popular programs survived every attack, and later Republican presidents added new federal responsibilities in security and education.

The impeachment of President Clinton in 1998 deepened the divide between the parties. The House impeached him over personal misconduct, but the Senate acquitted him and he finished his term. Many Americans saw the impeachment as a partisan pursuit, and the parties came out of it further apart than before.

The election of 2000 showed the same split. Neither Al Gore nor George W. Bush had 270 electoral votes without Florida, and the Supreme Court's decision in Bush v. Gore settled the recount in Bush's favor. Eight years later, Barack Obama's election brought a Democratic president who expanded government's role in health care despite conservative opposition. Look at the photograph: Obama signs the Affordable Care Act in March 2010.

![President Obama signs the Affordable Care Act, March 23, 2010](u9/5s24-ch30-mcq-03.jpg)
Figure 4: President Obama signs the Affordable Care Act on March 23, 2010, with Vice President Biden and Speaker Pelosi behind him. Public domain (U.S. federal government work).

Opposition to the health-care law helped fuel the Tea Party, a movement that rose sharply after 2009 among voters who saw government as too big. And in 2016, Donald Trump's campaign, built on immigration, border security, and trade deals, split the conservative movement itself: mainstream Republicans grew uneasy with its populist wing. The turn had remade politics, but it had not settled it.

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0268 (Topic 9.1)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage: between 1980 and the present, the United States passed through three great changes — a conservative turn after 1980, the end of the Cold War, and globalization, with new technology and the attacks of September 11 remaking American life.

a. Identify one change described in the passage that reshaped American politics after 1980.

Model answer: The passage describes a conservative turn after 1980, as Ronald Reagan's election brought tax cuts and deregulation and a smaller federal role in the economy. The movement behind him kept shaping American politics for decades.

b. Explain one way the end of the Cold War changed the United States' role in the world.

Model answer: With the Soviet Union gone, the United States stood alone as the world's only superpower. It used that position to lead coalitions abroad, as in the Gulf War of 1991, when it expelled Iraq from Kuwait.

c. Explain one continuity in American life across the period 1980 to the present.

Model answer: The United States stayed deeply engaged in the world throughout the period, from the Cold War through the Gulf War and the wars after September 11. Its debates over the size and purpose of the federal government also never ended, running from Reagan's presidency through the present.

How to think about it: part a asks for one change from the passage, so take it straight from the text — the conservative turn. Part b asks for a change in America's role, so name what the Cold War's end made possible: sole-superpower leadership. Part c asks for a continuity, so name something that ran through the whole period, such as engagement abroad or the argument over government's size.

## Key terms

- **New Right** — the conservative movement that rose in the 1970s and won power with Reagan's election in 1980.

- **Moral Majority** — the evangelical political organization founded by Jerry Falwell in 1979.

- **supply-side economics** — the theory that cutting taxes on businesses spurs investment and grows the whole economy.

- **Reaganomics** — Reagan's program of tax cuts, deregulation, and domestic spending cuts.

- **deregulation** — the rollback of federal rules on business, accelerated under Reagan.

- **PATCO strike** — the 1981 strike by air-traffic controllers that Reagan ended by firing more than 11,000 strikers.

- **stagflation** — inflation and unemployment rising together, as in the 1970s.

- **Contract with America** — the Republican platform behind the party's 1994 takeover of Congress.

- **welfare reform** — the 1996 law that ended the federal guarantee of welfare aid and added work requirements.

- **Iran-Contra affair** — the secret sale of arms to Iran to fund the Contras in Nicaragua, exposed in 1986.

- **Sandra Day O'Connor** — the first woman to serve on the Supreme Court, appointed by Reagan in 1981.

- **Geraldine Ferraro** — the first woman on a major party's national ticket, the Democratic vice-presidential nominee in 1984.
