By the early 1890s the United States was the world's leading industrial power, and nearly everyone agreed the government had helped make it so. The argument was over what the government should do next. This lesson answers one question: who tried to change the Gilded Age, what did they want the government to do, and why did so little change?

## Reformers, mostly women

Much of the reform energy of the Gilded Age came from women. Women's clubs gave middle-class women a public role beyond the home, and the idea of **maternalism**, that women's work as mothers qualified them to clean up public life, helped justify reform work outside the home. The clubs ran libraries, pushed for clean water and kindergartens, and pressed city governments to act.

Temperance was the largest of these movements. The Woman's Christian Temperance Union, led by Frances Willard, framed the vote as "Home Protection": women needed the ballot to protect their homes from drunken husbands and saloon keepers. Carry Nation took a blunter path, smashing saloons with a hatchet in Kansas around 1900. Both women show the same pattern: reformers used the language of home and family to claim a place in politics.

Settlement houses were reform of another kind. Jane Addams's Hull House, opened in Chicago in 1889, served immigrants with classes, clubs, and kindergartens. Andrew Carnegie gave a different answer to the same question of what wealth was for: he spent his fortune on libraries and schools, arguing that the rich should administer surplus wealth for the public good. The reformers disagreed among themselves about whether private gifts or government action should do the work, and that disagreement runs through the whole period.

## Farmers organize

Farmers were among the first to organize against the new economy. The **Grange**, founded in 1867, won state laws regulating railroad rates, and the Supreme Court upheld those laws in Munn v. Illinois in 1877, ruling that states could regulate businesses "affected with a public interest." The Farmers' Alliances of the 1880s grew bolder, and in 1892 they became the **People's Party**, the Populists, whose **Omaha Platform** demanded government ownership of railroads, free coinage of silver, and an income tax.

The platform's words were blunt: "Transportation being a means of exchange and a public necessity, the government should own and operate the railroads in the interest of the people." And again: "We demand free and unlimited coinage of silver and gold at the present legal ratio of 16 to 1." The farmers' complaint was simple: they produced more and earned less, while the railroads and the banks set the terms.

In 1894 the unemployed marched on Washington. Jacob Coxey led an army of jobless men from Ohio to the capital, demanding public works and more money in circulation. Look at the stereograph: a column of men marches along a dirt road toward Washington, carrying banners that read "More Money Less Misery Good Roads." The marchers reached the capital and were arrested. Their demand, that the government act directly against hard times, was the Populist idea carried into the streets.

## The money question

The farmers' deepest grievance was money. Prices fell for thirty years after the Civil War, helping shoppers and hurting debtors. A farmer who borrowed when wheat sold high had to repay when wheat sold low, and the debt grew heavier every year. **Free silver** seemed to be the answer: coining silver would expand the money supply, raise prices, and lighten debts. Creditors and bankers loved the **gold standard** for the same reason farmers hated it: gold kept money tight and prices low.

Congress tried compromise. The Bland-Allison Act of 1878 ordered the Treasury to buy a limited amount of silver each month. The Sherman Silver Purchase Act of 1890 went further, ordering the Treasury to buy 4.5 million ounces of silver each month and pay for it with Treasury notes. Then came the **Panic of 1893**: banks failed, unemployment soared, and President Cleveland repealed the silver purchases.

The money question came to a head in 1896. William Jennings Bryan won the Democratic nomination with his "Cross of Gold" speech, telling the convention, "Burn down your cities and leave our farms, and your cities will spring up again as if by magic; but destroy our farms and the grass will grow in the streets of every city in the country."

Look at the cartoon: a giant silver mine owner sits on a box marked "16 to 1" and works a small Bryan dummy waving a paper that reads "Free Silver Harangues," while other dummies wait in boxes at each side. The caption reads, "If the show succeeds, he'll get all the profits." The charge is that Bryan was the silver interests' puppet, not the farmers' champion.

## A government that would not act

The Gilded Age federal government was powerful in some directions and idle in others. It gave railroads more than 175 million acres, kept tariffs high to protect manufacturers, and sent troops against strikers. But when ordinary Americans asked for help, it often refused. President Grover Cleveland vetoed a bill in February 1887 that would have spent $10,000 on seed grain for Texas farmers after a drought. His message was blunt: "though the people support the Government, the Government should not support the people."

Reformers pressed Congress to restrain railroads and trusts, and Congress answered with two laws that looked strong on paper. The **Interstate Commerce Act** of 1887 created the Interstate Commerce Commission, the first federal regulation of railroads. The **Sherman Antitrust Act** of 1890 outlawed combinations in restraint of trade.

Both proved weak in practice. The Commission was chronically short of money, and the courts narrowed its powers. The Sherman Act was narrowed by the Supreme Court in United States v. E. C. Knight in 1895. The Populists challenged Cleveland's view directly: where he saw relief as outside the government's job, they demanded that the government own the railroads and coin silver freely. The government of the 1890s helped business far more readily than it helped ordinary Americans, and the Populists said so plainly.

## Politics of the near-tie

Gilded Age elections were close, and the closeness shaped everything. In 1876 Democrat Samuel J. Tilden won 51.0 percent of the popular vote and still lost the Electoral College, 184 to 185, to Republican Rutherford B. Hayes. From 1876 through 1892, no winning candidate won a majority of the popular vote: Hayes won with 48.0 percent, Garfield with 48.3, Cleveland with 48.9 and 46.0, and Benjamin Harrison with 47.8.

In 1888 Cleveland even won the popular vote and lost the presidency. The parties were evenly matched, and neither could risk bold action.

The closeness produced bargains, not programs. In 1890, under President Benjamin Harrison, the same Congress passed the Sherman Silver Purchase Act and the McKinley Tariff, which raised tariff rates to about 48 percent, the highest yet. Westerners who wanted silver purchases voted for the high tariff that eastern manufacturers wanted, and eastern protectionists voted for the silver purchases. Critics called it the **Billion-Dollar Congress** for its spending, and voters punished it in the midterm elections that fall, when Democrats won control of the House.

The parties ran on patronage as much as on policy. The **spoils system** gave government jobs to the winning party's supporters, and office-seekers crowded every new administration.

In 1881 a disappointed office-seeker assassinated President Garfield, and the shock finally moved Congress to act. The **Pendleton Act** of 1883 required open, competitive examinations for classified federal jobs, so that fitness, not party service, decided who got hired. It covered only part of the civil service at first, and the spoils system survived for years, but the principle of merit had entered federal hiring.

## 1896 and what stayed

Look at the cartoon: giant money-bag figures in top hats, labeled with trust names, tower over tiny senators at their desks, under a sign reading "This is a Senate of the monopolists by the monopolists and for the monopolists." Joseph Keppler drew "The Bosses of the Senate" for Puck in January 1889.

The cartoon is a joke about a Senate, not the Senate, but its charge is serious: the trusts owned the chamber that was supposed to regulate them. Voters had little reason to expect reform from Congress.

![Giant money-bag figures towering over tiny senators at their desks](u6/u6-bosses-of-senate.jpg)
Figure 3: Joseph Keppler, "The Bosses of the Senate," Puck, January 23, 1889. Public domain.

The near-tie broke in 1896. William McKinley, standing for the gold standard, won with 51 percent of the popular vote, the first winning majority since Grant's in 1872, and Republicans dominated national politics for a generation.

Bryan's farm coalition was outnumbered by industrial America: McKinley carried the cities and the factory vote, while Bryan's strength lay in the rural South and West. The industrial North and East could outvote the farm South and West, and the Populist movement, fused with Bryan, faded after its defeat.

What changed in Period 6, and what stayed the same? The economy changed completely: an industrial nation replaced a farming one, cities filled, and corporations grew larger than states. Politics changed less: the two-party system, the tariff debate, and private control of business stayed in place, and the racial order of the South survived Reconstruction. The reformers named the problems, the workers, the farmers, and the immigrants, but the answers would wait for the next century.

The Supreme Court blessed that racial order in 1896, when Plessy v. Ferguson ruled that racial segregation did not violate the Fourteenth Amendment.

## Key terms

- **Maternalism** — the idea that women's work as mothers qualified them to clean up public life.

- **Grange** — the farmers' organization founded in 1867 that won state laws regulating railroad rates.

- **People's Party** — the Populist Party, founded in 1892, demanding railroad ownership, free silver, and an income tax.

- **Omaha Platform** — the People's Party platform of 1892, demanding government ownership of railroads and free coinage of silver at 16 to 1.

- **Free silver** — the demand for unlimited coinage of silver and gold at a ratio of 16 to 1, to expand the money supply.

- **Gold standard** — the monetary system in which paper money is backed by gold alone, favored by bankers and creditors.

- **Panic of 1893** — the depression that began in 1893, with bank failures and soaring unemployment.

- **Spoils system** — the practice of giving government jobs to the winning party's supporters.

- **Pendleton Act** — the 1883 civil service reform law requiring competitive examinations for classified federal jobs.

- **Interstate Commerce Act** — the 1887 law creating the Interstate Commerce Commission, the first federal regulation of railroads.

- **Sherman Antitrust Act** — the 1890 law outlawing combinations in restraint of trade.

- **Billion-Dollar Congress** — the 1890 Congress, criticized for its spending, that passed the silver act and the McKinley Tariff.

a. Identify one federal law passed in the Gilded Age to regulate business.

Model answer: The Interstate Commerce Act of 1887 created the Interstate Commerce Commission to regulate railroads. It was the first federal law to regulate a private industry.

b. Explain why early federal regulation of business proved weak in practice.

Model answer: The Interstate Commerce Commission was chronically short of money, so it could not enforce its orders. The courts also narrowed the new laws, as the Supreme Court ruled in 1895 that manufacturing was not interstate commerce and lay beyond the Sherman Act.

c. Evaluate the extent to which federal regulation changed the American economy in the period 1865–1898.

Model answer: Federal regulation changed the economy only a little in this period: the new laws put the idea of regulation into federal law, yet the Commission and the Sherman Act left the trusts largely untouched. The change was real as a precedent, but small as a restraint on business.

How to think about it: part a asks for one federal law, so name the Interstate Commerce Act of 1887 or the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890. Part b asks why regulation proved weak, so name short funding and courts that narrowed the laws. Part c asks you to judge the extent of change, so weigh new federal power against business still setting the terms.

### Worked item 2 — apush-saq-0197 (Topic 6.13)

The stimulus is Section 2 of the Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act, January 16, 1883.

> An Act to regulate and improve the civil service of the United States, January 16, 1883, Section 2:
>
> “First, for open, competitive examinations for testing the fitness of applicants for the public service now classified or to be classified hereunder. Such examinations shall be practical in their character, and so far as may be shall relate to those matters which will fairly test the relative capacity and fitness of the persons examined to discharge the duties of the service into which they seek to be appointed.”
>
> “Second, that all the offices, places, and employments so arranged or to be arranged in classes shall be filled by selections according to grade from among those graded highest as the results of such competitive examinations.”
>
> President James A. Garfield had been shot in July 1881 by a disappointed office seeker and died that September.

Source: Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act, January 16, 1883, Section 2 (National Archives transcript). Both quoted rules are exact.

a. Identify the change the Pendleton Act made in federal hiring.

Model answer: The act replaced party favor with open, competitive examinations for classified federal jobs. Applicants had to show fitness for the duties of the job, and offices went to those graded highest on the examinations. Hiring would rest on merit rather than on service to the winning party.

b. Explain how the assassination of President Garfield led Congress to pass the Pendleton Act.

Model answer: Garfield was shot by a disappointed office seeker, which showed the country the danger of the spoils system. The killing gave civil-service reform a public urgency it had lacked before. Congress, which had resisted reform, passed the Pendleton Act in 1883 in answer to that shock.

c. Evaluate the extent to which the Pendleton Act ended the spoils system.

Model answer: The act ended the spoils system only partly. At first it covered only a small share of federal jobs, so most patronage hiring stayed in place. Top policy posts still changed with each president, though the share of merit jobs grew over time.

How to think about it: part a asks what the Pendleton Act changed, so name open competitive examinations for classified jobs. Part b asks how Garfield's assassination led to the Act, so link the killer's search for office to the demand for reform. Part c asks how far the Act ended the spoils system, so note that it covered only classified jobs at first.

### Worked item 3 — apush-saq-0199 (Topic 6.14)

The stimulus is an excerpt from Booker T. Washington's Atlanta Exposition address, September 18, 1895.

> In his 1895 address at the Atlanta Exposition, Booker T. Washington told this story: “A ship lost at sea for many days suddenly sighted a friendly vessel. From the mast of the unfortunate vessel was seen a signal, ‘Water, water; we die of thirst!’ The answer from the friendly vessel at once came back, ‘Cast down your bucket where you are.’” He then applied the story to Black Southerners: “To those of my race who depend on bettering their condition in a foreign land or who underestimate the importance of cultivating friendly relations with the Southern white man, who is their nextdoor neighbour, I would say: ‘Cast down your bucket where you are’-cast it down in making friends in every manly way of the people of all races by whom we are surrounded. Cast it down in agriculture, mechanics, in commerce, in domestic service, and in the professions.” And he told the white South that the races could live together this way: “In all things that are purely social we can be as separate as the fingers, yet one as the hand in all things essential to mutual progress.”

Source: Booker T. Washington, Atlanta Exposition address, September 18, 1895. Public domain; wording and punctuation follow The Booker T. Washington Papers, vol. 3. The lead-in and bridge sentences are the writer’s framing; the quoted story, the application passage, and the ‘separate as the fingers’ sentence are Washington’s own words.

a. Identify Washington’s advice to Black Southerners in this passage.

Model answer: Washington advised Black Southerners to seek opportunity where they already lived, in the South. Instead of looking elsewhere for help, they should “cast down their bucket” and build their lives through work in agriculture, mechanics, and business. The passage presents the South itself as the place to find a future.

b. Explain how Washington’s advice reflected continuity in race relations in the South.

Model answer: Washington’s advice accepted the racial order of the South as it stood. He did not call for an immediate end to segregation or for Black Southerners to claim full political rights at once. By urging work within the existing system, he continued the approach of accommodation rather than direct challenge.

c. Evaluate the extent to which Washington’s strategy addressed the problems facing Black Americans in the 1890s.

Model answer: Washington’s strategy addressed Black Americans’ economic problems only in part. It offered a real path to skills, property, and self-support, yet it left segregation and disfranchisement in place, as Plessy v. Ferguson showed in 1896. Economic progress without political rights proved an incomplete answer to the problems of the decade.

How to think about it: part a asks for Washington's advice, so state it in his image: cast down your bucket where you are. Part b asks how it showed continuity, so name what he accepted for the time being: segregation and delayed political rights. Part c asks how far his strategy worked, so weigh real economic gains against the rights it left unclaimed.
