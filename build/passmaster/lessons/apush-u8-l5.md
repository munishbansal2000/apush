By 1970 the postwar boom was over, and Americans learned what limits felt like: limits on growth, on energy, on money, and on trust in their government. The decade ended with a new conservatism rising and an old confidence gone. This lesson answers how the environmental movement, the energy crisis, stagflation, and Watergate changed what Americans expected from their country.

## A movement for the earth

In September 1962, Rachel Carson's **Silent Spring** warned that the pesticide DDT was building up in food chains, thinning the shells of birds' eggs and poisoning the water. The chemical industry attacked her, but the book sold millions and made pollution a public issue. Carson had shown that progress had a cost, paid by the land, the water, and the people who lived on them.

In January 1969, an oil well off Santa Barbara, California, blew out and spread oil across the beaches. The photograph shows the spill from the air: dark water around a drilling rig, with a second patch of oil spreading at the left. The spill pushed the public further toward action.

![Aerial view of oil spreading around a drilling rig](u8/santa-barbara-oil-spill-1969.jpg)
Figure 1: The Santa Barbara oil spill, 1969. Public domain (U.S. Geological Survey photograph).

Action came fast. On January 1, 1970, President Nixon signed the National Environmental Policy Act, requiring the government to study the environmental effects of its projects. Three months later, on April 22, 1970, twenty million Americans took part in the first **Earth Day**, in teach-ins borrowed from the antiwar movement. The photograph shows a crowd around a tall American flag that day. Nixon created the **Environmental Protection Agency** later that year, and Congress passed the **Clean Air Act**, setting national air standards.

![Crowd around a tall flag on Earth Day, 1970](u8/earth-day-1970.jpg)
Figure 2: Earth Day, April 22, 1970. Public domain (National Park Service photograph).

The movement kept growing. The Endangered Species Act of 1973 protected plants and animals facing extinction, and the government set aside millions of acres as wilderness. Environmentalism had become a permanent part of American politics, and it would shape arguments about energy, growth, and the land for decades.

## Energy, oil, and nuclear fear

In October 1973, Arab oil producers cut off shipments to the United States for aiding Israel in the Yom Kippur War. The **oil embargo** brought gas lines, closed stations, and an energy crisis. Gasoline rose from 39 cents a gallon in 1973 to 53 cents in 1974, and the government set a 55-mile-per-hour national speed limit to save fuel. Americans learned their comfort depended on oil from abroad.

The crisis did not end. Gasoline reached 86 cents a gallon in 1979 and $1.19 in 1980. President Carter signed the law creating the Department of Energy in 1977, put solar panels on the White House roof and told Americans in a televised address on July 15, 1979, that the country faced a crisis of confidence. In 1978 he brokered the Camp David Accords between Egypt and Israel, but his presidency would be judged by events beyond his control.

In November 1979, Iranian revolutionaries seized the American embassy in Tehran and held 52 Americans hostage for 444 days. The hostages were released in January 1981, the day Carter left office. The crisis made Americans feel helpless, and it helped elect Ronald Reagan. At home, Congress passed the Federal Land Policy and Management Act in 1976, and westerners who wanted federal land opened to mining and grazing answered with the Sagebrush Rebellion.

Nuclear power added a new fear. In March 1979, a reactor at Three Mile Island in Pennsylvania partly melted down, and thousands of nearby residents fled. The photograph shows the plant's four cooling towers beyond farm fields and houses. No one died, and little radiation escaped, but it ended new nuclear plant building in the United States for a generation.

![Cooling towers beyond farm fields and houses](u8/three-mile-island-1979.jpg)
Figure 3: Three Mile Island, April 6, 1979. Public domain (National Archives photograph).

## Stagflation and the Sunbelt

**Stagflation** was the decade's hardest economic problem: prices rising and jobs disappearing at the same time. Inflation ran at 5.7 percent in 1970, 11.0 percent in 1974, 9.1 percent in 1975, 11.3 percent in 1979, and 13.5 percent in 1980. In 1975, both inflation and unemployment stood above 8 percent. The old remedies failed: spending to create jobs raised prices, and cutting spending to fight prices cost jobs.

Presidents tried and failed to stop it. In 1971, Nixon froze wages and prices for ninety days, and prices rose again when the freeze ended. In 1974, President Ford handed out WIN buttons, for Whip Inflation Now, and asked Americans to spend less. Neither effort worked, because the causes lay deeper: oil prices, the cost of the Vietnam War, and a slowdown in productivity that no slogan could fix.

Factories closed while the economy grew. Manufacturing fell from 25 percent of American jobs in 1970 to 21 percent in 1980, and the number of factory jobs stayed about flat while the economy added about 20 million jobs elsewhere. The shift hit the older industrial states hardest, and it fed a new argument about whether American industry could compete with Japan and West Germany.

People and power moved south and west, to the **Sunbelt**. Warm weather, air conditioning, defense plants, and lower taxes drew jobs and families to the South and West. The South's share of the House rose from 106 seats in 1960 to 116 in 1980, while California grew from 38 to 45 seats, Florida from 12 to 19, and Texas from 23 to 27. The political map of the country was being redrawn.

## The conservative turn

The conservative movement began with students. In September 1960, young conservatives met at William F. Buckley's home in Sharon and wrote the Sharon Statement, calling for free markets, limited government, and firm resistance to communism. They formed Young Americans for Freedom, and their ideas spread in the Republican Party. In 1964, Barry Goldwater carried those ideas into the presidential race and lost in a landslide, but the movement survived.

The Republican Party learned to win in the South. Richard Nixon's Southern Strategy appealed to white Southerners who had left the Democratic Party over civil rights, and the South began its long move to the Republicans. The party that had lost the South for a century began winning its votes, its governors, and its seats in Congress.

The courts gave conservatives new causes. In **Roe v. Wade** (January 22, 1973), the Supreme Court ruled that the Constitution protected a woman's right to an abortion, drawing on the privacy right of Griswold v. Connecticut (1965). The **Equal Rights Amendment**, which would have guaranteed equal rights for women, won 30 states by 1973, then stalled three short of the 38 it needed, as Phyllis Schlafly's STOP ERA campaign argued it would hurt women.

In California, voters passed **Proposition 13** in June 1978, capping property taxes at 1 percent of a home's value. The photograph shows Jerry Falwell at a rally of the **Moral Majority**, founded in 1979, with American flags behind him and a choir praying. The movement's churches registered voters and turned out in elections.

![Falwell at a microphone with flags behind him](u8/falwell-i-love-america-1979.jpg)
Figure 4: Jerry Falwell at an I Love America rally, 1979. Public domain (Columbus Metropolitan Library photograph).

In 1972, the Supreme Court halted the death penalty in Furman v. Georgia. In 1976, Gregg v. Georgia let it return.

## Trust falls, and what changed

**Watergate** began as a small crime. On June 17, 1972, five men broke into Democratic headquarters in the Watergate building. They worked for the Committee to Reelect the President, as part of a campaign of spying on Nixon's opponents. The White House covered it up, and reporters Woodward and Bernstein kept digging until the cover-up unraveled.

The cover-up brought down a president. In October 1973, Nixon fired the special prosecutor in the Saturday Night Massacre, and his attorney general resigned rather than do it. The Supreme Court ordered Nixon to hand over his tapes, which showed he had joined the cover-up within days. The House Judiciary Committee adopted articles of impeachment on July 27 to 30, 1974. The full House never voted: Nixon resigned on August 9, 1974, and Ford pardoned him a month later, on September 8.

Trust in government fell with Nixon. In 1964, 77 percent of Americans trusted the government to do the right thing. By 1980, about 25 percent said so. President Carter came to Washington as an outsider and signed the 1977 treaties returning the Panama Canal by 1999. In January 1980, after the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan, he announced the Carter Doctrine, pledging military force if needed to defend American interests in the Persian Gulf.

The period closed with a new president. Ronald Reagan won the election of 1980, and the Cold War still shaped every presidency. The White House had changed parties four times between 1945 and 1981: in 1953, 1961, 1969, and 1977. The federal government had grown while defense took a smaller share. More than half of women worked for pay by 1980, college enrollment had grown about fivefold, and the population grew from 151 million to 227 million. The debate over top-down or grassroots change was still open.

## Key terms

- **Silent Spring** — Rachel Carson's 1962 book warning that the pesticide DDT was building up in food chains and poisoning the water.

- **Earth Day** — the day of environmental teach-ins first held on April 22, 1970, drawing twenty million Americans.

- **Environmental Protection Agency** — the federal agency, created in 1970, that enforces environmental law.

- **Clean Air Act** — the 1970 law that set national standards for the air Americans breathed.

- **Oil embargo** — the 1973 cutoff of Arab oil shipments to the United States, which brought gas lines and an energy crisis.

- **Stagflation** — the combination of rising prices and disappearing jobs that marked the 1970s economy.

- **Sunbelt** — the South and West, where warm weather, air conditioning, and defense plants drew jobs, families, and political power.

- **Moral Majority** — the religious conservative movement founded by Jerry Falwell in 1979, which registered voters and turned out in elections.

- **Equal Rights Amendment** — the proposed amendment to guarantee equal rights for women, which won 30 states by the end of 1973 and stalled three short of ratification.

- **Roe v. Wade** — the Supreme Court's 1973 decision that the Constitution protected a woman's right to an abortion.

- **Proposition 13** — the 1978 California measure that capped property taxes at 1 percent of a home's value.

- **Watergate** — the 1972 break-in at Democratic headquarters and the cover-up that forced President Nixon to resign in 1974.
