Between 1844 and 1877 the United States became a continental nation, tore itself apart over slavery, and then tried to rebuild itself. This lesson covers the first step: how and why the nation spread across the continent, and why every new piece of land raised the same question about slavery.

## A period in three acts

Period 5 runs from 1844 to 1877, and it falls into three acts. First the nation spread across the continent, annexing Texas, settling Oregon, and taking California and the Southwest from Mexico. Then the question of slavery in the new lands split the nation and brought civil war. Then the war ended slavery, and Reconstruction tried to define what freedom would mean.

The thread is land. Every new territory raised the same question: would slavery be allowed there? Northerners and Southerners gave opposite answers, and each answer hardened into a political position. By 1860 the positions had hardened into parties, and the parties into separate nations.

Each territory forced the question again. The Wilmot Proviso (1846) tried to bar slavery from the Mexican Cession. California’s rush forced the Compromise of 1850, admitting it as a free state. The Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854) let settlers vote on slavery; the Dred Scott decision (1857) said Congress could not bar it; John Brown’s raid (1859) terrified the South. After the war, Black Codes and terror held Black Southerners down, and the Compromise of 1877 withdrew the last federal troops.

Most Northerners in 1850 were not abolitionists. Abolition, the demand to end slavery at once, was a minority position. But many Northerners held a **free-soil** idea: western lands should be open to small farmers working their own land, without competition from large plantations worked by enslaved people. That idea, more than abolition, drove Northern politics in the 1850s.

The Civil War settled the question of slavery, and the amendments that followed remade the Constitution. The 13th Amendment ended slavery, the 14th made Black Americans citizens, and the 15th promised Black men the vote. Whether those promises would hold is the story of the lessons that follow.

## The idea: Manifest Destiny

In 1845 the newspaper editor John L. O'Sullivan gave the expansion a name: **Manifest Destiny**. He called it the belief that Providence, God's plan, had allotted the continent to the United States. The idea said that spreading across the continent was not a choice but a duty.

> "to overspread the continent allotted by Providence for the free development of our yearly multiplying millions."

The idea matched what many Americans already wanted. Settlers moved west for cheap land, for gold, and for a fresh start, more than for any divine plan. Politicians used the idea to justify annexation, war, and treaties. The idea also carried a racial claim: many white Americans believed that Mexicans, Native peoples, and Black Americans could not govern themselves, and that Anglo-Saxon settlers would bring order.

Look at the painting. John Gast's American Progress, painted in 1872, shows a woman in white floating westward, carrying a school book and stringing telegraph wire. Settlers, wagons, and trains follow her. To the left, Native families and buffalo move away into darkness. The painting presents expansion as light bringing schools and railroads to an empty land. It shows the people who already lived there only as figures retreating into darkness, and it leaves out the wars that took their land.

## Trails west

Before the land was American, the trails were already busy. The **Oregon Trail** ran about 2,000 miles from Missouri to the Pacific Northwest. The Santa Fe Trail ran south to New Mexico, and the California Trail branched off toward the gold fields. About 300,000 people traveled these trails between 1840 and the Civil War, usually in wagon trains.

The Applegate Trail was the southern route into Oregon that Jesse Applegate's party opened in 1846, running past what is now Roseburg and Grants Pass toward the Rogue Valley. The trail itself was a wagon road cut through mountains and river crossings.

The trails were dangerous. The Donner Party, snowbound in the Sierra Nevada in the winter of 1846–1847, lost 39 of its 87 members, and some survivors ate the dead to live. Yet the death rate on the trails was only slightly higher than for Americans in general. Native peoples were far more likely to work as guides and trade with the migrants than to attack them.

Some migrants traveled for faith. In 1847 the Mormons, members of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, settled by the Great Salt Lake in Utah, fleeing persecution in the East. The land was still Mexican territory when they arrived. The war with Mexico made it American soil within a year. This Mormon migration shows that not every move west was about land or gold.

## Texas and Oregon

Texas came first. Mexico had invited American settlers into its northern province, offering large grants of land. By 1836 about 30,000 Americans lived there, and they quarreled with the Mexican government over taxes, slavery, and local rule. In March 1836 they declared independence. Mexican troops under Santa Anna took the Alamo in San Antonio, killing its 180 to 250 defenders, including Davy Crockett and Jim Bowie. Six weeks later Sam Houston's army routed Santa Anna at San Jacinto and captured him.

The Lone Star Republic wanted to join the United States, but slavery politics blocked it: Texas would enter as a slave state. It waited until the election of 1844, when James K. Polk won on a promise to annex Texas and claim Oregon. Before Polk took office, Tyler pushed Texas through Congress by a joint resolution, a law passed by both houses instead of a treaty. Texas became a state in 1845. **Annexation**, the adding of territory to the nation, made the Union bigger and the slavery question sharper.

Oregon was settled by bargaining, not war. American expansionists demanded all of Oregon up to 54°40′, shouting "Fifty-four Forty or Fight." Polk, unwilling to fight Britain and Mexico at once, negotiated instead. The **Oregon Treaty** of 1846 drew the border at the 49th parallel and gave the United States what is now Oregon, Washington, and parts of Idaho, Wyoming, and Montana. The same year, American settlers in California declared the Bear Flag Republic, a short-lived independent republic that lasted until American troops took over.

## Gold, Japan, and the price of expansion

Gold changed the pace. In January 1848 gold was found at Sutter's Mill in California, a week before the United States formally took the land from Mexico. About 80,000 **forty-niners** arrived in 1849, and more than 300,000 had come by 1855. California's population rose from 92,597 in 1850 to 379,994 in 1860. A remote Mexican province became a booming American state in a single decade, and its rush for statehood forced Congress to face the slavery question.

Expansion reached beyond the continent. With West Coast ports in American hands, the United States wanted trade with Japan, which had shut out Western ships for two centuries. In 1853–1854 Commodore Matthew C. Perry sailed into Japanese waters with a letter from President Fillmore and a show of force, and Japan opened its ports to American trade. Look at the scroll. A Japanese artist painted one of Perry's steam locomotives, a machine the Japanese had never seen, with the care of someone recording a wonder.

![A steam locomotive painted on a Japanese scroll of Perry's mission, 1854–1858](u5/barrons-2027-ch07-02.jpg)
Figure 2: A steam locomotive from Perry's mission to Japan, painted on a Japanese scroll, 1854–1858. British Museum, public domain.

Expansion had a price, and others paid it. The Native peoples of California saw their population fall from about 150,000 in 1848 to under 30,000 by the Civil War, as disease, violence, and land loss struck together. The government pushed Native peoples onto reservations, and settlers broke the treaties that promised the remaining land would be left alone. Mexicans who stayed in the Southwest faced conflicts over control of the land after the war.

Every new territory raised the same question, and the next lesson follows it into war: would slavery be allowed in the land the United States had just taken?

### Worked item 2 — apush-saq-0136 (Topic 5.2)

a. Identify one territorial gain the United States made between 1844 and 1848, as described in the passage.

b. Explain how the idea of Manifest Destiny encouraged territorial expansion.

Model answer: Manifest Destiny taught that Providence had allotted the whole continent to the United States, so expansion looked like a duty rather than a choice. That belief gave leaders a reason to annex Texas, claim Oregon, and fight Mexico for California and the Southwest.

c. Explain one way expansion created conflict within the United States.

Model answer: Each new territory raised the question of whether slavery would be allowed there, and Northerners and Southerners gave opposite answers. The Wilmot Proviso tried to ban slavery in land taken from Mexico, and the fight over that question deepened the sectional split.

How to think about it: part a asks for one gain, so take it straight from the passage. Part b asks how the idea encouraged expansion, so link the belief to the action it justified. Part c asks for a conflict, so name the slavery question and one fight it caused.

## Key terms

- **Manifest Destiny** — the belief, named by John L. O'Sullivan in 1845, that Providence had allotted the continent to the United States.

- **free-soil** — the idea that western lands should be open to small farmers working their own land, without competition from slave labor.

- **Oregon Trail** — the about 2,000-mile overland route from Missouri to the Pacific Northwest.

- **annexation** — the adding of new territory to the nation.

- **Oregon Treaty** — the 1846 agreement with Britain that drew the Oregon border at the 49th parallel.

- **forty-niners** — the migrants who rushed to California for gold in 1849.
