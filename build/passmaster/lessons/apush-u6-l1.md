By 1865 the United States owned a continent it had not yet settled. By 1898 the map was filled with farms, railroads, mines, and towns, and the peoples who had lived there first had lost most of their land. This lesson answers one question: how did Americans remake the West in thirty years, and what did that remaking cost?

## The war ends, and the West opens

The Civil War ended in 1865, and Reconstruction followed. For twelve years the federal government kept troops in the South and argued over the rights of freed people. That argument ended with the **Compromise of 1877**: Republican Rutherford B. Hayes took the presidency, and the last federal troops left the South. Washington turned its energy elsewhere, and much of it went west.

Four years later, the **Transcontinental railroad** joined the coasts. On May 10, 1869, two locomotives met nose to nose at Promontory Summit, Utah, and a golden spike joined the Union Pacific and Central Pacific lines. Look at the photograph: a crowd of workers and officials stands between the two engines on bare ground. The line meant fast, cheap travel and shipping between the two coasts, and it meant settlers could reach the Plains in days instead of months.

![Two locomotives meet nose to nose at Promontory Summit, Utah, May 10, 1869](u6/5s24-ch17-mcq-07.jpg)
Figure 1: The golden spike ceremony at Promontory Summit, Utah, May 10, 1869. Public domain (Andrew J. Russell photograph, National Archives).

The government paid for this growth with land. The Pacific Railway Acts gave railroad companies more than 175 million acres, which they sold to finance the lines. Railroad track grew from about 35,000 miles in 1865 to over 190,000 miles by 1900. Every new mile of track pulled settlers, towns, and markets further west.

The government also made treaties as it built. The 1868 Treaty of Fort Laramie ended Red Cloud's War and promised the Lakota the Great Sioux Reservation, including the Black Hills. Gold found there in 1874 broke the promise: miners poured in, the army could not keep them out, and the treaty land was carved away.

## Land, steel, and wheat

The **Homestead Act** of 1862 offered 160 acres to any settler who would live on the land for five years and improve it. Millions of acres passed into private hands, and the railroads carried settlers west while carrying wheat and cattle east. Farming on the Plains was hard in ways eastern farmers never faced: little rain, few trees, and thick prairie sod that had to be cut before seed could go in.

Machines made the hard work pay. Steel plows, reapers, and threshers let fewer farmers grow far more grain. Look at the chart: American wheat production rose from 254 million bushels in 1870 to 599 million in 1900, more than doubling while the number of farmers fell. The Plains became the breadbasket of a nation that was learning to feed itself and sell grain abroad.

![Bar chart of United States wheat production, 1870 to 1900](u6/u6-wheat-production-1870-1900.png)
Figure 2: United States wheat production, 1870-1900, in millions of bushels. Source: USDA data.

Yet more grain did not mean more money. Falling crop prices left many farmers in debt, and the railroads set the freight rates farmers had to pay. The **Bonanza farm** was the extreme answer: huge wheat farms run like factories, with hired crews and machines. Most settlers worked smaller plots and watched prices fall year after year.

Farmers organized. The Grange won state laws capping grain-elevator and railroad charges, and in Munn v. Illinois (1877) the Supreme Court upheld them for businesses 'clothed with a public interest.' But Wabash v. Illinois (1886) barred states from interstate rates, so Congress passed the Interstate Commerce Act (1887), banning rebates and unjust charges. The Commission had little power to set rates, and the courts narrowed it further. Their anger would build further, into the Populist movement of Lesson 5.

## Boomtowns, mines, and cattle

Where miners struck gold or silver, a **Boomtown** appeared almost overnight. The Comstock Lode in Nevada drew thousands in 1859, and Virginia City grew from a camp into a city of theaters, newspapers, and saloons. Mark Twain wrote about it in 1863: a town where speculation ran wild and real business went on at the same time.

Mining boomtowns drew Chinese, Irish, Mexican, and Cornish workers beside native-born Americans, mixing fortunes made in a week with violence, disease, and sudden busts. When the ore ran out, the towns emptied just as fast. Buffalo Bill's Wild West later turned that West into a show of cowboys, sharpshooters, and staged battles.

The Homestead Act also reached New Mexico, where Hispano villagers held common land under Spanish titles. Anglo squatters took the commons, and the Act gave them federal legitimacy, while courts rejected the Spanish titles for want of English documentation. Las Gorras Blancas, the White Caps, cut fences, burned property, and won legislative seats in 1890 — direct action and politics, yet the land was still lost.

Cattle gave the Plains a second economy: Texas ranchers drove herds north to Kansas rail towns, where the beef shipped east, worked by Mexican vaqueros and Black and white cowboys on the long drives. **Barbed wire**, patented in 1874, let farmers fence the open range, and the long drives faded by the late 1880s. The open range closed into fenced farms and ranches.

Wyoming Territory gave women the vote in 1869, the first in the nation. Women were scarce, and their labor, running farms, teaching, and holding settlements together, was central to frontier life; the all-male legislature hoped the vote would draw more settlers. The act let women vote and hold office on the same terms as men.

## Native peoples lose the Plains

The same expansion destroyed the world of the Plains peoples. The railroads helped destroy the buffalo herds in the 1870s and 1880s, as hunters shot the animals for hides and sport and the railroads carried the meat and bones east. Without the buffalo, Plains peoples lost food, shelter, and trade goods at once.

Native peoples fought back. In 1876 the Lakota and their Cheyenne allies destroyed Lieutenant Colonel George Custer's command at the Battle of the Little Bighorn. The victory only brought a harsher answer: the army forced the Lakota onto a **Reservation**, land set aside for Native peoples under federal control, and kept them there with rations and soldiers.

Reformers answered with a different plan. Helen Hunt Jackson's A Century of Dishonor, published in 1881, charged the government with a shameful record of broken treaties.

The reformers' answer was the **Dawes Act** of 1887, which broke reservations into individual allotments of 160 acres per family head, held in federal trust for 25 years. The idea was that private property would turn Native peoples into farmers. Instead, the best land was declared surplus and sold to white settlers, and Native peoples lost about two-thirds of their remaining land, from about 138 million acres in 1887 to about 48 million by 1934.

Carlisle Indian Industrial School, founded in 1879, taught the same lesson in a classroom. Children were taken far from their families, and the school stripped away their languages, their dress, and their customs. Its founder, Richard Henry Pratt, stated the aim in 1892: 'Kill the Indian in him, and save the man.' The Dawes Act divided the land; Carlisle tried to divide the people from their past.

## The Ghost Dance and Wounded Knee

In 1877 the Nez Perce showed another path. Rather than surrender to removal, Chief Joseph led his people toward Canada — families, not an army, on a flight of over a thousand miles. They were caught in the Bear Paw Mountains of Montana, with their chiefs dead, their children freezing, and their people scattered. Joseph surrendered, saying he was tired of fighting. The flight showed what had changed: a decade earlier the government still treated tribes as nations to bargain with; now it forced them onto reservations.

By the late 1880s the reservation system was strangling Plains peoples: the buffalo were gone, rations were cut, and allotment was breaking up what land remained. In 1889 a Northern Paiute holy man named Wovoka preached a vision of renewal. If Native peoples lived righteously and performed the **Ghost Dance**, the dead would return, the buffalo would come back, and whites would disappear. The dance spread across the Plains as spiritual resistance, not a war plan.

Federal officials read it as a threat. In December 1890 reservation police tried to arrest the Lakota leader Sitting Bull and killed him in the attempt.

Days later, on December 29, 1890, the Seventh Cavalry surrounded Big Foot's band of Minneconjou Lakota at **Wounded Knee** Creek, South Dakota. During the disarmament a shot was fired, and the soldiers opened up with rifles and Hotchkiss guns, killing roughly 250 to 300 Lakota, many of them women and children. Look at the photograph: men load bodies onto a wagon in the snow while mounted soldiers watch from a ridge. Organized Native military resistance effectively ended there.

![Men loading bodies onto a wagon in the snow at Wounded Knee](u6/original-misc-ghostdance-05.jpg)
Figure 3: Burial of the Lakota dead at Wounded Knee, winter of 1890-1891. Public domain (Library of Congress).

## A settled continent, an unsettled question

By 1890 eight new western states had joined the Union since the Civil War, and the census declared the frontier line closed. The West was settled, fenced, mined, and railed, and its grain and beef fed an industrial nation. That is the change this lesson teaches: in one generation, federal land policy and the railroad turned a continent of Native nations and open range into farms, mines, and towns.

Frederick Jackson Turner read the same census bulletin and drew a famous conclusion. In his 1893 essay he argued the frontier had made America: breaking old customs, calling out new institutions, and shaping a democratic, self-reliant character. The claim has been criticized ever since, because the 'free land' Turner praised was Native peoples' land, taken by treaty-breaking, war, and federal force.

Much stayed the same. Racial inequality outlived slavery: Black Southerners faced violence, disfranchisement, and segregation, and Native peoples faced poverty and dependence on reservations.

The farmers' troubles also reshaped national politics. William Jennings Bryan, the Democrats' 1896 nominee, demanded free silver at 16 to 1 in his 'Cross of Gold' speech, but William McKinley won the election — industrial America outvoted rural America, and gold stood. Congress answered the trust anger with the Sherman Antitrust Act in 1890; it had barred Chinese laborers for ten years with the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882. Carnegie's 'trustee for the poor' met its challenge at Homestead in 1892.

The army's campaigns, the railroads, and the land laws worked together: each new line of track brought settlers, and each new settlement brought demands for more land. The West was remade, but the questions it raised, about who gained and who paid, were still open when the period ended.

## Key terms

- **Compromise of 1877** — the bargain in which Rutherford B. Hayes took the presidency and the last federal troops left the South.

- **Transcontinental railroad** — the rail line completed in 1869 that joined the eastern and western coasts at Promontory Summit, Utah.

- **Homestead Act** — the 1862 law offering 160 acres to settlers who lived on the land for five years.

- **Bonanza farm** — a huge wheat farm run like a factory, with hired crews and machines.

- **Barbed wire** — cheap fencing, patented in 1874, that let farmers close off the open range.

- **Boomtown** — a town that grew almost overnight around a mine and often emptied just as fast.

- **Reservation** — land set aside for Native peoples under federal control.

- **Dawes Act** — the 1887 law that broke reservations into 160-acre allotments, held in trust for 25 years, and sold the surplus to white settlers.

- **Ghost Dance** — Wovoka's 1889 religious movement promising the return of the dead and the buffalo.

- **Wounded Knee** — the December 29, 1890 killing of roughly 250 to 300 Lakota by the Seventh Cavalry in South Dakota.

> Historians often describe the Compromise of 1877 as the hinge between Reconstruction and the Gilded Age. In that bargain, Republican Rutherford B. Hayes took the presidency and the last federal troops left the South. With Washington's attention turned elsewhere, Southern states rewrote their laws and constitutions to strip Black citizens of political power, while the nation's energy poured into railroads, factories, and cities.

Source: Original secondary passage written for this question, following 5 Steps to a 5, 2024, Chapter 16, and Barron's Premium 2027, Chapter 8, Topic 6.1.

a. Identify ONE way the end of Reconstruction changed the South after 1877.

Model answer: With federal troops gone, Southern white leaders moved quickly to restore white rule: states rewrote constitutions with poll taxes and literacy tests that stripped Black men of the vote, and Jim Crow laws soon divided daily life by race. The federal government, which had once protected Black rights, no longer stood in the way.

b. Explain ONE way industrialization changed American life in the decades after the Civil War.

Model answer: Railroads and factories pulled millions of people into cities, where immigrants and rural migrants crowded into tenements and worked long shifts in mills and plants. The nation shifted from a rural, farming society to an urban, industrial one, with new wealth for owners and new hardships for workers.

c. Explain ONE continuity from the years before the Civil War that carried into the Gilded Age.

Model answer: Racial inequality continued: slavery was gone, but Black Southerners still faced violence, disfranchisement, and segregation, a second-class status enforced by law and custom. The promise of the Reconstruction amendments survived on paper while daily life preserved much of the old racial order.

How to think about it: part a asks for one Southern change after 1877, so name disfranchisement, Jim Crow, or the loss of federal protection. Part b asks for one industrial change, so name cities, factories, or tenements. Part c asks for one continuity from before the Civil War, so name racial inequality, which outlived slavery.
