The civil rights movement began with lawsuits and boycotts, and by the early 1960s it had become a mass movement of students, marchers, and voters. Then it widened: other groups claimed the same language of equality, and young people remade the culture around them. This lesson answers how the movement won its laws, how it split over what came next, and how its language spread to new causes.

## Students take the lead

On February 1, 1960, four freshmen from North Carolina A&T sat down at the lunch counter of a Woolworth's store in Greensboro and asked to be served. They were refused, and they came back the next day, and the next. The **sit-in** spread to more than fifty cities in two months, and students who had never protested before found that a lunch counter could be a battlefield. The students formed their own organization, the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee, known as SNCC.

In May 1961, the Congress of Racial Equality sent the **Freedom Rides**: Black and white riders traveling together through the South to test whether bus stations obeyed the Supreme Court's Boynton decision of 1960, which had outlawed segregation in interstate travel. In Anniston, Alabama, a mob burned a bus with riders still inside. The Kennedy administration first asked the riders to stop, then sent federal marshals to protect them, and the Interstate Commerce Commission finally ordered bus stations desegregated.

Birmingham, Alabama, was the movement's hardest test. In the spring of 1963, Martin Luther King Jr. led marches against the city's segregation, and the police answered with dogs and fire hoses. King was jailed, and from his cell he wrote the Letter from a Birmingham Jail, on April 16, 1963, answering white ministers who told him to wait. Children marched too, filling the jails in the Children's Crusade, and the pictures of dogs and hoses shocked the nation.

## March, law, and the vote

On August 28, 1963, more than 200,000 people marched on Washington for jobs and freedom. The photograph shows Martin Luther King Jr. in the crowd, wearing a button for the march. King closed the day with his dream speech, telling the crowd his children would be judged by their character and not by the color of their skin.

![King at the March on Washington, 1963](u8/barrons-2027-pt2-12.jpg)
Figure 1: Martin Luther King Jr. at the March on Washington, August 28, 1963. Public domain (National Archives photograph).

The march pushed Congress toward the **Civil Rights Act of 1964**, signed on July 2, 1964. Title II outlawed segregation in hotels, restaurants, and other public places. Title VII barred job discrimination and created the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission to enforce it. For the first time since Reconstruction, the federal government had real tools to enforce equality in daily life.

The vote was next. In the summer of 1964, hundreds of volunteers went to Mississippi for Freedom Summer, registering Black voters. Three workers, James Chaney, Andrew Goodman, and Michael Schwerner, were murdered. When the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party asked to be seated at the Democratic convention, it was offered two seats and refused them. The fight moved to Selma, where marchers were beaten on the Edmund Pettus Bridge on March 7, 1965, a day called Bloody Sunday.

President Johnson answered with a speech to Congress on March 15, 1965, in which he borrowed the movement's own words: we shall overcome. Congress passed the **Voting Rights Act of 1965** on August 6, 1965, outlawing literacy tests and sending federal examiners to register voters. In Mississippi, Black voter registration rose from 6.7 percent in 1964 to 59.8 percent in 1967. The vote was finally protected by federal law.

## Black Power and the movement's limits

Not everyone agreed on how the movement should go forward. Malcolm X, who had been the leading spokesman of the Nation of Islam, left the organization on March 8, 1964, and traveled to Mecca for the Hajj in April and May of that year. He returned with a broader message, open to working with whites, and in February 1965 he was assassinated. His life showed that the movement held more than one vision of freedom.

In June 1966, Stokely Carmichael used the words **Black Power** at a march in Greenwood, Mississippi, calling for Black people to build their own political and economic strength instead of asking white liberals for help. The Black Panther Party, founded in Oakland the same year, carried guns to watch the police and ran free breakfast programs and health clinics in Black neighborhoods. For many young Black Americans, pride and self-defense mattered more than integration.

The cities burned while the laws passed. In August 1965, the Watts neighborhood of Los Angeles erupted in six days of rioting after a traffic stop. President Johnson appointed the Kerner Commission, which reported that the nation was moving toward two societies, one Black and one white, separate and unequal. In April 1968, Martin Luther King Jr. was assassinated in Memphis, and riots broke out in more than a hundred cities. Congress answered with the Fair Housing Act of 1968, outlawing discrimination in housing.

## New voices claim equality

Farm workers claimed the movement's language next. In September 1965, Filipino workers struck the grape fields around Delano, California, and Cesar Chavez and the National Farm Workers Association joined them. A nationwide grape boycott brought the strike into American kitchens, and in 1970 the growers signed contracts with the **United Farm Workers**. In 1966 the workers marched 300 miles from Delano to Sacramento, and the march carried their cause across the country.

Native Americans claimed it too. In November 1969, a group calling itself the Indians of All Tribes occupied Alcatraz Island, a closed federal prison on unused federal land, saying the land belonged to them under an old treaty. The occupation lasted into 1971, and the federal government moved toward self-determination, letting tribes run their own affairs.

![Wall on Alcatraz painted WELCOME and INDIAN LAND, 1969](u8/alcatraz-welcome-indian-land-1969.jpg)
Figure 2: The Alcatraz occupation, 1969. Public domain (National Park Service photograph).

Women and gay Americans organized as well. The **National Organization for Women**, founded in 1966, demanded equal pay and equal work. **Title IX**, passed in 1972, barred sex discrimination in schools receiving federal money, and girls playing high school sports rose from 294,015 in 1971-72 to more than 2,000,000 in 1977-78. In June 1969, a police raid on the **Stonewall** Inn in New York set off days of protest that started the gay rights movement.

The courts set the limits of the new equality. In 1971, Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg approved busing to desegregate schools, but in 1974, Milliken v. Bradley refused to order busing across city and suburban lines. In 1978, the Bakke decision struck down racial quotas in college admissions while allowing race to count as one factor among many. Chicano activists formed La Raza Unida in 1970 to win political power in the Southwest.

## Young people remake the culture

Young people built their own politics. In 1962, the **Students for a Democratic Society** issued the Port Huron Statement, calling for participatory democracy and condemning racism, poverty, and the Cold War. Two years later, students at Berkeley protested a university ban on political tables and fundraising, demanding the right to speak freely on campus.

Mario Savio's speech about bodies upon the gears became the anthem of the **Free Speech Movement**. Berkeley became a model: the sit-in had forced the university to back down, other campuses copied it, and Berkeley veterans carried the tactic into the antiwar movement.

![Mario Savio speaking on Sproul Hall steps](u8/mario-savio-sproul-1966.jpg)
Figure 3: Mario Savio speaks at Berkeley, 1966. Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 3.0.

Music carried the new culture further than politics did. On February 9, 1964, the Beatles played on the Ed Sullivan Show, Bob Dylan's electric set at Newport in 1965 told folk fans that the old acoustic sound was gone. Rock music had become the voice of a generation.

![Four portraits of the Beatles in dark suits](u8/beatles-ed-sullivan-1964.jpg)
Figure 4: The Beatles in 1964. Public domain (Library of Congress photograph).

The **counterculture** went further still. Hippies gathered in San Francisco's Haight-Ashbury in 1967, rejecting materialism, careers, marriage, and the suburban routine. In August 1969, more than 400,000 people gathered at Woodstock, a festival the promoters had expected to draw 50,000. Four months later, a man was killed at Altamont, and the dream of a peaceful counterculture cracked.

The young also won political power. The 26th Amendment, ratified on July 1, 1971, lowered the voting age to eighteen, and eighteen-year-olds voted in 1972. Yet the movement's political impact stayed limited. The New Left distrusted the Old Left's parties and unions, and its energy went into protest and culture rather than into winning elections.

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0255 (Topic 8.10)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage on the Birmingham campaign of 1963.

> The Birmingham campaign of 1963 showed how a local protest could move the whole country. When police turned fire hoses and dogs on demonstrators, including schoolchildren, television and newspapers carried the pictures into homes across the United States. Public opinion shifted toward the movement, and President Kennedy sent a civil rights bill to Congress.

> After Kennedy's assassination, President Johnson pressed Congress to pass the bill, and it became the Civil Rights Act of 1964. The act banned segregation in public places and discrimination in jobs. Yet the act did not end poverty or the everyday segregation that Black Americans faced in housing and in Northern cities.

Source: Original secondary passage written for this question; facts from the grounding books.

a. Identify one way the Birmingham campaign gained national attention, as described in the passage.

Model answer: Television and newspapers carried pictures of police turning fire hoses and dogs on demonstrators, including schoolchildren, into homes across the United States. Those pictures put the campaign before the whole country.

b. Explain one reason President Johnson was able to get the Civil Rights Act passed in 1964.

Model answer: Johnson took up Kennedy's bill after the assassination, when sympathy for Kennedy and Johnson's own skill in Congress gave him leverage. Johnson pressed reluctant legislators hard, and the bill passed in the summer of 1964.

c. Explain one limit of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 shown in the passage.

Model answer: The passage says the act did not end poverty or the everyday segregation Black Americans faced in housing and in Northern cities. The act ended legal segregation in public places and jobs, but it left those daily conditions in place.

How to think about it: part a asks how Birmingham gained national attention, so name the television and newspaper pictures of police violence against demonstrators. Part b asks why Johnson was able to get the Civil Rights Act passed in 1964, so use sympathy after Kennedy's assassination, Johnson's skill in Congress, or the pressure of Birmingham and public opinion. Part c asks for one limit of the Act shown in the passage, so name what it did not end: poverty, or everyday segregation in housing and Northern cities.

### Worked item 2 — apush-saq-0258 (Topic 8.11)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage on how other groups pressed their claims after the Black civil rights movement.

> The successes of the Black civil rights movement of the 1960s inspired other Americans to press their own claims. In California, farmworkers led by Cesar Chavez used strikes and a nationwide boycott of grapes to win union contracts in 1970.

> On Alcatraz Island, Native activists occupied a closed federal prison from 1969 to 1971, demanding that the government honor its treaties. Women organized the National Organization for Women in 1966 and pressed the government to enforce the ban on sex discrimination in jobs. Each group borrowed tactics from the civil rights movement, such as marches, boycotts, and direct action, and each aimed them at a different injustice.

Source: Original secondary passage written for this question; facts from the grounding books.

a. Identify one group described in the passage that pressed its own claims after the Black civil rights movement.

Model answer: The passage describes farmworkers led by Cesar Chavez, who used strikes and a grape boycott to win union contracts in 1970. They are one group that pressed its own claims after the Black civil rights movement.

b. Explain one reason the farmworkers' grape boycott helped win union contracts in 1970.

Model answer: The boycott worked because shoppers across the country stopped buying California table grapes, which cost the growers sales they could not afford to lose. Background: The strikers alone could not shut down the harvest, but the boycott gave them allies among consumers, and the growers signed contracts in July 1970.

c. Explain one way the Native activists' goals on Alcatraz differed from the goals of the Black civil rights movement of the early 1960s.

Model answer: The Native activists demanded that the government honor its treaties with Indian peoples, which was a claim about tribal sovereignty and past promises. Background: They also claimed the return of land taken from them. Background: The early Black civil rights movement instead sought integration into American life and an end to segregation in public places and voting.

How to think about it: part a asks for one group described in the passage that pressed its own claims, so name the farmworkers, the Alcatraz activists, or the women of NOW. Part b asks why the grape boycott helped win contracts in 1970, so use shoppers refusing to buy grapes and the sales the growers could not afford to lose. Part c asks how the Native activists' goals differed from the Black movement's, so contrast treaty rights and tribal sovereignty with integration and an end to segregation.

## Key terms

- **Sit-in** — the tactic of occupying a segregated lunch counter or other public place and refusing to leave until served.

- **Freedom Rides** — the 1961 journeys by Black and white riders traveling together through the South to test desegregation of interstate bus travel.

- **Civil Rights Act of 1964** — the law that outlawed segregation in public places and barred job discrimination, enforced by the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission.

- **Voting Rights Act of 1965** — the law that outlawed literacy tests and sent federal examiners to register voters in the South.

- **Black Power** — the call, voiced by Stokely Carmichael in 1966, for Black people to build their own political and economic strength.

- **United Farm Workers** — the union, led by Cesar Chavez, that organized farm workers in California through strikes, marches, and boycotts.

- **National Organization for Women** — the women's rights organization founded in 1966, demanding equal pay and equal work; its 1967 Bill of Rights added control over their own bodies.

- **Title IX** — the 1972 law barring sex discrimination in schools receiving federal money, which opened school sports to girls.

- **Stonewall** — the 1969 protest at the Stonewall Inn in New York, after a police raid, that became the start of the gay rights movement.

- **Students for a Democratic Society** — the student organization that issued the Port Huron Statement in 1962, calling for participatory democracy.

- **Free Speech Movement** — the Berkeley student movement of 1964, led by Mario Savio, demanding the right to political activity on campus.

- **Counterculture** — the youth culture of the 1960s that rejected suburban careers, marriage, and the routines of mainstream life.
