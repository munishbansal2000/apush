What did Americans do with the republic they had won — and who did it leave behind? This lesson answers that question. In the 1790s Americans built a government, split into parties, and spread west, while slavery grew and most people still farmed.

## Hamilton's money and the first parties

Alexander Hamilton's first task as secretary of the Treasury was the debt. In his Report on Public Credit of January 1790, he called a national debt, if not excessive, "a national blessing," and proposed the federal government pay the national debt at full value and take over the states' war debts. Paying at full value would show lenders the government paid its debts, and taking over the states' debts would tie creditors to the national government.

The plan most directly benefited speculators who had bought old debt certificates at a fraction of their value. Critics objected that it rewarded speculators and made careful states pay for states that had not paid their own debts. Farmers and Southern planters gathered behind Jefferson and Madison, and that opposition hardened into the first political parties.

The bank fight made the split a constitutional one. Jefferson argued in February 1791 that Congress held only listed powers, and a bank was not one: stepping beyond them would take possession of a boundless field of power. That is strict construction — reading the Constitution's powers narrowly. Hamilton answered with loose construction: the **necessary and proper clause** lets Congress carry out its powers. Washington signed the bank bill in February 1791, siding with Hamilton.

Look at the engraving of the Bank of the United States in Third Street, Philadelphia, drawn by W. Birch and Son in 1799. The grand white building shows Hamilton's vision in stone: a strong national government tied to commerce and stable money.

## A nation between France and Britain

The French Revolution forced the young republic to choose. When France and Great Britain went to war, Washington's Neutrality Proclamation of April 22, 1793, declared **neutrality**: the United States would stay friendly and impartial toward both sides. Many Americans opposed neutrality: they felt America owed France help for its aid. Supporters answered the United States was too young and weak for a European war. When Citizen Genêt fitted out privateers in American ports in 1793, Washington enforced neutrality even against France.

Jay's Treaty, signed November 19, 1794 and ratified in 1795, settled the quarrel with Britain for the moment. Britain agreed to leave its forts in the American Northwest within eighteen months, but American merchants got only limited trading rights in the British West Indies, and British seizures of American ships continued.

Britain agreed to compensate merchants for seized cargoes, but paid nothing to planters for enslaved people who had fled to British lines, and Americans still owed debts to British merchants. Federalists defended it as the best bargain available, keeping peace and trade with Britain. Southern planters who lost by it disagreed.

Pinckney's Treaty, signed October 27, 1795, settled the southern border instead: Spain opened the Mississippi River to American shipping and set the boundary between the United States and Spanish Florida.

As Washington left office, he published his Farewell Address on September 19, 1796 — published in a Philadelphia newspaper. He advised the United States to steer clear of permanent alliances, and warned against "the baneful effects of the spirit of party."

Both warnings reflected the division of the 1790s between Federalists, who leaned toward Britain, and **Democratic-Republicans**, who leaned toward France. The address also set a precedent: Washington announced he would not seek a third term.

## Parties in power and the election of 1800

In 1794, farmers of western Pennsylvania refused to pay Hamilton's excise tax on whiskey, in what became the **Whiskey Rebellion**. Whiskey mattered because distilling grain made it compact and easy to carry — their cash crop. Washington reviewed about 13,000 militiamen from four states at Fort Cumberland, then returned to Philadelphia while Henry Lee led them on, showing the national government could now enforce its laws.

![Washington reviewing the Western Army at Fort Cumberland, 1794](u3/washington-reviews-western-army-1794.jpg)
Figure 2: Washington reviewing the Western Army at Fort Cumberland, Maryland, attributed to Frederick Kemmelmeyer, after 1795. Public domain.

By the late 1790s the two parties stood for different Americas. Federalists stood for strong government, commerce, and Britain; Democratic-Republicans for farming, states' rights, and France. In 1798, the Federalist Congress passed the **Alien and Sedition Acts** to silence Democratic-Republican criticism and weaken the opposition party. The Sedition Act made it a crime to publish false, scandalous, and malicious writings against the government.

Madison answered in the Virginia Resolution of December 1798, claiming the states could interpose to arrest a federal law they judged unconstitutional — a claim later called **nullification**. The Kentucky Resolutions made the same claim. The acts backfired: the Democratic-Republicans gained strength and won in 1800.

The election of 1800 went to the House of Representatives because Jefferson and Aaron Burr tied at 73 votes each. A tie was possible because each elector cast two unnamed votes, one for each of two men, without naming which man was his choice for president and which for vice president. John Adams won 65 votes and Charles Cotesworth Pinckney 64.

In February 1801, the House chose Jefferson on the 36th ballot. The election is remembered as a turning point because it showed that power could pass from one rival party to another by election, without violence.

## An American identity

Political independence left a cultural question: what was an American? Crèvecœur answered in his Letters from an American Farmer of 1782 that the American was a new man, formed by many European peoples blending into one people.

Noah Webster made the point. His American Spelling Book of 1783 taught schoolchildren American spellings — theater for theatre, color for colour, honor for honour. A school speller mattered: it put shared American spellings before children across the states.

Americans also built their identity through heroic stories. Congress commissioned John Trumbull's large painting of the Declaration more than forty years after the event, showing the founders as dignified leaders.

Women's claims grew from the same ideas. Judith Sargent Murray, writing in 1779 and published in 1790, argued that women's apparent inferiority comes from custom and unequal education, not from any natural difference. **Republican motherhood** gave women a recognized civic role raising virtuous citizens, but Murray went further, claiming women's minds are equal by nature and deserve schooling for themselves.

Abigail Adams wrote her husband on March 31, 1776, to 'remember the ladies' in the new laws, borrowing the Revolution's argument against taxation without representation. Weems's Life of Washington (1800) added the cherry-tree story in its 1806 edition to teach honesty. Phillis Wheatley, enslaved in Boston, published her Poems in London in 1773, claiming a public voice the laws denied her.

Religion showed the same split between reason and feeling. In August 1801, thousands gathered at Cane Ridge, Kentucky, for a six-day camp meeting led by Barton W. Stone. Preachers from Presbyterian, Methodist, and Baptist churches preached, and estimates put the crowd at 10,000 to 20,000 in a state of about 220,000. The **Second Great Awakening** brought revival religion to ordinary frontier people, while some educated Americans followed deism, trusting reason over conversion.

## Moving west

Settlers moved into the Ohio country, and a confederation of Native nations resisted them. In 1791 Little Turtle helped defeat St. Clair's army. In August 1794 General Anthony Wayne defeated the confederation at the Battle of Fallen Timbers. In 1795 the confederation signed the **Treaty of Greenville**, ceding most of what is now Ohio to the United States.

The Land Ordinance of 1785 divided the land north of the Ohio into townships six miles square, each cut into thirty-six sections of 640 acres — but no buyer could buy less than a whole section, at least $640.

The **Northwest Ordinance** of 1787 set the path from territory to statehood: first a governor and judges ruled, then an assembly formed once 5,000 free adult men lived there, and at 60,000 free inhabitants the territory became a state. It banned slavery north of the Ohio — but its fugitive proviso let owners reclaim enslaved people who escaped into the territory. Vermont joined in 1791, Kentucky in 1792, Tennessee in 1796.

The country grew: the 1790 census counted 3,929,214 people, including 697,681 enslaved people, and the 1800 census counted 5,308,483 — a gain of 35 percent in ten years, yet most Americans still lived in the countryside. The census existed to apportion House seats among the states, and the population grew from high birth rates and immigration. More than 25,000 refugees reached the United States from Saint-Domingue.

Look at George Caleb Bingham's painting of Daniel Boone escorting settlers through the Cumberland Gap, made in 1851–52. The painting is a later imagining, not a record.

## What changed and what stayed the same

Step back and ask what the period changed. In 1763, Americans were ruled by a king in London, and Parliament made the laws. By 1800, Americans elected their own president and Congress under a written Constitution. That is the great change of the period.

What stayed the same is just as important. Voting was limited to men with property in most colonies in 1763, and still limited to men with property in most states in 1800. New Jersey's constitution of 1776 let "all inhabitants" worth £50 vote, so some women voted there, until laws in 1807 limited the vote to white men.

For enslaved people, the contrast is sharpest. The Declaration said all men are created equal, yet the enslaved population grew from 697,681 in 1790 to 893,602 in 1800. The Constitution said Congress could not ban the importation of enslaved people before 1808, protecting slavery.

Northern emancipation moved forward while Southern slavery grew: the Northwest Ordinance's ban made the Ohio River a line, and south of it settlers carried slavery into Kentucky and Tennessee, where the cotton gin of 1793 made cotton pay.

For women, ideas shifted but legal standing stayed much the same. Adams's letter and Murray's essay borrow the Revolution's language, and by 1800 women had a recognized civic role raising virtuous citizens — but not the vote or office, and not equality before the law.

For Native peoples, the change ran the other way. In 1763, Britain's Proclamation barred settlement west of the Appalachians. In 1783, the Treaty of Paris gave the United States the land east of the Mississippi, and Native nations took no part in the treaty. At Fallen Timbers in 1794 and Greenville in 1795, Native nations lost most of Ohio. At every step, others decided Native claims to the land.

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0092 (Topic 3.10)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage: Hamilton's program funded the old debt, assumed the states' debts, and chartered a national bank, tying the wealthiest Americans to the federal government — and dividing the country into the first parties.

a. Identify ONE goal of Hamilton’s financial program described in the passage.

Model answer: One goal was to tie the wealthiest Americans to the new federal government. By funding the debt at full value and chartering a national bank, Hamilton gave creditors and merchants a stake in the government’s survival.

b. Explain ONE way Hamilton’s program contributed to the formation of political parties.

Model answer: The program gave opponents a shared cause to organize around. Farmers and Southern planters who saw little benefit in banks and bonds gathered behind Jefferson and Madison, and that opposition hardened into the Democratic-Republican Party against Hamilton’s Federalists.

c. Explain ONE limit of the passage as evidence about the 1790s.

Model answer: The passage states the author’s judgment that the program divided the country, but it gives no voices from the 1790s themselves. A reader cannot tell from this passage alone how ordinary farmers or merchants felt, or whether the parties formed because of the program or for other reasons.

How to think about it: Part a asks for one goal of Hamilton's program, so name tying creditors to the government. Part b asks how the program formed parties, so name who gathered in opposition and behind whom. Part c asks for a limit of the passage, so ask whose voices it leaves out.

### Worked item 2 — apush-saq-0095 (Topic 3.11)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage: republican motherhood gave women a civic duty to raise virtuous sons and shape the morals of the men around them, and it opened new arguments for educating girls, so female academies grew in the 1790s.

a. Identify ONE civic duty that republican motherhood assigned to women.

Model answer: Republican motherhood gave women the civic duty of raising sons to be virtuous citizens of the republic. Mothers were also expected to shape the morals and manners of the men around them.

b. Explain ONE way republican motherhood expanded opportunities for women.

Model answer: Because mothers needed knowledge to teach their sons, supporters of republican motherhood argued that girls should be educated. Female academies grew in the 1790s, giving more girls formal schooling than their mothers had received.

c. Explain ONE limit of republican motherhood as a step toward equality for women.

Model answer: Republican motherhood gave women no vote and no office, so it left formal political power in men’s hands. It also kept women in a largely domestic role, valuing them as mothers and moral guides rather than as citizens in their own right.

How to think about it: Part a asks for one civic duty, so name raising virtuous sons. Part b asks how it expanded opportunities, so follow the argument for educating girls. Part c asks for a limit, so name what it did not give: vote, office, or equality.

### Worked item 3 — apush-saq-0099 (Topic 3.13)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage: between 1754 and 1800 Americans went from British subjects to citizens of a republic with constitutions and a Bill of Rights, yet most still farmed, slavery grew in the South, women gained no vote, and Native peoples lost land.

a. Identify ONE change in American government between 1754 and 1800.

Model answer: Americans replaced rule by a king and Parliament with an elected republic under a written Constitution. The new government divided power among three branches and added a Bill of Rights.

b. Explain ONE way life stayed the same for enslaved people, women, or Native peoples across Period 3.

Model answer: For enslaved people in the South, slavery did not end with independence: the enslaved population grew from about 500,000 in 1776 to 893,602 in 1800. The Constitution even protected the slave trade until 1808, so bondage stayed legal and central to southern life.

c. Explain ONE way a revolutionary ideal led to a change after 1776.

Model answer: The Declaration's claim that all men are created equal pushed northern states toward gradual emancipation. Pennsylvania freed children born to enslaved mothers at age 28, and Massachusetts courts ended slavery there. The ideal did not end slavery, but it gave reformers language to use against it.

How to think about it: Part a asks for one change in government, so name the republic that replaced king and Parliament. Part b asks for one continuity, so pick a group and show what stayed the same for them. Part c asks how an ideal led to change, so name the ideal and the northern law it pushed.

## Key terms

- **necessary and proper clause** — the clause letting Congress make laws needed to carry out its listed powers, also called the elastic clause.

- **Whiskey Rebellion** — the 1794 refusal of western Pennsylvania farmers to pay Hamilton's excise tax on whiskey, put down by Washington's army.

- **neutrality** — Washington's 1793 policy of staying friendly and impartial toward both France and Great Britain in their war.

- **Democratic-Republicans** — the party of Jefferson and Madison, standing for farming, states' rights, and France.

- **Alien and Sedition Acts** — the 1798 Federalist laws aimed at silencing Democratic-Republican criticism, including the Sedition Act.

- **nullification** — the claim in the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions that states could interpose to arrest a federal law they judged unconstitutional.

- **Second Great Awakening** — the revival religion that brought camp meetings to ordinary frontier people, as at Cane Ridge in 1801.

- **republican motherhood** — the idea that mothers should be educated to raise sons as virtuous citizens of the republic.

- **Northwest Ordinance** — the 1787 law that set the path from territory to statehood and banned slavery north of the Ohio River.

- **Treaty of Greenville** — the August 3, 1795 treaty in which Native nations ceded most of what is now Ohio to the United States.
