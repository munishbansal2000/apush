How did a rebellion against the strongest empire on earth win — and what did its talk of liberty mean for the people it left out? This lesson answers that question. The Americans won by outlasting Britain, and the ideals they proclaimed were claimed by people their authors never meant to include.

## Lexington to Bunker Hill

In March 1775, Patrick Henry told the Second Virginia Convention that three million people, armed in the holy cause of liberty, were invincible by any force Britain could send — and closed with "give me liberty or give me death." The speech moved Virginia to authorize military preparations.

On the morning of April 19, 1775, British regulars marched to Lexington and Concord to seize colonial arms. British regulars stood in formation on the town green and fired a volley, while militiamen scattered and fell. Americans later called the first shot "the shot heard round the world" because the clash turned resistance into open rebellion.

Two months later, on June 17, 1775, came the Battle of Bunker Hill. The British won the hill but paid a heavy price in casualties, and the battle showed that the American **militia** — part-time citizen soldiers — would stand and fight.

Trumbull painted the battle in 1834. In the center, General Joseph Warren lies dying, held by a fellow officer, while militiamen in plain clothes fight hand to hand with British regulars. A Black soldier stands at the far right, evidence that Black men fought in the Patriot ranks from the start.

![John Trumbull, The Death of General Warren at the Battle of Bunker's Hill, 1834](u3/death-general-warren-bunker-hill-trumbull.jpg)
Figure 1: John Trumbull, "The Death of General Warren at the Battle of Bunker's Hill," 1834. Public domain.

Even then, most delegates still hoped for reconciliation. In July 1775 the Second Continental Congress sent the Olive Branch Petition to King George III, blaming the troubles on Parliament. The king refused to read it and declared the colonies in rebellion. The petition's failure closed the door on reconciliation, and the Declaration's list of the king's abuses was aimed at persuading foreign powers, especially France, that the cause was just.

## Endurance at Valley Forge

The **Continental Army**'s gravest danger in the winter of 1777–1778 was not a British attack but the collapse of its supply system. Congress lacked the power to levy taxes, so it had to request funds from the states, and it paid for the war by printing money that soon lost its value. At Valley Forge, Pennsylvania, Washington's troops went hungry, and his letter to Congress in December 1777 warned that the army might starve.

Look at William Trego's painting of the march to Valley Forge, made in 1883, more than a century after the event. Washington rides a white horse through the snow, leading ragged Continentals in thin coats. The painting is not a record of that December day — it is how later Americans remembered the winter: a test of endurance that made the army, rather than broke it.

![William B. T. Trego, The March to Valley Forge, December 19, 1777, painted 1883](u3/march-to-valley-forge-trego.jpg)
Figure 2: William B. T. Trego, "The March to Valley Forge," December 19, 1777, painted 1883. Washington leads ragged Continentals through snow. Public domain.

The army that marched out of Valley Forge in June 1778 differed from the army that marched in, mainly because Baron von Steuben had drilled it into a disciplined professional force. Steuben, a Prussian officer who had volunteered for the American cause, taught the men to march, load, and fight in formation. Endurance and drill, not a single great victory, kept the army alive through the hardest winter of the war.

## Saratoga and the French alliance

The victory that changed the war came at **Saratoga**. Burgoyne's army marched south from Canada in 1777, expecting General Howe to march up the Hudson to meet him. Howe marched to Philadelphia instead, and Burgoyne's army was surrounded and forced to surrender on October 17, 1777. Look at Trumbull's painting: Burgoyne, in a red coat, hands his sword to the American general Horatio Gates, who stands with his arm outstretched, American officers and soldiers around them.

![John Trumbull, Surrender of General Burgoyne, 1821](u3/surrender-burgoyne-saratoga-trumbull.jpg)
Figure 3: John Trumbull, "Surrender of General Burgoyne," December 1821. Burgoyne hands his sword to Gates at Saratoga, October 17, 1777. Public domain.

The surrender mattered most because it convinced France to ally openly with the United States. France signed the **Treaty of Alliance** on February 6, 1778, whose stated purpose was to maintain the absolute and unlimited independence of the United States. France had waited until Saratoga proved the Americans could win; after it, the rebellion became a war on two fronts that Britain could not afford.

Britain's commanders had expected a quick victory in 1775. The Royal Navy controlled the seas and could land troops anywhere, and Britain held the coastal cities. But the colonies were a vast interior behind a narrow coastal strip, and Britain could hold cities without holding the country. The historian Gordon Wood argues the Americans won less because their army was better than because the British never found a way to hold the country they marched through. The Americans only had to outlast it.

## The South and Yorktown

In 1778 the British shifted their campaign to the South, believing **Loyalists** were strongest there. They won battles — Charleston in May 1780, where some 5,000 Americans surrendered, and Camden that August — but they could not hold the countryside. At Kings Mountain in October 1780, American militia destroyed a Loyalist force. At Cowpens in January 1781, American troops destroyed British light troops. At Guilford Courthouse in March 1781, the British won the field but with heavy losses.

Nathanael Greene, who took command of the southern army in December 1780, never won a pitched battle. Instead he divided his small army, kept it moving, and forced Cornwallis to chase him across the Carolinas. The strategy traded ground for time: Cornwallis had to march long distances through hostile country, losing men to hunger, desertion, and small fights, while Greene's army stayed intact and kept recruiting. In the end the British held the battlefield and lost the countryside, which was the only ground that mattered.

The campaign drove Cornwallis north into Virginia, where he fortified **Yorktown**. There Washington and the French general Rochambeau besieged him by land, while the French fleet under de Grasse won the Battle of the Virginia Capes and blocked his escape by sea. Cornwallis surrendered on October 19, 1781.

He did not attend the ceremony himself; he sent his second-in-command, General Charles O'Hara, to present the sword. Yorktown ended major fighting because it convinced Britain the war was unwinnable: Parliament voted to end offensive operations, and the Treaty of Paris of 1783 recognized American independence and made the Mississippi the western boundary of the United States.

## Whose liberty?

In November 1775, Lord Dunmore, the royal governor of Virginia, issued **Dunmore's Proclamation**, offering freedom to enslaved people and indentured servants of rebels willing to bear arms for the king. Short of soldiers, Dunmore could gain men while striking at the Patriot economy. Virginia answered by offering pardon to those who returned — and threatening punishment for those who did not.

Both sides bid for enslaved people's choices, because those choices could decide the war. Enslaved people used wartime promises to seek freedom, while neither side offered emancipation. The king was offering freedom to the enslaved, so Patriots had to explain why their liberty did not extend to the people they held.

In March 1776, Abigail Adams asked her husband to "remember the ladies" in the new code of laws and not to put unlimited power into the hands of husbands — borrowing the Patriots' own argument that laws without representation were tyranny. John Adams laughed the request off. Yet **republican motherhood**, the idea that followed, gave women a recognized civic role raising virtuous citizens, while keeping them out of politics.

The **Loyalists**, meanwhile, were left to states that seized their property and drove them away, a fate a London print of 1783 mourned: "Is this a peace, when Loyalists must bleed?"

Loyalists saw America's future inside the empire: the British constitution already protected liberty, and rebellion would bring anarchy. Patriots and Loyalists disagreed about where liberty lived — republican self-government against loyalty to the Crown — but both sides claimed to defend English liberty, property, and order, and both feared tyranny: Patriots feared the tyranny of king and Parliament, Loyalists the tyranny of mobs and committees.

## The ideals travel

Phillis Wheatley, enslaved in Boston, published her Poems on Various Subjects in London in 1773, when enslaved people were denied literacy and a public voice.

The frontispiece, attributed to Scipio Moorhead, an enslaved Boston artist, shows Wheatley at a desk with quill and book, while the caption names her as the Negro servant of John Wheatley of Boston. In "On Being Brought from Africa to America," she credits her enslavers' faith, then turns it against white scorn: "Remember, Christians, Negroes, black as Cain, / May be refin'd, and join th' angelic train."

![Frontispiece to Phillis Wheatley's Poems on Various Subjects, 1773](u3/wheatley-frontispiece-1773.jpg)
Figure 4: Frontispiece to Phillis Wheatley, "Poems on Various Subjects," 1773, attributed to Scipio Moorhead. Wheatley at a desk with quill and book. Public domain.

After independence, the northern states began to end slavery, but slowly and on their own terms. Vermont's constitution of 1777 banned slavery outright, and Massachusetts courts ended it in 1783. Most northern states chose **gradual emancipation** instead: Pennsylvania's law of 1780 freed children born after that date only at twenty-eight, and Connecticut and Rhode Island passed similar laws in 1784.

No southern state followed. Slavery stayed legal in the South, and after the cotton gin of 1793 it grew more profitable — the enslaved population grew from about 500,000 in 1776 to about 700,000 in 1790.

In **Saint-Domingue**, the French colony on Hispaniola, about 500,000 enslaved Africans outnumbered about 30,000 free whites and 30,000 free people of mixed race. News of the slave rebellion there alarmed southern planters: enslaved people could overthrow a slave society and win independence for themselves.

In France, the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen of 1789 echoed the American claim that men are born and remain free and equal in rights.

## Worked items

### Worked item 1 — apush-saq-0077 (Topic 3.5)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage: historian Gordon Wood argues the Americans won less because their army was better than because the British never found a way to hold the country they marched through, and the Americans only had to outlast them.

a. Identify ONE reason Wood gives for the American victory in the Revolutionary War.

Model answer: Wood argues that the Americans won because the British could not hold the country they marched through: they could win battles and take cities, but they could not make the American people stop resisting. The British army was strong in the field, yet it could not occupy the vast countryside behind the coastal cities it held. Victory therefore went to the side that could keep the war going, not to the side that won the most battles.

b. Explain ONE way the British strategy failed to deal with the problem Wood describes.

Model answer: The British strategy depended on taking cities and winning set-piece battles, which assumed the rebellion would collapse once its army was beaten and its ports were held. That assumption failed because the rebellion lived in the countryside, where militia and local committees kept resisting after every British victory. The British could therefore hold New York or Charleston while the war went on around them, and each year of occupation cost London more money and men.

c. Explain ONE other reason, not mentioned in the passage, why the Americans won the war.

Model answer: A second reason is the French alliance: after Saratoga, France sent money, troops, and a navy, and at Yorktown the French fleet blocked Cornwallis's escape by sea while Washington besieged him by land. Without French help the Americans could not have forced a British army to surrender, and without the threat of a wider war Britain might have kept fighting. The alliance turned a colonial rebellion into a war on two fronts that Britain could not afford.

How to think about it: Part a asks for one reason Wood gives, so name what the British could not do. Part b asks how British strategy failed, so ask what taking cities assumes about where a rebellion lives. Part c asks for another reason not in the passage, so name the French alliance and what it did at Yorktown.

### Worked item 2 — apush-saq-0080 (Topic 3.5)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage: when Nathanael Greene took command in the South in December 1780 the cause looked lost, so he divided his small army, kept it moving, and forced the British to chase him across the Carolinas.

a. Identify ONE strategy Greene used in the southern campaign.

Model answer: Greene divided his small army and kept it moving, forcing the British to chase him across the Carolinas instead of meeting them in one great battle. He fought sharp, limited engagements such as Cowpens and Guilford Courthouse and then slipped away before the British could destroy his force. The strategy traded ground for time, wearing the British down while keeping his own army alive.

b. Explain ONE reason Greene's strategy worked even though he never won a pitched battle.

Model answer: The strategy worked because it made the British pay for every mile: Cornwallis had to march long distances through hostile country, losing men to hunger, desertion, and small fights, while Greene's army stayed intact and kept recruiting. A British victory like Guilford Courthouse cost so many men that it weakened the winners more than the losers. In the end the British held the battlefield and lost the countryside, which was the only ground that mattered.

c. Explain ONE way the southern campaign contributed to the American victory in the war.

Model answer: The southern campaign drove Cornwallis north into Virginia, where he fortified Yorktown and was cut off by Washington and the French fleet in the autumn of 1781. Greene's wearing-down of the British in the Carolinas left Cornwallis with too few men and too little support to hold the South. The campaign therefore set up the final surrender: the British lost the South first, and then lost their last southern army at Yorktown.

How to think about it: Part a asks for one strategy, so name what Greene did with his small army. Part b asks why it worked without a pitched victory, so count the cost of chasing: hunger, desertion, and small fights. Part c asks for the campaign's contribution, so follow Cornwallis north to where the chase ended.

### Worked item 3 — apush-saq-0081 (Topic 3.6)

The stimulus is a short secondary passage: after independence the northern states ended slavery slowly — Vermont banned it outright in 1777, Massachusetts courts ended it in 1783, and Pennsylvania, Connecticut, and Rhode Island chose gradual laws — while no southern state followed.

a. Identify ONE way northern states ended slavery after independence.

Model answer: One way was gradual emancipation: Pennsylvania's law of 1780 freed children born after March 1, 1780, when they reached twenty-eight, so slavery faded over a generation rather than ending at once. Vermont took the other path, banning slavery outright in its constitution of 1777.

b. Explain ONE reason northern emancipation was gradual rather than immediate.

Model answer: Lawmakers wanted to protect slave owners' property rights, so they let owners keep the labor of children until adulthood. A sudden end to slavery would also have divided the new nation by antagonizing the South, where slavery was deeply embedded.

c. Explain ONE limit of northern emancipation.

Model answer: Gradual laws freed almost no one alive when they passed, since only children born after the law went free, and only decades later. Slavery also continued in the South, where it grew stronger after the cotton gin made slave-grown cotton profitable.

How to think about it: Part a asks for one way northern states ended slavery, so name gradual emancipation or Vermont's ban. Part b asks why gradual, so think about whose property the laws protected. Part c asks for one limit, so ask who went free, when, and where slavery kept growing.

## Key terms

- **militia** — part-time citizen soldiers, such as the men who fought at Lexington and Bunker Hill, distinct from the full-time Continental Army.

- **Continental Army** — the full-time American army raised by Congress and commanded by George Washington.

- **Saratoga** — the American victory of October 17, 1777, where Burgoyne surrendered, convincing France to ally with the United States.

- **Treaty of Alliance** — the February 6, 1778 agreement in which France pledged to maintain American independence.

- **Yorktown** — the siege and surrender of October 19, 1781, which ended major fighting in the Revolutionary War.

- **Loyalists** — Americans who wanted to keep ties with Britain, about 15 to 20 percent of colonists in 1775–1776.

- **Dunmore's Proclamation** — Lord Dunmore's November 7, 1775 offer of freedom to enslaved people of rebels willing to bear arms for the king.

- **gradual emancipation** — northern laws that freed children born to enslaved mothers only when they reached adulthood, such as age twenty-eight in Pennsylvania.

- **republican motherhood** — the idea that mothers should be educated to raise sons as virtuous citizens of the republic.

- **Saint-Domingue** — the French sugar colony on Hispaniola where enslaved people rose in 1791 and eventually founded Haiti.
