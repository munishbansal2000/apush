# Rubric Map — DBQ: Johnson vs. Congress, 1865–1869

**Source file:** `build/reclaim-merged/staged/barrons-2027/pt1-dbq.json` (id: `barrons-2027-pt1-dbq`)
**Test:** test-02
**Prompt:** "Between 1865 and 1869, President Andrew Johnson clashed with congressional Republicans over Reconstruction in the South. Evaluate the relative importance of the causes of this conflict."

**Rubric reminders:** thesis must *rank* causes (relative importance), not just list them; sourcing on at least 2 docs; evidence from at least 3 docs (argument on at least 6); at least one specific beyond-docs fact (vetoes, 14th Amendment, Tenure of Office Act/impeachment all qualify).

## 1. Sourcing opportunities per document

- **Doc 1 (Lincoln, Second Inaugural, Mar 4, 1865):** A war president speaking at his inauguration to the public, setting a reconciliation tone. The key sourcing move: Johnson later claimed to be executing Lincoln's lenient policy — so Doc 1 is the *contested precedent* both sides fought over. A student can analyze its vagueness ("malice toward none") as the reason it could be cited by Johnson and dismissed by Republicans after the Black Codes.
- **Doc 2 (Mississippi Black Codes, 1865):** A white legislature, months after emancipation, writing law for state courts and officials to enforce. Purpose: restore white control of Black labor through vagrancy law. Because it is a statute rather than an opinion, it is proof of *action* — the strongest evidence that lenient restoration produced near-slavery, which is what radicalized Congress.
- **Doc 3 (Louisiana labor regulations, 1865):** A former-Confederate legislature writing for employers and justices of the peace. Purpose: bind freedpeople to plantation labor through contracts they could not leave. The humiliating particulars (punishment for "impudence," wages docked for sickness) reveal the economic machinery of the codes; a student can source the audience to show this was a working system, not rhetoric.
- **Doc 4 (Frank Leslie's engraving, Jan 1867):** A Northern illustrated paper depicting a freedman auctioned to pay a vagrancy fine, aimed at the Northern middle class. Purpose: dramatize injustice for readers far from the South. Honest sourcing acknowledges the Northern-paper POV may heighten the drama — but the practices it depicts are the ones the codes themselves authorized (Doc 2). A student can use it to show how media pressure built the Northern political will behind congressional Reconstruction.
- **Doc 5 (SC Colored People's Convention petition, Nov 1865):** Formerly enslaved South Carolinians petitioning Congress directly for equal laws, schools, homesteads, and political rights. Because the authors are the freedpeople themselves, the document counters the frame that Reconstruction was something Northern whites did *to* Black Southerners — they were organized claimants. The petition genre itself is evidence of Black political agency months after emancipation.
- **Doc 6 (Tilden speech, 1868):** The Democrats' national leader speaking to partisan supporters, charging that Reconstruction is a Republican power grab (a manufactured Black electorate delivering "twenty senators and fifty representatives"). A student must handle this as a hostile witness: even if self-serving, it documents the partisan-power dimension of the conflict and shows why Democratic resistance hardened around every veto fight.
- **Doc 7 (Stevens speech, Jan 1867):** The Radical Republican House leader addressing his congressional colleagues, demanding full legal equality and shaming moderates for stopping at "unchaining" four million people. Spoken to Congress rather than the public, it is legislative persuasion — the moral-ideological core of the Republican position. A student can use the insider audience to argue the break with Johnson was driven by conviction, not just strategy.

## 2. Beyond-the-documents evidence

- **Johnson's 1866 vetoes of the Freedmen's Bureau Bill and the Civil Rights Act** — the concrete veto fights that defined the conflict; Congress overrode him on the Civil Rights Act, the first major law ever passed over a presidential veto.
- **The Fourteenth Amendment (proposed 1866, ratified 1868)** — Congress's constitutional answer to the Black Codes and to Johnson: citizenship and equal protection written beyond a president's reach.
- **The Reconstruction Acts of 1867** — Congress imposed military Reconstruction on the South over Johnson's opposition, the legislative high-water mark of the break.
- **The Tenure of Office Act (1867) and Johnson's impeachment (1868)** — the conflict's climax: the House impeached him, and the Senate acquitted by a single vote.
- **The Memphis and New Orleans massacres (1866)** — white mob violence against freedpeople and Black troops, which northern voters read as proof that Johnson's lenient South could not protect Black life.

## 3. Complexity pathways

1. **Rank the causes explicitly.** The strongest essays will argue the conflict was *primarily* about the meaning of emancipation — whether freedom required federal guarantees of equality (Docs 2–5, 7) — while partisan power calculations (Doc 6) intensified but did not create the fight. A student can equally defend the reverse ranking; what earns the point is the weighing, with doc evidence attached to each side of the scale.
2. **Johnson's structural bind.** Analyze how Lincoln's vague leniency (Doc 1) plus the Black Code provocation (Docs 2–4) made Johnson's position nearly impossible: defending the southern states meant defending near-slavery. Complexity comes from showing the collision was overdetermined — not just clashing personalities.
3. **Center Black agency.** Use Doc 5 to argue that freedpeople themselves forced the conflict onto Congress's agenda — the cause was not only white actors colliding. A student can add nuance by noting the backlash this agency invited (the 1866 massacres as beyond-docs evidence), so Black political action appears as both a cause of congressional resolve and a trigger of southern violence.
