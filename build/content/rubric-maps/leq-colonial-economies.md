# Rubric Map — LEQ: Colonial Economies and Social Structures

**Source file:** `build/reclaim-merged/staged/fivesteps-2024/exam1-leq.json` (id: `5s24-exam1-leq-02`)
**Test:** test-09
**Prompt:** "Evaluate the extent to which differing economies shaped differing social structures in the English colonies in North America."
**Targeted reasoning:** Comparison

**Rubric reminders:** 6 points — thesis (a defensible claim taking a position on *extent*, more than a restatement); contextualization (broader context: England's mercantilist push for staple exports, the colonial labor shortage, the regions' different natural endowments, migration waves); evidence (at least 2 specific examples = 1 pt; used to support the argument = 1 pt); analysis & reasoning (comparison structures the argument — regions weighed against each other, not just described in turn = 1 pt; complexity = 1 pt).

## 1. Thesis framing

**Strong example thesis:** "Colonial economies shaped colonial societies to a great extent: the plantation South's staple crops produced a hierarchical, enslaved-labor society; New England's mixed farming and trade produced town-centered, relatively egalitarian communities; and the Middle Colonies' grain and commerce produced diverse, middling societies — though religion, founding purposes, and a shared English legal culture also left their mark, so economy explains much but not everything."

**Why it earns the point:** It runs the three-way comparison the prompt demands, maps each economy onto its social order, and takes a measured position on extent ("to a great extent... but not everything") with the qualifier named.

**What a restatement looks like (earns nothing):** "Differing economies shaped differing social structures in the English colonies." No position on extent, no regions named, no comparison actually performed.

## 2. Contextualization scope

The regional economies need their setting: England's mercantilist design pushing colonies toward staple exports; the chronic colonial labor shortage that made bound labor attractive everywhere early on; the three regions' different natural endowments — Chesapeake tobacco lands, New England's thin soils and good harbors, the Middle Colonies' fertile valleys; and successive migration waves (English, then German, Scots-Irish, and enslaved Africans). Keep the frame on the English colonies in the colonial period — no French or Spanish comparisons, no Revolution material.

## 3. Evidence areas

### The plantation South: staple crops → hierarchy
- Tobacco (and later rice and indigo) → a planter elite, a large enslaved workforce, dispersed plantations, few towns, and a sharp racial hierarchy hardened by slave codes.
- The turn to enslaved African labor was earliest and deepest here (first Africans in Virginia, 1619).

### New England: mixed economy → town society
- Thin soils → mixed farming, fishing, shipbuilding, and Atlantic trade → compact towns, town meetings, high literacy, family labor; slavery present but small-scale.

### The Middle Colonies: grain and commerce → diversity
- Fertile land → grain exports (the "bread colonies") and commerce → ethnic and religious diversity (Dutch, Germans, Quakers, Scots-Irish), prosperous middling farmers, and growing cities like Philadelphia.

### Qualifying evidence: what economy doesn't explain
- Indentured servitude was common early in *all* regions — the labor starting point was shared.
- New England's town institutions owed much to Puritan congregationalism, not just farming; and all three regions shared English patriarchy, English law, and racial slavery as an institution.

## 4. Complexity pathways

1. **Channeling vs. determining.** Economies channeled but didn't dictate: Puritanism shaped New England towns as much as farming did. A student who weighs multiple causes inside the comparison — rather than crediting economy alone — earns complexity.
2. **Comparison with a time dimension.** Regional differences were sharpest by the mid-1700s, yet all three regions were growing more commercially connected and more culturally English — the student tracks both divergence and convergence.
3. **The shared-roots paradox.** The same English migrants, law, and religion produced sharply different societies under different economic conditions — which actually strengthens the economy claim *while* acknowledging its limits, a genuinely two-sided judgment.
