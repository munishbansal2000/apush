# Rubric Map — LEQ: The First Great Awakening and National Identity, 1700–1765

**Source file:** `build/reclaim-merged/staged/princeton-25e/leq-test3.json` (id: `leq-test3-spare-a`)
**Test:** test-07
**Prompt:** "Assess how far the First Great Awakening transformed American national identity between 1700 and 1765."
**Targeted reasoning:** Continuity & Change

**Rubric reminders:** 6 points — thesis (a defensible claim taking a position on *how far* identity was transformed, more than a restatement); contextualization (broader context: declining fervor among later colonial generations, established churches holding formal monopolies, growing intercolonial print networks); evidence (at least 2 specific examples = 1 pt; used to support the argument = 1 pt); analysis & reasoning (continuity-and-change structures the argument = 1 pt; complexity = 1 pt).

## 1. Thesis framing

**Strong example thesis:** "The First Great Awakening moved colonial religious life toward a shared, emotional, intercolonial experience and taught colonists to judge authority for themselves — the beginnings of a common American outlook — but it stopped well short of creating a national identity: it split churches into Old and New Lights, stayed religious rather than political, and left colonists loyal British subjects."

**Why it earns the point:** It specifies what changed, how far it went, and where it stopped — a genuine line of reasoning the essay must defend, with change and continuity built into one claim.

**What a restatement looks like (earns nothing):** "The First Great Awakening transformed American national identity between 1700 and 1765." This asserts transformation without saying what changed, how much, or what didn't.

## 2. Contextualization scope

The revivals need their setting: religious fervor cooling among second- and third-generation colonists; established churches (Anglican in the South, Congregational in New England) holding formal monopolies; growing intercolonial print networks and travel that let a revival in one colony become news in another; Enlightenment questioning of traditional authority running in parallel. Stay inside 1700–1765 — the Revolution is outside the window and may only be gestured at, never used as evidence.

## 3. Evidence areas

### Evidence of transformation
- **Itinerant preaching across colonial lines:** George Whitefield's tours (1739–41) drawing thousands in colony after colony; Jonathan Edwards's "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God" (1741); Gilbert Tennent's attack on an unconverted ministry (1740).
- Emphasis on personal conversion and individual religious judgment, weakening deference to the established clergy.
- Growth of dissenting denominations — Baptists and Presbyterians especially — and New Light colleges such as the College of New Jersey, founded 1746.

### Evidence of limits and continuity
- **Old Lights vs. New Lights:** the revivals split congregations — the Awakening divided as much as it united.
- It remained a religious movement: no political program, no challenge to British rule; colonists still celebrated the monarchy.
- Geographic unevenness: strongest in the Middle Colonies and New England, far less felt in the South.

## 4. Complexity pathways

1. **Change and continuity together.** Religious authority was challenged while political loyalty to Britain continued untouched — the student holds both at once instead of choosing one story.
2. **Unity through division.** The same revivals that gave colonists a shared intercolonial experience also fractured their churches — a built-in qualification of the "national identity" claim, argued from the evidence rather than asserted.
3. **Judging the kind of change.** A student can argue the Awakening mattered less for what it built (no institutions of nationhood) than for the habits it taught — questioning authority, communicating across colonial lines — weighing significance rather than just measuring size.
