# Rubric Map — LEQ: Ideas of Government and the First Party System, 1780s–1790s

**Source file:** `build/reclaim-merged/staged/fivesteps-2024/exam2-leq.json` (id: `5s24-exam2-leq-02`)
**Test:** test-06
**Prompt:** "Evaluate the extent to which differing ideas about the nature of government shaped the emergence of political parties in the 1780s and 1790s."
**Targeted reasoning:** Causation

**Rubric reminders:** 6 points — thesis (a defensible claim taking a position on *extent*, more than a restatement); contextualization (broader context: the Articles of Confederation's weakness — Shays's Rebellion, 1786–87 — the Constitution's unresolved ambiguities, the French Revolution of 1789); evidence (at least 2 specific examples = 1 pt; used to support the argument = 1 pt); analysis & reasoning (causation structures the argument = 1 pt; complexity = 1 pt).

## 1. Thesis framing

**Strong example thesis:** "Differing ideas about the nature of government were the central force behind the first party system: Hamilton's vision of an energetic national government clashed with Jefferson's and Madison's insistence on strict limits, and that clash — over the Bank, assumption, and Britain versus France — organized Federalists and Democratic-Republicans; yet events and personalities sharpened divisions that ideas alone might have left abstract."

**Why it earns the point:** It takes a position ("the central force... yet events sharpened"), names the competing ideas, and identifies the mechanism by which ideas became parties (specific policy fights). The essay must now prove both halves.

**What a restatement looks like (earns nothing):** "Differing ideas about the nature of government shaped the emergence of political parties in the 1780s and 1790s." This repeats the prompt without a position on extent or any account of *how* ideas became parties.

## 2. Contextualization scope

The party fight needs its setting: the Articles of Confederation's demonstrated weakness (Shays's Rebellion, 1786–87) pushing the framers toward a stronger government; the Constitution's own unresolved ambiguity about how much power the new government really held — the Federalist/Anti-Federalist debate left the question open; and the French Revolution (1789) and its wars, which forced Americans to take sides. Keep the frame in the 1780s–90s through the election of 1800; the Confederation period is context, and Jefferson's presidency is outside except as a closing note.

## 3. Evidence areas

### The Hamiltonian vision: energetic national government
- Hamilton's financial program: the *Report on Public Credit* (1790) with assumption of state debts; the Bank of the United States (1791), defended by loose construction of the "necessary and proper" clause; a protective tariff to build manufactures.

### The Jeffersonian vision: limited government, agrarian republic
- Jefferson and Madison answered with strict construction — the Bank as unconstitutional — and a republic of independent farmers in which states' rights guarded liberty.

### Where ideas became parties
- **Foreign policy:** Federalists tilted toward Britain (Jay's Treaty, 1794); Democratic-Republicans toward France (the Citizen Genêt affair, 1793); the Quasi-War with France (1798–1800) deepened the split.
- **Federal power vs. liberty at home:** the Whiskey Rebellion (1794) and Washington's show of federal force; the Alien and Sedition Acts (1798) answered by the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions (1798–99).
- **The election of 1800:** the first transfer of power between rival parties — Jefferson's "Revolution of 1800" — proving the party system now organized national politics.

### Qualifying evidence
- Ideas alone don't explain everything: regional economic interests (merchants vs. farmers) and personal rivalries (Hamilton vs. Jefferson) also drove alignment.

## 4. Complexity pathways

1. **Multiple causation.** Ideas, interests, and events each did work: the Bank fight was simultaneously constitutional, economic, and personal. A student who shows the three strands braided together — rather than crediting ideas alone — earns complexity.
2. **The anti-party irony.** The very men who warned against parties built them; Federalists who championed order passed the repressive Alien and Sedition Acts, handing Jefferson the liberty issue. Contradictions inside each camp deepen the argument.
3. **Principles under pressure.** Madison co-wrote *The Federalist* defending energetic government, then co-authored the Virginia Resolution defending states' rights a decade later. A student can argue that ideas shaped the parties but events reshaped the ideas — causation running both ways.
