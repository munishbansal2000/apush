# Rubric Map — DBQ: U.S. Foreign Policy in Latin America, 1899–1917

**Source file:** `build/reclaim-merged/staged/fivesteps-2024/exam1-dbq.json` (id: `5s24-exam1-dbq`)
**Test:** test-06
**Prompt:** "Analyze the effects of American foreign policy in Latin America during the period 1899–1917."

**Rubric reminders:** thesis must make a claim about the *effects* (on Latin America, on the U.S., or both — pick a line of reasoning); sourcing on at least 2 docs; evidence from at least 3 docs (argument on at least 6); at least one specific beyond-docs fact. Note: Doc 4 is an original illustration in period style, produced for this item — cite it as an illustration of the argument, not as a period artifact.

## 1. Sourcing opportunities per document

- **Doc 1 (Platt Amendment, 1903):** A congressional amendment imposed on Cuba as the price of American withdrawal; its purpose is to legalize future U.S. intervention. The word "consents" does the sourcing work — Cuba's consent was coerced (no Platt, no withdrawal). A student can analyze the legal form masking domination: a protectorate written in treaty language.
- **Doc 2 (Hay-Bunau-Varilla Treaty, Nov 18, 1903):** A treaty signed days after Panama's U.S.-backed secession from Colombia — but Panama's "representative" was a French canal-company agent. The signatory's identity is the story: the treaty was negotiated with the beneficiary, not the new nation. A student can use that to argue U.S. policy manufactured a compliant client state — intervention by treaty rather than troops.
- **Doc 3 (Roosevelt Corollary, Dec 1904):** The president announcing doctrine to Congress as national policy. The language — "chronic wrongdoing," "civilized nation," "international police power" — claims a unilateral right to police the hemisphere. As an annual message it is a public declaration, the era's thesis statement; a student can source it as the ideological engine driving the period's interventions.
- **Doc 4 ("The Full Dinner Pail" cartoon):** An original illustration in 1900s style: Uncle Sam at a banquet of dishes labeled Cuba, Puerto Rico, Panama, and Nicaragua, while a "U.S. investor" carves the roast and "U.S. Marines" wait as waiters. The carving investor is the angle — the economic motive visualized, with force standing ready to serve it. Pairs naturally with Docs 5 and 6 on dollar diplomacy.
- **Doc 5 (Taft, Dec 1912):** The president defending his record to Congress, presenting dollar diplomacy as peaceful, modern statecraft. "Substituting dollars for bullets" is Taft's own euphemism, and the defensive tone of a fourth annual message shows he knew the policy was controversial. A student can take him at his word (policy aimed at trade) and then test that claim against Doc 6's critique.
- **Doc 6 (Winslow, Anti-Imperialist League):** An activist warning the American public that authorizing the U.S. to collect Honduran and Nicaraguan revenue "in the interest of American capitalists" invites endless intervention. The counter-voice matters: even contemporaries saw through the benevolence frame. The mechanism he names — finance becoming control — lets a student argue dollar diplomacy eroded sovereignty by design, not accident.
- **Doc 7 (Wilson, Apr 20, 1914):** The president asking Congress to authorize force after the Tampico incident, framing it as vindicating American "rights and dignity." The angle: even the "moral diplomacy" president used military intervention. A student can source this to show continuity of interventionism across administrations despite sharply different rhetoric.

## 2. Beyond-the-documents evidence

- **The Panama Canal (completed 1914)** — the strategic prize of Docs 2–3: a decade of construction under U.S. control, the hemisphere's great engineering and power-projection fact.
- **The occupation of Veracruz (April–November 1914)** — Wilson's Marines seizing a Mexican port after Tampico; the Doc 7 request made real, and a humiliation Mexicans long remembered.
- **The U.S. occupation of Haiti (1915–1934)** — Marines taking the country after political chaos, running its finances and constabulary; the Corollary's logic extended.
- **The U.S. occupation of the Dominican Republic (1916–1924)** — same pattern: customs receivership backed by troops.
- **Pershing's Punitive Expedition (1916–1917)** — 10,000 U.S. troops chasing Pancho Villa into Mexico and failing to catch him; intervention's limits on display.

## 3. Complexity pathways

1. **Continuity across administrations.** Roosevelt (the Corollary), Taft (dollars), Wilson (morality — plus Marines): different labels, same interventionism. A student can argue the rhetoric changed more than the policy — a strong "effects" thesis is that Latin America experienced one continuous American protectorate policy wearing three presidents' slogans.
2. **Effects cut both ways.** By its own lights U.S. policy brought order, investment, and the canal (Docs 2–5); by Latin American lights it cost sovereignty and bred lasting resentment (Docs 1, 6, and the occupations). A student can split "effects" into effects *on Latin America* vs. effects *on the United States* (strategic gains, but also entanglements and anti-Americanism) — analyzing the asymmetry is the complexity.
3. **Economic vs. strategic motives.** Weigh the investor's carving knife (Doc 4) against the canal-and-strategy motive (Docs 2–3): a student can argue the two reinforced rather than competed with each other — the canal served both commerce and naval power — and that the Anti-Imperialist League's error (Doc 6) was treating finance as the whole story.
