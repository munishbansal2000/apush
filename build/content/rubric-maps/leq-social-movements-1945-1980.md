# Rubric Map — LEQ: Social Movements, 1945–1980

**Source file:** `build/reclaim-merged/staged/princeton-25e/leq-test1.json` (id: `leq-test1-main`)
**Test:** test-05
**Prompt:** "Assess how far social movements reshaped American society between 1945 and 1980."
**Targeted reasoning:** Continuity & Change

**Rubric reminders:** 6 points — thesis (a defensible claim taking a position on *how far* society was reshaped, more than a restatement); contextualization (broader context: postwar prosperity and the GI Bill, Cold War pressure on American racism, television carrying protest into living rooms); evidence (at least 2 specific examples = 1 pt; used to support the argument = 1 pt); analysis & reasoning (continuity-and-change structures the argument = 1 pt; complexity = 1 pt).

## 1. Thesis framing

**Strong example thesis:** "Between 1945 and 1980, social movements fundamentally reshaped American society — the civil rights movement dismantled legal segregation, the women's movement expanded equality in education and work, and the counterculture challenged traditional norms — though each advance met backlash, and legal change consistently outran social change."

**Why it earns the point:** It names specific movements and specific changes, takes a position on extent ("fundamentally reshaped... though"), and builds the continuity-and-change tension (law vs. lived experience) into the claim itself.

**What a restatement looks like (earns nothing):** "Social movements reshaped American society between 1945 and 1980." No position on how far, no named movements, no line of reasoning.

## 2. Contextualization scope

The movements need their moment: postwar prosperity and the GI Bill expanding education and suburbs; WWII's democratic rhetoric raising expectations at home; Cold War competition making American racism a diplomatic liability; television carrying images of protest into living rooms for the first time. Stay inside 1945–1980 — context is the immediate postwar setting, not the 19th century. The 1980 election can close the story; Reagan's presidency is outside the window.

## 3. Evidence areas

### Evidence movements reshaped society
- **Civil rights:** *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954), the Montgomery bus boycott (1955–56), the Civil Rights Act (1964), and the Voting Rights Act (1965) — the legal architecture of Jim Crow dismantled.
- **Women's movement:** Betty Friedan's *The Feminine Mystique* (1963), the National Organization for Women (1966), Title IX (1972), and *Roe v. Wade* (1973) — equality pressed into education, work, and reproductive rights.
- **Broader ferment:** the antiwar movement, Earth Day and the EPA (1970), Stonewall (1969) and gay rights organizing, the American Indian Movement, and the Chicano movement.

### Evidence of continuity and limits
- **Backlash:** Nixon's "silent majority" appeal (1969), white flight and suburban resistance to busing, the Equal Rights Amendment's failure to win ratification, and the conservative coalition behind Reagan's 1980 victory.
- Legal victories did not end de facto segregation or close the wage gap — change in the law, continuity in lived experience.

## 4. Complexity pathways

1. **Old ideals, new claimants.** The movements drew on older American traditions — the Declaration's equality, earlier suffrage and labor organizing — to demand new change. Continuity of ideals, change in who could claim them: both at once.
2. **Backlash as part of the story, not the epilogue.** Resistance wasn't separate from the movements; their very success mobilized the opposition that limited them. A student who treats backlash as caused by the movements' gains is doing causal complexity.
3. **Uneven change across movements.** Civil rights remade the law faster than the women's movement remade the workplace — comparing the movements' different trajectories lets the student qualify "how far" with precision instead of one blanket verdict.
