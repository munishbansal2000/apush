# Rubric Map — DBQ: The Supreme Court and Progressive Regulation, 1885–1920

**Source file:** `build/reclaim-merged/staged/fivesteps-2024/exam2-dbq.json` (id: `5s24-exam2-dbq`)
**Test:** test-10
**Prompt:** "To what extent did the Supreme Court advance or inhibit Progressive regulation of corporations in the period 1885–1920?"

**Rubric reminders:** thesis must weigh *advance vs. inhibit* (a one-sided answer is fine if defended, but the docs support both); sourcing on at least 2 docs; evidence from at least 3 docs (argument on at least 4); at least one specific beyond-docs fact. The docs run chronologically — a student can trace the doctrine's movement from 1895 to 1917.

## 1. Sourcing opportunities per document

- **Doc 1 (In re Debs, 1895):** The Court upholding Eugene Debs's jailing for the Pullman boycott, reading the Sherman Act broadly. The asymmetry is the story: the Sherman Act's first great use was *against a union*, not a trust. A student can source the irony — the statute progressives hoped would tame corporations debuted as a labor weapon — to argue the Court inhibited reform's allies before it ever touched reform's targets.
- **Doc 2 (U.S. v. E. C. Knight, 1895):** The Court voiding the Sugar Trust breakup by separating "manufacture" from "commerce." The legal distinction is the evidence: by defining monopoly out of federal reach, the Court gutted the Sherman Act against the era's great trusts. A student can analyze the formalism as a shield — doctrine doing political work — making this the leading "inhibited" case.
- **Doc 3 (Smyth v. Ames, 1898):** The Court striking down Nebraska's railroad-rate law and settling that corporations are Fourteenth Amendment "persons." The repurposing is the angle: a Reconstruction amendment meant to protect freedpeople, redirected to shield corporations from state regulation. A student can use it to argue the Court blocked reform at the state level, forcing progressives toward the harder federal route.
- **Doc 4 (Lochner v. New York, 1905):** The Court voiding New York's bakers'-hours law as "an illegal interference" with liberty of contract. "The act is not a health law" — the Court substituting its own judgment for the legislature's about what counts as health regulation. A student can source this as the signature case of judicial obstruction: Lochner-era doctrine blocked Progressive labor laws for a generation.
- **Doc 5 (Muller v. Oregon, 1908):** The Court upholding Oregon's women's-hours law on explicitly paternalist grounds ("the two sexes differ"), "without questioning" Lochner. The reasoning is the point: regulation survived only when framed around women's difference, not workers' rights generally. A student can argue this "advance" came wrapped in inequality — and note the famous Brandeis brief (sociological evidence) as the method that won it.
- **Doc 6 (Standard Oil, 1911):** The Court dissolving Standard Oil — but inventing the "rule of reason," reaching only "unreasonable" restraints of trade. The double edge is the angle: the breakup is the headline, but the rule narrowed the Sherman Act going forward. A student can use it as a victory that contained future victories — the pivot point of the "advance" story.
- **Doc 7 (Wilson v. New, 1917):** The Court upholding the Adamson Act's eight-hour railroad day under the commerce clause. Compare with Doc 2's cramped commerce clause: the distance traveled between 1895 and 1917 *is* the student's change-over-time evidence. The wartime context (1917, a threatened national railroad strike) explains the deference — a student can source the timing to argue doctrine followed emergency as much as principle.

## 2. Beyond-the-documents evidence

- **Northern Securities v. United States (1904)** — the Court dissolving the great railroad holding company; Roosevelt's trust-busting win, pairing with Doc 6.
- **The Hepburn Act (1906)** — Congress giving the ICC real rate-setting power over railroads; the legislative advance the Court in Doc 3 had made necessary.
- **The Clayton Antitrust Act and the Federal Trade Commission Act (1914)** — Wilson-era statutes rebuilding antitrust on the wreckage of Doc 2's formalism.
- **Hammer v. Dagenhart (1918)** — the Court striking down the federal child-labor law; proof the Court was *still* inhibiting Progressive regulation at the period's end — the essential qualifier for any "advance" thesis.
- **The Adamson Act (1916)** — the eight-hour railroad law behind Doc 7, passed under strike threat; shows Congress acting where the Court once blocked.

## 3. Complexity pathways

1. **Inhibition that shaped progressivism.** Argue the Court inhibited more than it advanced — but that the obstruction *shaped* the movement: Docs 2–4 forced reformers to build the federal administrative state (ICC, FTC) and to draft around doctrine. The "inhibit" story contains the "advance" story, since progressivism's institutions were designed against judicial resistance.
2. **The asymmetry of doctrine.** The Court was activist for corporations and against unions (Docs 1, 3), restrained only selectively (Docs 5–7): liberty of contract for business, police power for favored regulation. A student can argue the pattern wasn't judicial restraint vs. activism but *whose* interests the activism served — the inconsistency is the argument.
3. **A Court that moved with politics.** 1895–1905 reads as a wall (Docs 1–4); 1908–1917 as cracks, then breakthrough (Docs 5–7). A student can argue judicial doctrine followed political pressure — Roosevelt's trust-busting, Wilson's legislation, wartime emergency — as much as it led it, and qualify "the Court" as not monolithic across 35 years. Hammer v. Dagenhart (1918) then caps the nuance: the old Court wasn't gone.
