# Rubric Map — DBQ: Slavery and National Unity, 1844–1861

**Source file:** `build/reclaim-merged/staged/princeton-25e/dbq-test3.json` (id: `dbq-test3`)
**Test:** test-05
**Prompt:** "Evaluate the extent to which slavery challenged American national unity in the period from 1844 to 1861."

**Rubric reminders:** thesis must judge the *extent* of the challenge; sourcing on at least 2 docs; evidence from at least 3 docs (argument on at least 6); at least one specific beyond-docs fact. Note: Docs 4, 6, 7 are image descriptions — cite what they show accurately.

## 1. Sourcing opportunities per document

- **Doc 1 (O'Sullivan, "Annexation," July 1845):** A Democratic editor promoting Texas annexation to magazine readers under the banner of "manifest destiny." The providential language ("Providence," "yearly multiplying millions") is the angle: a slavery-expanding act sold as national destiny. A student can source the audience (magazine readers = public opinion-shaping) to argue expansionism and slavery were entangled from the start — the unity challenge began as a *sales pitch*.
- **Doc 2 (Polk's war message, May 11, 1846):** The president addressing Congress to justify war with Mexico. "War exists by the act of Mexico" is a president manufacturing casus belli for an audience that must vote men and money. A student can analyze the framing as executive drive for the very territory that would become the slavery-extension battleground — the war's purpose matters less than what its spoils forced the nation to decide.
- **Doc 3 (Wilmot, House speech, Feb 8, 1847):** A *Northern Democrat* demanding slavery be barred from the Mexican War territories. The party label is the sourcing gold: a Democrat breaking with a Democratic president's expansion shows slavery splitting parties along sectional lines. His concession — "I was in favor of the annexation of Texas" — shows even pro-expansion Northerners drew a line at extension, which a student can use as direct evidence of slavery challenging unity inside national institutions.
- **Doc 4 (Kansas-Nebraska map, 1854):** A cartographer's illustration of popular sovereignty's reach, with the Missouri Compromise line erased. The map's visual argument is the angle: a student can read it as the nation geographically partitioned by the slavery question — free, slave, and "open" territories color-coded. As a summarizing illustration, it shows how one act nationalized the conflict.
- **Doc 5 (Taney, Dred Scott, 1857):** The Chief Justice writing for the Court to the nation, intending to settle slavery judicially. The sweeping claim — "no word can be found in the Constitution" limiting slave property — shows overreach, and the *failure* of the settlement is the angle: a student can argue that even the highest legal authority could not contain the conflict, so unity could not be imposed from above. The decision outraged the North instead.
- **Doc 6 (railroad maps, 1850 and 1860):** Paired maps of rail networks — dense northern integration vs. sparse southern lines. The comparison is the evidence: economic development itself followed sectional lines, giving the political split a material base. A student can source this as the structural underpinning — the sections were growing apart economically even before they split politically.
- **Doc 7 ("Prospect of a Smash Up" cartoon, 1860):** Republican-leaning press satirizing the Democrats' sectional split (Douglas/Johnson vs. Breckinridge/Lane) for voters. The train-vs.-wagon metaphor and the "smash up" warning are contemporary visual proof that slavery shattered the national party system on the eve of secession. A student can note the satire's audience — this was public, electoral knowledge, not insider gossip.

## 2. Beyond-the-documents evidence

- **The Compromise of 1850 (including the Fugitive Slave Act)** — the great attempted settlement; the Fugitive Slave Act's enforcement in the North (personal-liberty laws in response) showed compromise deepening the crisis.
- **"Bleeding Kansas" (1854–1859)** — popular sovereignty producing actual warfare between pro- and anti-slavery settlers; the Doc 4 map's policy made violent.
- **The caning of Charles Sumner (May 1856)** — a South Carolina congressman beating a Massachusetts senator on the Senate floor; sectional hatred made physical inside the Capitol.
- **John Brown's raid on Harpers Ferry (October 1859)** — the South read it as proof the North meant war on slavery; the North made Brown a martyr; mutual radicalization.
- **The election of 1860 and secession winter** — Lincoln's victory without a single southern electoral vote, South Carolina's secession (December 1860): the unity challenge's endpoint.

## 3. Complexity pathways

1. **Slavery as cause vs. slavery as entanglement.** Argue slavery challenged unity most where it intersected other forces — territorial expansion (Docs 1–3), economic divergence (Doc 6) — or argue the reverse: slavery was the master cause and expansion merely its vehicle. Either way, the point is earned by weighing the relationship, not by listing events.
2. **The failed-settlement pattern.** The Compromise of 1850, Kansas-Nebraska, and Dred Scott were each meant to settle the question, and each made things worse. A student can structure the essay around that pattern — every "solution" deepened the crisis — which itself demonstrates how fundamental the challenge to unity was.
3. **Chronic tension turned acute.** Sectional division predated 1844 (the Missouri Compromise of 1820, the Nullification Crisis of 1832–33). A student can argue slavery did not create disunity from nothing in 1844; the period turned a chronic sectional tension into an acute crisis — a change-over-time argument *within* the prompt's dates that qualifies the "extent."
