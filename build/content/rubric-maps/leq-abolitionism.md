# Rubric Map — LEQ: Abolitionism, 1830–1860

**Source file:** `build/reclaim-merged/staged/princeton-25e/leq-drill.json` (id: `leq-drill-main`)
**Test:** test-08
**Prompt:** "Assess how far the abolitionist movement fostered change in America between 1830 and 1860."
**Targeted reasoning:** Causation

**Rubric reminders:** 6 points — thesis (a defensible claim taking a position on *how far* change went, more than a restatement); contextualization (broader context: the Second Great Awakening's moral urgency, the cotton boom entrenching slavery, westward expansion reopening the slavery-extension question, the Missouri Compromise of 1820 as the last settlement); evidence (at least 2 specific examples = 1 pt; used to support the argument = 1 pt); analysis & reasoning (causation structures the argument = 1 pt; complexity = 1 pt).

## 1. Thesis framing

**Strong example thesis:** "Between 1830 and 1860, the abolitionist movement forced slavery onto the national agenda — building mass antislavery sentiment through print and petition, feeding new political parties, and provoking a proslavery counterattack — yet it freed almost no enslaved people directly and hardened Southern defenses of slavery, so its great change was political rather than immediate."

**Why it earns the point:** It names the mechanism (print, petition, parties), the counter-effect (hardened Southern defenses), and the precise *kind* of change — a line of reasoning the essay must follow, not just a verdict.

**What a restatement looks like (earns nothing):** "The abolitionist movement fostered change in America between 1830 and 1860." No position on how far, no account of what kind of change, nothing to defend.

## 2. Contextualization scope

The movement needs its setting: the Second Great Awakening's moral urgency, which turned reform into a religious duty; the cotton boom of the 1830s–50s making slavery more profitable and more entrenched even as abolitionism rose; westward expansion constantly reopening the question of slavery's extension; and the Missouri Compromise (1820) as the last great settlement the movement would shatter. Stay inside 1830–1860 — the Civil War and emancipation are outside the window and can only be a closing glance, not evidence.

## 3. Evidence areas

### Evidence abolitionists caused change
- **Moral agitation:** William Lloyd Garrison's *The Liberator* (1831) and the American Anti-Slavery Society (1833) — immediatism spread through mass print and petition campaigns.
- **Making slavery concrete:** Frederick Douglass's *Narrative* (1845) and the *North Star* (1847), slave narratives generally, and the Underground Railroad (Harriet Tubman most famously active in the 1850s) brought slavery's reality before Northern readers.
- **Political fallout:** the gag rule (1836–44) proving abolitionist petitions rattled Congress; mob violence against abolitionists (Elijah Lovejoy murdered, 1837); antislavery carried into electoral politics by the Liberty Party (1840), the Free Soil Party (1848), and the Republican Party (1854).

### Evidence of limits and backlash
- Slavery grew: cotton-kingdom expansion, the Fugitive Slave Act (1850), and *Dred Scott* (1857).
- Southern ideology hardened: Calhoun's "positive good" defense (1837), proslavery mobs and laws.
- Most white Northerners — even opponents of slavery's extension — did not embrace abolitionist racial egalitarianism.

## 4. Complexity pathways

1. **One cause, opposite effects by region.** Abolitionism changed the North and provoked the South — the same movement simultaneously advanced antislavery and deepened the sectional divide that led to war. Regional divergence as complexity.
2. **The change they wanted vs. the change they caused.** Abolitionists sought immediate emancipation and got, instead, a political crisis over slavery's extension — distinguishing intended from actual effects is a sophisticated causal judgment.
3. **Qualifying "change" precisely.** The movement's victories were in sentiment and politics, not in freeing people — a student who draws that line exactly, rather than claiming total success or total failure, earns complexity through precision.
