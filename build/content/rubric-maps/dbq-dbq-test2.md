# Rubric Map — DBQ: American Imperialism, 1890–1945

**Source file:** `build/reclaim-merged/staged/princeton-25e/dbq-test2.json` (id: `dbq-test2`)
**Test:** test-09
**Prompt:** "Evaluate the extent to which the American state can be described as imperialist from 1890 to 1945."

**Rubric reminders:** thesis must take a position on the *label* "imperialist" — full, partial, or qualified — and defend it across the period; sourcing on at least 2 docs; evidence from at least 3 docs (argument on at least 4); at least one specific beyond-docs fact. Doc 3 (1848) predates the prompt — usable for continuity/contrast, not as evidence of 1890–1945 policy.

## 1. Sourcing opportunities per document

- **Doc 1 (Adams to Schurz, Dec 21, 1898):** An elite anti-imperialist writing privately to a fellow anti-imperialist against Philippine annexation. The audience (the converted) means the letter's value is its *appeal to American precedent* — Mexico, Venezuela — rather than European models. A student can source it to show the anti-imperialist case was argued from American tradition, and use the private form as evidence of candid strategy among the opposition.
- **Doc 2 (Lenin, Imperialism, 1917):** A Marxist revolutionary's theoretical work for socialist readers, arguing American expansion is capitalist imperialism. The hostile-external-witness angle cuts two ways: he supports the imperialist label, but he *needs* America to fit his theory, so a student should note the ideological lens. Bonus: he quotes Lincoln and the American anti-imperialists — even the attack on U.S. empire borrows American ideals.
- **Doc 3 (Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, 1848):** The formal treaty text settling the Mexican-American War — conquest with a $15 million payment clause. A student can analyze the form-vs.-substance gap: purchase language covering conquest. Its use is comparative: it establishes the pre-1890 pattern so the prompt's period can be judged as continuity (same playbook, new ocean) or contrast.
- **Doc 4 (McCutcheon cartoon, 1914):** Pro-intervention press celebrating uplift — before/after panels of the Philippines, Hawaii, Puerto Rico, Cuba, and Panama transformed by American intervention. The before/after structure *is* the imperial self-image: intervention as liberation. A student can read it against Docs 2 and 5 to show how empire was sold domestically as a civilizing mission.
- **Doc 5 (Buell, 1900):** An American colonial administrator promoting "benevolent assimilation" to the public. The paternalism is the evidence — "proper care and direction," Filipinos to be "won over to a brotherly regard." Even the praise ("intelligent and polite people") is framed as justifying tutelage. A student can quote the author's own words to reveal the imperial mindset without needing hostile commentary.
- **Doc 6 (U.S. Census, 1940):** Federal statistics with no argumentative motive: the Philippines at 16,356,000 people, plus 2,477,023 more across Puerto Rico, Hawaii, Alaska, Guam, and other possessions — over 18 million colonial subjects in 1940. A student can source the numbers' neutrality of purpose: hard quantitative evidence that the United States *remained* an imperial power at the prompt's endpoint, whatever the rhetoric said.
- **Doc 7 (Gillam cartoon, 1899):** A pro-expansion magazine urging McKinley to "finish the canal" and "make our national expansion complete," for mass readers. The popular-enthusiasm angle: imperialism as a celebrated national project, not just elite policy. The shovel-and-rifles imagery fuses commerce and force — a student can use it to argue expansion had a genuine popular constituency.

## 2. Beyond-the-documents evidence

- **The Spanish-American War (1898)** — the seizure of the Philippines, Puerto Rico, and Guam; the war that created the overseas empire the DBQ debates.
- **The Philippine-American War (1899–1902)** — the brutal suppression of Filipino independence (Aguinaldo's republic crushed); the reality behind Doc 4's uplift panels.
- **The annexation of Hawaii (1898)** — via the Newlands Resolution after the 1893 overthrow of the Hawaiian monarchy; empire by joint resolution.
- **The Open Door notes (1899–1900)** — Hay's demand for equal commercial access in China; the informal, commercial mode of American empire.
- **The Tydings-McDuffie Act (1934)** — Congress promising Philippine independence in 1946; the empire scheduling its own partial retreat.
- **The Good Neighbor Policy (1933)** — Roosevelt's renunciation of intervention; Marines withdrawn from Nicaragua (1933) and Haiti (1934); the 1930s turn away from the Doc 7 swagger.

## 3. Complexity pathways

1. **Imperialism with American characteristics.** Argue the label fits but needs qualification: the U.S. practiced a distinctive imperialism — informal and commercial (Open Door, dollar diplomacy) alongside formal colonies, wrapped in anti-colonial rhetoric (Docs 1–2 quoting American ideals), and eventually promising decolonization (Tydings-McDuffie). "Imperialist, but in an American mode" is a defensible, nuanced thesis.
2. **Change over time within the period.** The high-water mark (1898–1917, Docs 4 and 7) vs. the 1930s retrenchment (Good Neighbor, Tydings-McDuffie) — yet Doc 6 shows the empire persisted quantitatively in 1940. A student can argue the *form* of empire changed more than the *fact*: from interventionist swagger to managed, quieter dominion.
3. **Self-perception vs. reality.** Americans denied being imperialists while governing 18 million colonial subjects (Docs 4–5 vs. Doc 6) — the contradiction itself is the complexity. A student can argue the denial mattered: the anti-imperialist tradition (Doc 1) acted as a real constraint, producing a less annexationist empire than the European kind, without making it any less an empire.
