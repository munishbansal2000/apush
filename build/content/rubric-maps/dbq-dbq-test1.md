# Rubric Map — DBQ: U.S. Foreign Policy, 1914–1917

**Source file:** `build/reclaim-merged/staged/princeton-25e/dbq-test1.json` (id: `dbq-test1`)
**Test:** test-08
**Prompt:** "Evaluate the extent to which the United States foreign policy changed between 1914 and 1917."

**Rubric reminders:** thesis must judge the *extent* of change (continuity-and-change reasoning); sourcing on at least 2 docs; evidence from at least 3 docs (argument on at least 4); at least one specific beyond-docs fact. The docs run in chronological order — a student can trace the arc from Doc 1's neutrality to Doc 7's ultimatum.

## 1. Sourcing opportunities per document

- **Doc 1 (Wilson, Aug 19, 1914):** The president addressing Congress and the public — including immigrant groups — at the war's outbreak. Purpose: rally domestic unity behind neutrality and claim the mediator's role. The domestic audience is the angle: "drawn from many nations" explains why neutrality was also a domestic-peace strategy. This is the official baseline against which all later change is measured.
- **Doc 2 (Münsterberg to Wilson, Nov 19, 1914):** A German-American Harvard professor writing *privately* to the president to complain that American neutrality favors Britain in practice (cables vs. wireless, contraband rules, munitions sales). The private-letter form means candid grievance, and his disclaimer — he speaks for disturbed German-Americans, "in no way" for official Germany — shows the defensive framing dissent required. A student can use it to argue neutrality was contested at home and pro-Allied in effect.
- **Doc 3 (Lansing, War Memoirs, 1935):** The wartime (acting) secretary of state's retrospective, written twenty years later for general readers. The memoir caveat applies — hindsight and self-justification shape it — but as an insider's account of Britain's blockade strangling American trade, it documents the economic pressure tilting the U.S. toward the Allies. A student can source it carefully: valuable for what happened, discounted for why.
- **Doc 4 (Bryan to Senator Stone, Jan 20, 1915):** The secretary of state answering a Senate committee's charges of partiality point by point. The numbered legalistic rebuttal is the administration's paper trail of "neutrality" — and its very defensiveness (twenty answered charges) betrays the pressure. A student can use the form to argue continuity of *rhetoric* amid changing practice: the law said neutral while the trade figures said otherwise.
- **Doc 5 (German Embassy notice, May 1, 1915):** The German government warning Americans off British ships via the New York Times, days before the Lusitania sinking. Purpose: establish legal cover; audience: travelers. The timing is the angle: Germany was building its justification for submarine warfare in public, and a student can read it as the escalation mechanism — German actions pushing the U.S. from mediation toward confrontation.
- **Doc 6 (Customs inspector's report, 1915):** A bureaucratic inventory of the Lusitania's cargo — 5,468 cases of ammunition consigned to the British government. With no argumentative motive, the dry numbers are hard evidence: American trade massively favored the Allies. A student can source the document's neutrality of purpose to puncture the official neutrality claim — this is the "change beneath the surface" doc.
- **Doc 7 (Wilson to Congress, Sussex ultimatum):** The president threatening to sever diplomatic relations unless Germany abandoned submarine warfare against merchant vessels — the shift from impartial mediator to coercive moralist ("the responsible spokesmen of the rights of humanity"). A student can use the "rights of humanity" language as the endpoint of the rhetorical arc from Doc 1. **Date note:** the staged file dates this March 24, 1916 — that is the date the Sussex was torpedoed; Wilson's address to Congress was April 19, 1916 (confirmed: the Sussex was torpedoed March 24; Wilson's ultimatum address followed April 19).

## 2. Beyond-the-documents evidence

- **The sinking of the Lusitania (May 7, 1915)** — 1,198 dead including 128 Americans; the Doc 5–6 warning made catastrophe, and the event that made "neutrality" a harder sell.
- **The Sussex Pledge (May 1916)** — Germany's promise to restrict submarine warfare after Wilson's ultimatum (Doc 7); the crisis's temporary resolution.
- **The Zimmermann Telegram (January 1917, published March 1)** — Germany's offer of a Mexican alliance against the U.S.; the revelation that destroyed the last domestic case for neutrality.
- **Germany's resumption of unrestricted submarine warfare (February 1, 1917)** — the decision that forced Wilson's hand.
- **The U.S. declaration of war (April 6, 1917)** — the endpoint: from "impartial mediation" to belligerency in under three years.

## 3. Complexity pathways

1. **Rhetoric vs. practice.** Argue the real change happened in *practice* — trade and finance tilting to the Allies (Docs 2, 6), coercive diplomacy (Doc 7) — while *rhetoric* stayed "neutral" until 1917. Or invert it: the economic interests were constant, and what changed was the moral framing (mediator → "rights of humanity"). Either way, the point is earned by separating words from deeds and weighing which changed more.
2. **Who changed whom.** Analyze German actions (Docs 5, 7) as the driver — American policy changed reactively, each step an answer to a provocation. Then test the structural counter-argument: with trade and loans binding the U.S. to the Allies (Doc 6), intervention was likely regardless of German provocations. Weighing agency vs. structure is genuine complexity.
3. **Change that was real but late and reluctant.** Note the continuities: formal neutrality held until April 1917, Wilson genuinely pursued mediation (the House missions of 1915–16), and Bryan's legalism (Doc 4) shows an administration trying to *avoid* change. A student can argue the change was profound but compressed into the final months — an "extent" thesis with a timeline.
