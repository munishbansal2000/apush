# Rubric Map — LEQ: Military Conflicts and American National Identity, 1754–1877

**Source file:** `build/reclaim-merged/staged/barrons-2027/pt2-leq.json` (id: `barrons-2027-pt2-leq`)
**Test:** test-02
**Prompt:** "From the French and Indian War through the Civil War, Britain's North American colonies and then the United States fought a series of military conflicts. Explain how participation in these conflicts influenced American national identity in the period from 1754 to 1877."
**Targeted reasoning:** Causation

**Rubric reminders:** 6 points — thesis (a defensible claim establishing a line of reasoning about how military conflict shaped identity, more than a restatement); contextualization (broader context: Anglo-French imperial struggle and British war debt before the Revolution; Enlightenment ideas; Manifest Destiny and sectionalism before the Civil War); evidence (at least 2 specific examples = 1 pt; used to support the argument = 1 pt); analysis & reasoning (causation structures the argument = 1 pt; complexity = 1 pt).

## 1. Thesis framing

**Strong example thesis:** "The French and Indian War and the American Revolution began transforming colonial identity: British victory removed the French threat, and then Britain's postwar taxes and restrictions made many colonists question their allegiance, so that by 1776 a growing number thought of themselves as Americans rather than Britons — though a substantial Loyalist minority did not. The Civil War completed the transformation in a different key: it forged the 'United States' as a singular nation rather than a compact of states, and emancipation recast the national experiment as a test of whether the Declaration's promise of equality could be made real."

**Why it earns the point:** It explains *how* conflict caused identity change (removal of the French threat, then alienation by British policy, then Civil War nationalism) across the full span, and it qualifies the claim (Loyalists; "in a different key") instead of asserting one smooth story.

**What a restatement looks like (earns nothing):** "Participation in military conflicts influenced American national identity from 1754 to 1877." This repeats the prompt without explaining the mechanism or taking a position on how far identity changed.

## 2. Contextualization scope

The conflicts need their settings: the Anglo-French imperial struggle for North America and the British war debt that followed the 1763 victory; Enlightenment ideas of liberty and self-government circulating in the colonies; and, before the Civil War, Manifest Destiny and the sectional conflict over slavery. Keep context adjacent to the wars — pre-1763 imperial rivalry, the post-1763 debt crisis — not deep background like the 1607 founding, and nothing after 1877.

## 3. Evidence areas

### Evidence conflicts built a shared American identity
- **French and Indian War (1754–63):** shared colonial military experience fighting alongside (and resenting) British regulars; then the Proclamation of 1763, the Stamp Act (1765), and other postwar taxes alienated colonists from British identity.
- **American Revolution:** the Declaration of Independence (1776) articulating an "American" people distinct from Britons.
- **War of 1812:** a nationalist surge — "The Star-Spangled Banner" (1814), the Era of Good Feelings, and the collapse of the Federalist Party after the Hartford Convention (1814–15).
- **Civil War (1861–65):** emancipation and Lincoln's Gettysburg Address (1863) recasting the nation as a test of equality; the grammatical and political shift from "the United States are" to "the United States is."

### Evidence identity change was contested or incomplete
- **Loyalists** in the Revolution: a substantial minority rejected the new American identity — change was fought over, not universal.
- **Mexican-American War (1846–48):** expansion debates tied national identity to the slavery-extension crisis (the Wilmot Proviso, 1846) — the war divided the nation's self-image along sectional lines.
- The Civil War itself: the conflict that forged singular nationhood was a war Americans fought *against each other*.

## 4. Complexity pathways

1. **Wars divided as well as united.** Loyalists in the Revolution, sectional fury over the Mexican War, the Civil War — identity was forged through internal conflict, not just against foreign enemies. A student who argues unity and division together earns complexity.
2. **Whose identity?** Each expansion of "American" identity came with exclusions — Native peoples displaced, enslaved Black Americans uncounted. Qualifying *who* the new identity included turns a simple story into a layered one.
3. **Continuity inside the change.** British political traditions — common law, representative government — persisted even as allegiance shifted. Americans kept British constitutional ideas while rejecting British rule: change in loyalty, continuity in political culture.
