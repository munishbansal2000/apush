# Rubric Map — DBQ: Federalist Administrations, 1789–1801

**Source file:** `build/reclaim-merged/staged/fivesteps-2024/ch03-dbq.json` (id: `5s24-ch03-dbq`)
**Test:** test-01
**Prompt:** "To what extent did the Federalist administrations of George Washington and John Adams promote national unity and advance the authority of the federal government in the period 1789–1801?"

**Rubric reminders:** thesis must take a position on the *extent* (unity and authority can pull in opposite directions); sourcing needed on at least 2 docs; evidence from at least 3 docs (argument supported by at least 4); at least one specific beyond-docs fact.

## 1. Sourcing opportunities per document

- **Doc 1 (Washington, First Inaugural, Apr 30, 1789):** Washington speaks as the new, self-consciously above-party president addressing Congress and the nation. His purpose is to bless the new government and invite loyalty to it. Because he is *performing* unity rather than reporting it, a student can read his hopes ("no party animosities") as aspirational — and then measure Federalist practice (Docs 6–7) against his own stated ideal. Strong for a thesis that the Federalists failed their own test.
- **Doc 2 (Virginia resolutions against assumption, Dec 1790):** A state legislature, led by opponents of Hamilton's program, protests to Congress. Purpose: block assumption and brand it unconstitutional and sectional. The audience (Congress) and the regional voice (agricultural Virginia fearing a "monied interest") let a student argue that Federalist finance was *received* as sectional favoritism — evidence against unity from the losers' own words.
- **Doc 3 (Jefferson's bank opinion to Washington, Feb 15, 1791):** A cabinet secretary writes *privately* to the president urging a veto and defending strict construction. Because it is an internal memo rather than a public speech, it shows opposition brewing inside Washington's own administration. A student can use that privacy to argue that Federalist "authority" was contested even at the top — this is insider dissent, not outsider complaint.
- **Doc 4 (Hamilton's bank opinion, Feb 23, 1791):** Written for Washington alone to win his approval and establish implied powers. The audience of one matters: loose construction was first an elite administrative argument, not a public persuasion. A student can source this to explain *how* federal authority advanced (centralized executive interpretation) and why it bred backlash (no democratic consent sought).
- **Doc 5 (Washington's Whiskey Rebellion proclamation, Aug 7, 1794):** A presidential proclamation to the whole nation, issued to justify calling out the militia. The proclamation form itself is an exercise of new federal authority — the commander-in-chief frames force as law enforcement, not war on citizens. A student can analyze that framing choice: it reveals the Federalist belief that federal authority had to be *demonstrated* to be real.
- **Doc 6 (Sedition Act, July 14, 1798):** An act of a Federalist Congress passed during the Quasi-War crisis, addressed to courts and prosecutors. Its timing is the sourcing angle: a partisan majority legislating at a moment of foreign fear. A student can argue the crisis explains the Act but also indicts it — unity by coercion — and note its purpose (silencing the Republican press) is stated nowhere in the text but visible in the historical situation.
- **Doc 7 (Kentucky Resolutions, Nov 16, 1798):** A state legislature answers the Alien and Sedition Acts — but the draft was secretly Jefferson's. A student can analyze the hidden authorship: the sitting Vice President had to write anonymously to undermine the President, which itself measures how far unity had collapsed. Excellent sourcing angle on purpose (rally Republican resistance) and situation (nullification doctrine born here).

## 2. Beyond-the-documents evidence

- **The Bank of the United States, chartered 1791** — Washington signed it after weighing Docs 3 and 4; the direct outcome of the cabinet debate, and the institutional embodiment of implied powers.
- **Jay's Treaty (1794, ratified 1795)** — Federalist foreign policy tilted toward Britain; Republicans denounced it as betrayal, and the fight over it deepened partisan division nationwide.
- **Enforcement of the Sedition Act: the jailing of Congressman Matthew Lyon (1798)** — a sitting Republican congressman fined and imprisoned for criticizing Adams; shows what "advancing authority" looked like in practice.
- **The election of 1800 ("Revolution of 1800")** — the peaceful transfer of power to Jefferson, the voters' verdict on Federalist rule; useful for arguing the unity project ultimately failed at the ballot box.

## 3. Complexity pathways

1. **The unity–authority tradeoff.** The same measures that advanced federal authority (assumption, the bank, crushing the Whiskey Rebellion) actively damaged national unity. A student can organize the whole essay around that tension — arguing the prompt's two halves pull in opposite directions — instead of treating unity and authority as two separate checklists.
2. **Washington vs. the Federalists.** Distinguish the president's nation-building intentions (Doc 1) from the partisan Federalist record (Docs 2, 6, 7). Doc 3 shows Jefferson *inside* Washington's cabinet, so the student can argue unity eroded under the party system Washington tried to rise above — the failure belongs to partisanship, not to Washington's design.
3. **Institutional success, political failure.** Note the irony that Hamilton's implied-powers doctrine outlived its creators: Jefferson himself later used loose construction to justify the Louisiana Purchase (1803). A student can argue Federalist authority-building succeeded permanently even as Federalist unity-building collapsed — a genuinely nuanced "extent" answer.
