# Rubric Map — DBQ: Immigration Debates, 1754–1800 vs. 1875–1925

**Source file:** `build/reclaim-merged/staged/barrons-2027/pt2-dbq.json` (id: `barrons-2027-pt2-dbq`)
**Test:** test-07
**Prompt:** "Americans debated and addressed immigration policy in the period 1754–1800 and again in the period 1875–1925. Evaluate the extent to which the ways they debated and addressed immigration changed between the two periods."

**Rubric reminders:** thesis must judge the *extent of change* between the two periods (change-and-continuity reasoning); sourcing on at least 2 docs; evidence from at least 3 docs (argument on at least 6) with docs from *both* periods; at least one specific beyond-docs fact (Alien and Sedition Acts, Chinese Exclusion, or the 1920s quota laws all qualify).

## 1. Sourcing opportunities per document

- **Doc 1 (Franklin, written 1751, publ. 1755):** An English Pennsylvanian warning colonial readers that German immigrants will "Germanize" Pennsylvania. The angle: nativism *before the nation* — cultural anxiety in an agrarian colony, framed around language, customs, and even "complexion." A student can use it to show the early debate was about colonial identity and assimilation, with no federal machinery in sight.
- **Doc 2 (Crevecoeur, 1782):** A French immigrant turned American farmer, writing to *European* readers to celebrate his adopted country as a "melting pot." The audience explains the salesmanship — the melting pot is partly marketing to attract Europeans — but the immigrant-turned-celebrant POV gives it weight as testimony. A student can pair it with Doc 5 as the optimistic pole of both periods' debates.
- **Doc 3 (Sedgwick, House debate, 1794):** A Federalist congressman, amid the wars of the French Revolution, urging the House to restrict naturalization. The historical situation is the angle: with European radicalism seeming contagious, the early debate turned on political loyalty and national security — whether newcomers from warring Europe could be trusted with republican citizenship. A legislative speech is policy argument in action.
- **Doc 4 (McDonnell, Labor Standard, 1878):** A labor journalist defending Chinese immigrants to working-class readers, redirecting anger from foreigners to the low-wage system. The class-based, pro-immigration voice *from inside labor* complicates any "workers vs. immigrants" story. His shaming move — descendants of earlier immigrants repeating Know-Nothing bigotry — uses historical memory as argument; excellent for a nuance paragraph.
- **Doc 5 (Keppler, Puck, 1880):** An Austrian-born immigrant cartoonist picturing America as Noah's ark taking aboard the world's refugees, for a mass magazine audience. The artist's own immigrant status makes the optimism testimony rather than mere art, and the ark-vs.-storm metaphor answers Old World turmoil. A student can use it as the later period's visual counterpart to Crevecoeur.
- **Doc 6 (Madison Grant, 1916):** A wealthy eugenicist writing with the authority of racial "science" for elites and policymakers, warning that "new" immigrants are biologically inferior to earlier "Nordic" stock. The authority claim is the sourcing point: pseudoscientific racism gave later nativism a harder, categorical edge than Franklin's cultural worry — and the policymaker audience shows it was written to shape law, which it did.
- **Doc 7 (Beard, "Columbia's Unwelcome Guests," 1885):** A popular-magazine cartoon fusing economic, criminal, and radical stereotypes — newcomers as rats, anarchists, socialists, Mafiosi pouring from the "sewers" of Italy, Russia, and Germany. Unlike elite debate, this aimed at mass feeling. A student can contrast its visceral register with Doc 1's pamphlet: later nativism was a mass-persuasion industry.

## 2. Beyond-the-documents evidence

- **The Alien and Sedition Acts (1798)** — the early period's debate producing federal restriction amid Quasi-War fears: longer residency for citizenship, presidential deportation power.
- **The Chinese Exclusion Act (1882)** — the first federal law barring an entire nationality; the later period's shift to categorical, race-based exclusion.
- **The Immigration Act of 1917** — literacy test plus the Asiatic Barred Zone; restriction tightening on the eve of the quota era.
- **The Emergency Quota Act (1921) and the National Origins Act (1924)** — the quota system capping the later period's restrictionist triumph and freezing America's ethnic composition by law.
- **The Gentlemen's Agreement (1907–1908)** — the U.S. and Japan quietly curbing Japanese labor immigration; diplomacy as restriction, showing the later period's global reach.

## 3. Complexity pathways

1. **Continuity within change.** Anti-immigrant sentiment persisted from Franklin to Grant, but its basis (cultural worry → wages plus racial "science"), its scale (pamphlets → a federal quota system), and its consequences transformed. A student can argue change outweighs continuity in consequence while conceding emotional continuity — the classic qualified thesis this prompt rewards.
2. **Temporary vs. structural restriction.** Early restrictions were political and temporary (the Alien Acts expired; the 1798 fever broke), while later ones were structural and durable (exclusion, then quotas lasting four decades). A student can analyze *why* industrial-era nativism produced lasting law: organized labor, racial ideology, and a federal state with new administrative capacity.
3. **Neither period was monolithic.** Docs 2 and 5 (immigrant celebrants) and Doc 4 (labor defending the Chinese) show the "debate" framing needs nuance in both eras. A student can also compare what was at stake: the early debate asked who could be a *citizen of a republic*; the later asked who could *enter an industrial labor market* — different questions producing different answers.
