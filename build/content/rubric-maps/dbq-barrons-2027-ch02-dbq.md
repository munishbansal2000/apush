# Rubric Map — DBQ: WWI and American Change, 1915–1935

**Source file:** `build/reclaim-merged/staged/barrons-2027/ch02-dbq.json` (id: `barrons-2027-ch02-dbq`)
**Test:** test-03
**Prompt:** "Evaluate the extent to which American participation in World War I fostered political, economic, and social change in the United States in the period from 1915 to 1935."

**Rubric reminders:** thesis must judge the *extent* of change across political, economic, and social dimensions; sourcing on at least 2 docs; evidence from at least 3 docs (argument on at least 4); at least one specific beyond-docs fact. Note: Docs 2 and 6 are descriptive entries (the book reprinted titles/cartoons without excerpts) — cite them as described, accurately.

## 1. Sourcing opportunities per document

- **Doc 1 (Espionage Act amendment, June 1917):** Congress acting at Wilson's request, weeks after war entry, writing for prosecutors and the public. Purpose: criminalize interference with the war effort. The sourcing angle is the statute's breadth — "disloyal, profane, scurrilous, or abusive language" about the government is written into law. A student can use that vagueness as evidence that wartime political change was coercive by design.
- **Doc 2 (Morris editorial, Kansas City Sun, July 14, 1917):** A Black editor's open letter, addressed to Black readers and — by its form — to the president himself. Purpose: turn Wilson's "make the world safe for democracy" back on the nation and demand democracy at home. The rhetorical judo is the angle: wartime ideals became a lever for civil-rights claims precisely because the president had to be answered in his own language.
- **Doc 3 (Ashland Daily Press, Apr 1918):** A local Wisconsin paper reporting a tarring-and-feathering matter-of-factly to local readers — reporting, not condemning. The casual tone *is* the evidence: vigilante enforcement of conformity treated as ordinary news. A student can contrast it with Doc 1 to argue wartime political change worked socially as well as legally — community violence alongside statute.
- **Doc 4 (War Industries Board, June 1918):** The federal agency coordinating production, announcing its direction of iron and steel to producers and the public. The bureaucratic voice ("be it resolved") is the angle: unprecedented federal management of private industry presented as routine administration. A student can use it as the cleanest evidence of the war's economic change — the state directing the economy.
- **Doc 5 (Wilson to the Senate, Sept 30, 1918):** The president personally lobbying senators for the suffrage amendment. A president appearing in person before the Senate was rare, and that rarity is the sourcing point — it shows how seriously the war had shifted suffrage politics. The "partnership of privilege and right" framing also lets a student note the advance was conditional: rights earned through wartime service.
- **Doc 6 (Ireland cartoon, Columbus Dispatch, Mar 1919):** A mainstream Ohio daily, months after the armistice, amid the Red Scare and nativist surge. Purpose: express the view that America could not "digest" undesirable newcomers. The mainstream venue matters — this was respectable opinion, not fringe — and the timing captures the postwar reversal: wartime unity curdling into suspicion of foreigners and radicals.
- **Doc 7 (Harding, May 14, 1920):** The Republican presidential candidate speaking to a business audience, promising "not nostrums, but normalcy." The alliterative litany is campaign rhetoric for war-weary voters and businessmen — and a student can source it as evidence that containing the war's changes was itself an *active political choice*: dismantling wartime controls was a decision, not an accident.

## 2. Beyond-the-documents evidence

- **Schenck v. United States (1919)** — the Supreme Court upheld wartime speech restrictions ("clear and present danger"), giving Doc 1's coercion the judiciary's stamp.
- **The Nineteenth Amendment (1920)** — the suffrage victory that Wilson's 1918 address (Doc 5) helped secure; the war's most durable democratic expansion.
- **The Great Migration** — Black Southerners moving north during the war years for industrial jobs and to escape Jim Crow; the demographic engine behind Doc 2's demands.
- **The Palmer Raids (1919–1920)** — federal roundups and deportations of suspected radicals; the postwar Red Scare in action, pairing with Doc 6.
- **The Committee on Public Information (Creel Committee)** — the government's wartime propaganda machine; shows federal reach into public opinion alongside Doc 1's legal coercion.

## 3. Complexity pathways

1. **Change vs. reversal.** The war created real change (Docs 1–5) *and* a backlash that undid much of it (Docs 6–7). A student can argue the most lasting legacy was the precedent of an activist federal state, not a permanent social transformation — an "extent" thesis that weighs the wartime evidence against the postwar evidence instead of just counting changes.
2. **Unevenness by group.** The war expanded some Americans' power (women via suffrage, federal managers via the WIB) while contracting others' (dissenters under the Espionage Act, immigrants and radicals in the Red Scare, Black Americans facing the Red Summer of 1919). A student can argue the "extent" of change depended on who you were — change and reaction as simultaneous, not sequential.
3. **Accelerator, not origin.** Progressive Era reform predated the war (suffrage, regulation, labor organization). A student can argue the war accelerated trends already underway rather than inventing them — and that the "reversal" of Docs 6–7 was partly a return to prewar baselines, which qualifies how much change the war itself caused.
