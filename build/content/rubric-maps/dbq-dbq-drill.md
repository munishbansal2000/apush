# Rubric Map — DBQ: Fears of Communism, 1940–1959

**Source file:** `build/reclaim-merged/staged/princeton-25e/dbq-drill.json` (id: `dbq-drill`)
**Test:** test-04
**Prompt:** "Evaluate the extent to which fears of communism affected American society from 1940 to 1959."

**Rubric reminders:** thesis must judge the *extent* of the effect on *society* (not just government); sourcing on at least 2 docs; evidence from at least 3 docs (argument on at least 6); at least one specific beyond-docs fact. Note: several docs are paraphrases — cite the described argument accurately, and treat Doc 5 (1919) as context for the prompt's period, not evidence within it.

## 1. Sourcing opportunities per document

- **Doc 1 (decoded KGB cable, Nov 1944):** A Soviet operative's operational report to Moscow — never meant for American eyes. That secrecy is the sourcing gold: with no propaganda motive toward Americans, the cable is candid evidence that Soviet espionage in the U.S. was real (the Rosenberg network). A student can use it to argue anti-communist fear had a factual kernel, which makes the later excesses a story of proportion, not pure invention.
- **Doc 2 (Kennedy speech to Congress, Jan 1949):** A congressman attacking the Truman administration over the communist victory in China, speaking to the House and the public. Purpose: demand a harder anti-communist line and wound a Democratic president. The partisan motive lets a student argue anti-communism functioned as a domestic political weapon — "losing China" as a club — and the speaker's identity (a future liberal icon) shows how broadly the weapon was wielded.
- **Doc 3 (Truman speech, 1951):** The sitting president warning that fear was destroying the freedoms it claimed to defend — while defending his own anti-communism. The dual role is the angle: the chief Cold Warrior himself testifying to the climate (Americans afraid to sign their names to Declaration of Independence quotes). A student can use it as establishment evidence of the scare's height — criticism from inside, not from the left.
- **Doc 4 (Civil Defense magazine ad, 1955):** A company selling metal ID tags for schoolchildren to wear during atomic-bomb drills, aimed at schools and parents. The profit motive is the sourcing point: fear had become a market. A student can argue the scare's societal reach from this — it was commercialized and embedded in children's daily lives, not confined to Washington hearings.
- **Doc 5 (Literary Digest cartoon, 1919):** Nativist press imagery — a sinister immigrant anarchist with knife and bomb menacing the Statue of Liberty. Purpose: stoke fear of immigrant radicals during the First Red Scare. Dated 1919, it sits outside the prompt's 1940–1959 window, so a student should use it only for the continuity argument: the foreigner-as-radical image persisted across four decades, and the Second Red Scare echoed the First's fusion of nativism and anti-radicalism.
- **Doc 6 (McCarthy, Wheeling, Feb 1950):** A senator addressing a Republican women's club, via the press, claiming a list of 205 communists in the State Department. Purpose: gain national attention and power. The setting and the number are the angle: accusation as performance, a figure offered without evidence — the doc is the scare's *method* in miniature, and a student can source it to show how recklessness, not proof, drove the panic.
- **Doc 7 (Murrow broadcast, 1954):** A respected broadcast journalist warning television viewers that McCarthy confused dissent with disloyalty and that accusation is not proof. Purpose: check McCarthy's power. The medium matters — television's new national reach turned against the demagogue — and the establishment source matters: pushback came from inside the media elite. A student can use it to argue the scare had limits and an ending.

## 2. Beyond-the-documents evidence

- **Executive Order 9835 (1947)** — Truman's Federal Employee Loyalty Program: loyalty investigations of millions of federal workers; the scare institutionalized in the executive branch.
- **The HUAC Hollywood hearings (1947)** — the House Un-American Activities Committee's investigation of the film industry produced the Hollywood Ten and the blacklist; the scare's cultural beachhead.
- **The Alger Hiss case (1949–1950)** — Hiss convicted of perjury after Whittaker Chambers accused him of spying; the case that made "communists in government" credible to millions.
- **The McCarran Internal Security Act (1950)** — passed over Truman's veto; required communist organizations to register and authorized detention of suspected subversives in emergencies.
- **The Rosenberg executions (1953)** — Julius and Ethel Rosenberg executed for atomic espionage; the Doc 1 network's endpoint, and the moment the real-spy story fused with the panic in public memory.
- **The Army-McCarthy hearings (1954) and McCarthy's Senate censure (Dec 1954)** — the televised hearings broke him; the censure marked the scare's political end.

## 3. Complexity pathways

1. **Real kernel, wildfire excess.** Use Doc 1 (real espionage) against Docs 4 and 6 (commercialized fear, evidence-free accusation): argue the fear had a factual basis but metastasized far beyond it — and that the *societal* effect (blacklists, loyalty oaths, classroom drills) came from the excess, not the kernel. Extent judged by proportion.
2. **Top-down and bottom-up.** The scare worked through Washington (loyalty programs, HUAC, McCarran Act) *and* through culture and markets (Doc 4's ad, the Hollywood blacklist, textbook purges). A student can argue the societal effect was deepest precisely where government and culture reinforced each other — or distinguish genuine belief from political weaponization (Doc 2 vs. Doc 3).
3. **Limits and self-correction.** Doc 7 plus the 1954 censure let a student qualify the "extent": the society that panicked also pushed back — through journalism, the courts, and eventually the Senate itself. A strong thesis can argue the fear profoundly distorted American life for roughly a decade *and* that democratic institutions ultimately contained it.
