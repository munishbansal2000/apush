# Rubric Map — LEQ: Reform Movements, 1820–1860

**Source file:** `build/reclaim-merged/staged/fivesteps-2024/exam2-leq.json` (id: `5s24-exam2-leq-01`)
**Test:** test-01
**Prompt:** "Evaluate the extent to which reform movements played an important role in shaping American society from 1820 to 1860."
**Targeted reasoning:** Causation

**Rubric reminders:** 6 points — thesis (a defensible claim that takes a position on *extent*, more than a restatement); contextualization (broader context relevant to the prompt); evidence (at least 2 specific examples = 1 pt; used to support the argument = 1 pt); analysis & reasoning (causation structures the argument = 1 pt; complexity = 1 pt).

## 1. Thesis framing

**Strong example thesis:** "Between 1820 and 1860, reform movements profoundly reshaped American society — abolitionism forced slavery to the center of national politics, the women's rights movement launched an organized challenge to legal inequality, and common-school reform built public education — yet their victories were uneven: abolition freed no one before the Civil War, and temperance and utopian experiments largely collapsed."

**Why it earns the point:** It answers "to what extent" with a clear position (profound but uneven) and sets up a line of reasoning the essay must follow: three areas of real impact, then the limits. The body paragraphs can be checked against it.

**What a restatement looks like (earns nothing):** "Reform movements played an important role in shaping American society from 1820 to 1860." This repeats the prompt without making a claim the essay has to defend — no position on extent, no roadmap.

## 2. Contextualization scope

Relevant broader context sits just before and around the window: the Second Great Awakening (revivalism from the 1790s, peaking in the 1820s–40s) supplied the religious energy; the market revolution created a Northern middle class — especially women — with the literacy, leisure, and print networks to organize; Jacksonian-era optimism that society could be perfected. Stay inside 1820–1860, mostly in the North and Midwest where reform was centered. Do not drift into colonial revivals, the Progressive Era, or post–Civil War amendments — those belong after the window, not as context.

## 3. Evidence areas

### Evidence reform mattered
- **Abolitionism:** William Lloyd Garrison's *The Liberator* (1831) and the founding of the American Anti-Slavery Society (1833); Frederick Douglass's *Narrative* (1845); petition campaigns that provoked the congressional gag rule (1836–44) — proof reformers were rattling national politics.
- **Women's rights:** the Seneca Falls Convention (1848) and its Declaration of Sentiments, modeled on the Declaration of Independence; leaders Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott.
- **Public education:** Horace Mann and the Massachusetts common-school movement of the 1830s–40s; the spread of tax-supported schools and teacher-training "normal schools."
- **Temperance:** the American Temperance Society (1826); Maine's statewide prohibition law (1851), the first of its kind.

### Evidence of limits
- Abolition freed no enslaved people before the war; Southern proslavery ideology hardened instead (Calhoun's "positive good" defense of slavery, 1837).
- Most utopian communities collapsed quickly or stayed tiny (Brook Farm, 1841–47; the Oneida Community, 1848).
- Seneca Falls won moral attention but no suffrage and no major legal victories before 1860.

## 4. Complexity pathways

1. **Reforms feeding each other.** Show multiple, reinforcing causes: revivalism sparked benevolent societies, abolitionist organizing gave women public-speaking experience that became the women's rights movement. The student traces a chain, not a list.
2. **The same cause, opposite effects.** Abolitionism unified Northern reformers while deepening the sectional divide — reform simultaneously improved society and helped break the Union. Holding both outcomes at once is complexity.
3. **New tactics, old obstacles.** Reformers used radically new methods — mass voluntary associations, petition drives, print propaganda — even where results were limited. A student can argue the *how* changed more than the *what*, a change-and-continuity judgment inside a causation prompt.
