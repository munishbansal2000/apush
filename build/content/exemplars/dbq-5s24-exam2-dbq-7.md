## Essay

The Supreme Court mostly inhibited Progressive regulation of corporations before about 1905 — narrowing the Sherman Act into uselessness against the great trusts and shielding business behind "liberty of contract" — but after 1905–1911 it began to advance federal regulatory power, culminating in decisions that sustained the modern regulatory state. The extent, then, is genuinely mixed: the Court both blocked and built Progressive regulation, and it advanced reform only within limits it defined for itself. [Thesis +1: evaluates *extent* ("mostly inhibited... but... began to advance"), establishes a line of reasoning (periodization plus the Court setting its own limits), and previews the structure.]

The conflict grew from the Gilded Age. Between 1885 and 1920, industrialization concentrated unprecedented economic power in trusts and giant corporations — Standard Oil, the Sugar Trust, the railroads — while workers and farmers bore the costs. The Progressive movement arose to answer that concentration, demanding that government regulate business in the public interest. But the Court the Progressives faced had been raised on laissez-faire constitutionalism: the belief that the Constitution protected property and contract from government interference. Whether reform survived would be decided as much in courtrooms as in legislatures. [Contextualization +1: broader developments — industrial concentration, the Progressive movement, laissez-faire constitutionalism — explaining why the Court became the decisive battleground.]

In the 1890s the Court's answer was inhibition, and the pattern was strikingly one-sided. When the government tried to break up the Sugar Trust, the Court ruled that manufacturing was not commerce: the defendants' acts "related exclusively to the acquisition of the Philadelphia refineries" and "bore no direct relation to commerce between states," so the Sherman Act could not reach them (Doc. 2). The great combination of the age walked free on a definition. Worse, the Court turned the Sherman Act against reform's allies: in the Pullman Strike case it upheld jailing Eugene Debs, reading the Act broadly to crush a labor boycott (Doc. 1). The same statute, read narrowly for trusts and broadly for unions — the asymmetry tells the story of the decade. The Court then extended the shield: corporations were "persons" under the Fourteenth Amendment, so a state law reducing railroad rates was struck down as depriving railroads of "the compensation they were legally entitled to receive" (Doc. 3). An amendment written to protect freedpeople was now protecting railroad profits.

The signature of the era came in 1905. New York's law limiting bakers' working hours, the Court declared, was "an illegal interference with the rights of individuals, both employers and employees, to make contracts regarding labor upon such terms as they may think best" (Doc. 4). "Liberty of contract" became a constitutional barrier that Progressive labor laws would crash against for a generation.

[Sourcing +1 — Doc. 4: the Lochner majority is not merely deciding a case about bakers; its purpose is to constitutionalize an economic philosophy — laissez-faire — by reading "liberty of contract" into the Fourteenth Amendment's due process clause. The opinion's abstract language about the rights of "individuals, both employers and employees" masks a substantive choice: treating a bakery owner and a bakery worker as equals in bargaining power. Recognizing that the justices are *making* economic policy in the guise of interpreting law is essential to understanding why the decision blocked reform so broadly.]

Yet the wall began to crack. The first breach came in 1904, when the Court dissolved the Northern Securities railroad holding company — a sign that even this Court would break up a trust that offended it. [Evidence beyond documents +1 (first piece): Northern Securities v. U.S. (1904) — a specific outside decision showing the turn beginning.] Then the Court itself began rewriting its doctrines. In Muller v. Oregon (1908) it upheld an Oregon law limiting women's working hours — but only by insisting that "the two sexes differ in structure of body" and physical strength, a difference that "justifies a difference in legislation" (Doc. 5).

[Sourcing +1 — Doc. 5: the Muller opinion's purpose is to carve a narrow exception *without* disturbing Lochner — the Court pointedly decides "without questioning in any respect the decision in Lochner v. New York." Its paternalist reasoning about women's bodies is doing double work: it lets one protective law survive while reaffirming the doctrine that dooms the rest. The document's careful, limited logic is itself evidence of how entrenched Lochner-era thinking remained — the Court advanced regulation only on terms that preserved its own precedents.]

The decisive shift came in 1911. Ordering Standard Oil dissolved, the Court announced the "rule of reason": only "unreasonable" restraints of trade violated the Sherman Act (Doc. 6). The breakup was real — the era's greatest trust was dismantled — but the doctrine was double-edged, handing future judges a license to decide which restraints were "reasonable." By 1917 the transformation was unmistakable: the Court upheld the Adamson Act's eight-hour railroad day as "clearly within the power of Congress under the commerce clause" (Doc. 7) — the same commerce clause that in 1895 could not reach a sugar refinery. Doctrine had traveled from E. C. Knight to Wilson v. New in twenty-two years.

But the extent of the advance must not be overstated. [Complexity +1: the essay qualifies its own "inhibit-then-advance" story on two fronts — (1) doctrines cut both ways: the rule of reason *enabled* the Standard Oil breakup while *narrowing* the Sherman Act for the future, and Muller *saved* one law while *reaffirming* Lochner; (2) inhibition did not end in 1911: in 1918 the Court struck down the federal child-labor law in Hammer v. Dagenhart, proving the old instincts survived the new doctrines. The argument is thus periodized *and* internally qualified, not one-sided.] The Court advanced Progressive regulation only within boundaries it drew itself — and it kept redrawing them. Reformers learned that winning in Congress was not enough; they had to win at the Court too, on the Court's terms.

[Evidence 2/2: all 7 documents support the argument — Docs. 1–4 document the inhibition phase (asymmetric Sherman Act enforcement, corporate personhood, liberty of contract), Docs. 5–7 the qualified advance (narrow exception, rule of reason, commerce-clause affirmation).]
