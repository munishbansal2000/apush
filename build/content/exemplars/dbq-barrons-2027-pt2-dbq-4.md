## Essay

The debate over immigration changed a great deal between 1754–1800 and 1875–1925. In the early period, Americans mainly worried about whether immigrants would fit into American culture and whether they could be loyal to the new republic. In the later period, the debate was more about race and about whether immigrants would take jobs and lower wages. [Thesis +1: evaluates extent ("changed a great deal") with a line of reasoning — the *basis* of the debate shifted from culture and loyalty to race and economics.]

The later period was very different because of industrialization. Millions of immigrants came to work in factories and live in crowded cities, which created new economic pressures that did not exist in the early period, when America was mostly farms and small towns. This helps explain why the later debate focused so much on jobs and wages. [Contextualization +1: broader context — industrialization and urbanization — relevant to why the later debate took an economic form.]

In the early period, Benjamin Franklin worried that German immigrants would "swarm" into Pennsylvania and "Germanize" the colony instead of adopting English language and customs (Doc. 1). His concern was cultural — he feared the newcomers would not assimilate. By the later period, the nativist argument had become racial. Madison Grant claimed that the new immigrants from southern and eastern Europe were biologically inferior to the earlier "Nordic" immigrants, calling them "the weak, the broken, and the mentally crippled of all races" (Doc. 6). This was a much harsher argument than Franklin's, because it said whole groups of people could never become American no matter what they did.

The later nativism was also aimed at ordinary people through popular images. Frank Beard's 1885 cartoon "Columbia's Unwelcome Guests" showed immigrants as rats and criminals streaming from sewers labeled Italy, Russia, and Germany, with the new arrivals tagged as anarchists, socialists, and members of the Mafia (Doc. 7). This kind of harsh propaganda shows how intense anti-immigrant feeling had become by the later period.

The later period's fears produced much stronger laws. The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 was the first federal law to ban immigrants based on their nationality, which shows how far the country had moved toward restriction compared to the early period. [Evidence beyond documents +1: a specific outside fact — the 1882 act — connected to the argument about consequences.]

In conclusion, the immigration debate changed substantially: the early argument about culture and loyalty became a later argument about race and jobs, and the result was far more restrictive federal policy.
