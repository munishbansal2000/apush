# LEQ Exemplar — leq-ch3-main (test-10) — target score 6/6

**Prompt:** Assess how far the role of the federal government changed between 1830 and 1910.
**Period:** U4/U6 · **Themes:** PCE · **Reasoning:** Continuity & Change
**Score earned: 6/6** — Thesis 1 · Contextualization 1 · Evidence 2 · Analysis & Reasoning 2

---

## Essay

In 1830, the dominant American belief about the federal government was that it should do as little as possible. Andrew Jackson had just vetoed federal funding for the Maysville Road on the grounds that internal improvements were a state matter, and he would soon veto the recharter of the Bank of the United States and face down South Carolina's nullification of federal tariffs — every episode reinforcing the Jacksonian creed of limited federal power, low spending, and deference to the states. The federal government of 1830 delivered the mail, collected customs duties, managed foreign affairs, and not much else. By 1910, that government regulated railroad rates, inspected meat, conserved forests, and policed corporate trusts. The role of the federal government changed enormously between 1830 and 1910 — from a deliberately limited government to an active manager of the economy and the West — but the change was uneven: federal power grew dramatically in economic life while retreating on civil rights, and laissez-faire ideology constrained it throughout. [Thesis +1: defensible claim answering "how far," with a line of reasoning — transformation in economic governance, retreat in civil rights, ideological limits.] [Contextualization +1: establishes the 1830 baseline in concrete terms (Maysville veto, Bank veto, nullification crisis, what the government actually did) so the change has a starting point to be measured from.]

The Civil War was the great hinge. To fund the war and bind the West to the Union, the Republican Congress passed the Homestead Act, the Morrill Land-Grant Act, and the Pacific Railway Act — all in 1862 — giving away federal land to settlers, colleges, and railroads on a scale unimaginable in 1830. The government printed paper money ("greenbacks") under the Legal Tender Act, created a national banking system, and levied the first federal income tax. Reconstruction then stretched federal power into civil rights: the Thirteenth, Fourteenth, and Fifteenth Amendments wrote federal authority over slavery and citizenship into the Constitution, and the Freedmen's Bureau delivered aid and schooling directly to formerly enslaved people. The war had proven that the federal government *could* reshape the economy and society — and after 1865, Americans never fully returned to the Jacksonian assumption that it *shouldn't*. [Evidence +1 (identification): Homestead/Morrill/Pacific Railway Acts (1862), greenbacks, the national banking system, the Reconstruction amendments and Freedmen's Bureau — 2+ specific examples.] [Evidence +1 (support): each example is tied to the thesis — the 1862 laws show the *scale* of new federal activism, the amendments show the *new kind* of power (over civil rights), and the closing sentence connects them to the argument about a changed baseline.] [Analysis & Reasoning +1 (continuity-and-change frame): the paragraph explicitly measures 1860s activism against the 1830 baseline — "on a scale unimaginable in 1830" — organizing the evidence as change over time.]

After the war, the pattern continued in economic life: the Interstate Commerce Act of 1887 created the first federal regulatory commission to police railroad rates, and the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890 declared corporate monopolies illegal. Under Theodore Roosevelt, the Progressive-era government went further — the Hepburn Act of 1906 strengthened railroad regulation, the Pure Food and Drug Act and Meat Inspection Act of 1906 put federal inspectors into factories, and the new Forest Service (1905) began managing public lands for conservation. But the limits were just as real. The Supreme Court's *Lochner*-era decisions struck down labor regulations in the name of "freedom of contract," most famously in *Lochner v. New York* (1905). And on civil rights the federal government moved *backward*: after Reconstruction ended in 1877, Washington abandoned Black southerners to Jim Crow, a retreat the Court ratified in *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896). The government that regulated meatpacking would not protect voting rights. [Evidence +1 (identification): Interstate Commerce Act, Sherman Antitrust Act, Hepburn Act, Pure Food and Drug/Meat Inspection Acts, Forest Service, *Lochner*, *Plessy* — 2+ specific examples.] [Evidence +1 (support): the examples are split deliberately — regulation cases support the "change" half of the thesis, *Lochner* and *Plessy* support the "limits and retreat" half.] [Complexity +1: the essay's core judgment *is* complexity — expansion and contraction happening simultaneously, in different spheres, driven by different forces. The closing contrast ("regulated meatpacking but not voting rights") refuses a single-direction story, and the paragraph shows continuity (laissez-faire ideology, states'-rights resistance) operating inside the change.]

## Complexity, demystified

For change-over-time prompts, the easiest complexity move is **the uneven timeline**: change was real, but it did not move at the same speed — or in the same direction — everywhere. This essay runs that move as its whole thesis: economic regulation expanded while civil-rights protection contracted, and laissez-faire ideology persisted through both. Students often treat complexity as a paragraph to add at the end; here it is the *argument itself*. Any essay whose thesis already contains a "but" or a "while" is halfway to the point before the first body paragraph begins.
