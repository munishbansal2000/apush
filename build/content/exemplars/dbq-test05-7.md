# DBQ Exemplar — test-05 (princeton-25e/dbq-test3): Slavery and National Unity, 1844–1861

**Prompt:** Evaluate the extent to which slavery challenged American national unity in the period from 1844 to 1861.

**Target score: 7/7.** (Bracketed notes are teacher margin annotations, not student text.)

The Missouri Compromise of 1820 had drawn a line at 36°30′ to contain slavery's expansion, and for two decades the arrangement mostly held: the slavery question was managed, not solved. But the annexation of Texas in 1845 and the Mexican War reopened the question the Compromise had buried — would slavery spread into the vast new territories? From that moment, every territorial acquisition became a sectional battleground. [Contextualization +1: situates the prompt in the Missouri Compromise and the territorial consequences of expansion, establishing the baseline against which the 1844–61 challenge is measured.] Slavery challenged American national unity to a very great extent between 1844 and 1861: it turned territorial expansion into a sectional war over slavery's future, split even the Democratic Party along sectional lines, provoked a Supreme Court overreach in Dred Scott that inflamed rather than settled the issue, and finally shattered the national party system in the election of 1860. Economic divergence and expansion amplified the damage, but slavery was the central force — and the Union held formally until the 1860 election made the challenge terminal. [Thesis +1: a historically defensible claim establishing a line of reasoning about extent — very great, with slavery ranked above the amplifying factors and the endpoint qualified.]

Expansion and slavery were entangled from the start. John L. O'Sullivan's 1845 call for Texas annexation under the banner of "manifest destiny" — America's providential right to "overspread the continent" (Doc 1) — was, in substance, a demand to bring a vast slaveholding republic into the Union. [Sourcing +1 — Doc 1: O'Sullivan writes as a Democratic editor and expansionist promoting annexation to magazine readers; his purpose is to sell expansion, not to analyze slavery. The writer uses the author's position to show that the slavery question entered national politics inside the language of manifest destiny — expansionism carried the slavery fight with it from the beginning.] President Polk's 1846 war message, insisting that "war exists... by the act of Mexico herself" and calling on Congress to "vindicate... the honor, rights and dignity of this country" (Doc 2), produced the Mexican Cession — the very lands over which the slavery-extension fight would split the nation. Congressman David Wilmot then drew the sectional line openly: the issue was "not whether slavery shall exist unmolested where it now is, but whether it shall be carried to new and distant regions, now free, where the footprint of a slave cannot be found" (Doc 3). [Sourcing +1 — Doc 3: Wilmot speaks as a Northern Democrat addressing the House; his purpose is to bar slavery from the Mexican War territories. The writer uses the speaker's party identity to make the key point — when even the Democratic Party divides sectionally over slavery extension, national unity is genuinely cracking.]

The attempt to settle the question by letting territories decide only nationalized it. The Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854 repealed the Missouri Compromise line and opened Kansas and Nebraska to slavery under popular sovereignty — the map of its results shows a nation geographically sorting itself by the slavery question (Doc 4). The result was not a settlement but "Bleeding Kansas," three years of guerrilla violence between pro- and anti-slavery settlers. [Evidence beyond the documents +1: Bleeding Kansas is a specific fact outside the documents, used to support the argument — a known student bleed point, handled with a concrete fact.] The Compromise of 1850 had already shown the pattern: its Fugitive Slave Act, requiring northerners to aid in capturing runaways, embittered the North and proved that concessions to slavery poisoned unity rather than preserving it. Then the Supreme Court tried to end the debate by fiat. Chief Justice Taney's 1857 opinion in Dred Scott declared that "the right of property in a slave is distinctly and expressly affirmed in the Constitution" and that Congress had no greater power over slave property than over any other (Doc 5). [Sourcing +1 — Doc 5: Taney writes as a pro-southern Chief Justice aiming to settle the slavery question judicially by nationalizing slave property rights. The writer uses the author's position to explain the backlash: because the decision read as a sectional ruling, it outraged the North and deepened disunity instead of settling anything. A third sourced document is good teaching even though only two are required.] Rather than removing slavery from politics, the Court had nationalized it.

The sectional divergence was material as well as political. Paired railroad maps of 1850 and 1860 show a dense rail network knitting together the Northeast and Old Northwest — linking the Atlantic to Chicago and the upper Mississippi — while the South built far fewer and shorter lines (Doc 6). Economic development itself followed sectional lines, giving the two regions less and less in common. And the political system finally broke under the strain: an 1860 cartoon shows a locomotive labeled "Equal Rights" carrying Lincoln bearing down on the "Democratic Platform" wagon, pulled in opposite directions by the rival Democratic nominations of Douglas and Breckinridge (Doc 7). [Sourcing +1 — Doc 7: a Republican-leaning press cartoon satirizing the Democrats' sectional split for voters; its partisan purpose means it exaggerates for effect, but the writer correctly treats it as visual proof of the underlying fact — slavery had shattered the national party system on the eve of secession.] Lincoln's election that November, without carrying a single southern state, triggered South Carolina's secession in December 1860. [Evidence beyond the documents — second instance; only one is needed for the point, but the secession anchor strengthens the argument.]

Yet the extent claim needs its qualification. [Complexity +1: the writer qualifies the thesis rather than arguing one side — slavery was central but expansion and economic divergence were amplifiers, not mere background; and the challenge escalated over time rather than striking all at once. This is the complexity bleed point handled correctly.] The 1840s fights were political — the Wilmot Proviso never even passed the Senate — while the 1850s brought violence in Kansas, a judicial crisis in Dred Scott, and finally the collapse of the party system. And the Union held formally until the very end of the period: the challenge was severe and growing, but secession came only after the election of 1860 made the sectional verdict unmistakable. Slavery did not destroy national unity in 1844; it corroded, over seventeen years, every institution — parties, Congress, the Court, the map itself — that unity rested on.
