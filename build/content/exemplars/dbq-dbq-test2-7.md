## Essay

The American state can largely be described as imperialist from 1890 to 1945: it seized overseas colonies, ruled millions of colonial subjects, and justified the whole enterprise as uplift. But the label needs qualification. Anti-imperialism remained a live American tradition throughout the period, much of American power in the hemisphere worked through informal economic control rather than formal colonies, and even empire's defenders described it as temporary tutelage rather than permanent possession. The United States was an imperial power that refused to call itself one — and that denial shaped how the empire worked. [Thesis +1: evaluates *extent* ("largely... but needs qualification"), establishes a line of reasoning (formal colonies + informal control + the denial itself), and previews the counter-evidence the essay will address.]

The turn toward empire grew from the anxieties of the 1890s. The census of 1890 declared the frontier closed, just as industrial overproduction left American factories hungry for new markets; historian Frederick Jackson Turner warned in 1893 that the end of free land might close off the dynamism that had defined the nation. Around the world, European powers were carving Africa and Asia into colonies, and many Americans feared the United States would be left behind. The Spanish-American War of 1898 — fought, Americans told themselves, to free Cuba — handed Washington an empire almost by accident: the Philippines, Puerto Rico, and Guam. [Contextualization +1: broader developments — frontier's closing, industrial markets, the European scramble — explaining *why* imperialism became plausible in the 1890s. Evidence beyond documents +1 (first piece): the Spanish-American War of 1898 as the event that delivered the overseas empire.]

The imperial record is straightforward. By 1940 the United States ruled some 16.4 million people in the Philippines and another 2.5 million across Puerto Rico, Hawaii, Alaska, the Canal Zone, Guam, the Virgin Islands, and Samoa — over 18 million colonial subjects in all, a figure that had grown substantially since 1920 (Doc. 6). That is what an empire looks like when you count it. Popular culture celebrated the project: a 1914 Chicago Tribune cartoon showed the Philippines, Hawaii, Puerto Rico, Cuba, and Panama lifted from oppression into suits, holding papers labeled "Filipino Assembly," "Education," and "Self Govt." (Doc. 4), while an 1899 magazine cartoon had Uncle Sam urging President McKinley to "finish the canal... and make our national expansion complete" as American flags flew over the new possessions (Doc. 7). Colonial administrators supplied the paternalist theory: Filipinos, wrote one American official, were "intelligent and polite" and "capable of being won over," needing only "proper care and direction" to become "a strength to our nation" (Doc. 5) — the language of a guardian, not a conqueror.

[Sourcing +1 — Doc. 4: McCutcheon draws for a mass-circulation American newspaper; his purpose is celebration, not analysis, and his audience is readers who want to feel good about the flag. The cartoon's cheerful before-and-after panels are propaganda for benevolent empire — which is precisely why the document matters: it shows how imperialism was *sold* to the American public as uplift. A hostile witness would never draw this cartoon; a friendly one draws nothing else.]

Even critics outside the American tradition reached the imperialist verdict. Vladimir Lenin, writing in 1917, cited the American anti-imperialists' own charge — that Washington had promised the Filipino leader Aguinaldo independence, "then they landed troops and annexed it" — as proof that the United States practiced capitalist imperialism like any European power (Doc. 2). The Philippine-American War of 1899–1902, in which the United States fought Filipino independence forces for three years to keep the archipelago it claimed to have liberated, gives Lenin's charge its factual backbone. [Evidence beyond documents +1 (second piece): the Philippine-American War — the specific fact that turns annexation from a paper claim into a conquered reality.]

[Sourcing +1 — Doc. 2: Lenin writes as a Marxist revolutionary; his purpose is theoretical — to prove that imperialism is the inevitable product of capitalism — and his audience is fellow socialists. That purpose shapes the document: he *needs* America to be imperialist for the theory to hold, so he selects the most damning facts (the Aguinaldo betrayal) and discards the rest. He is a hostile witness, useful for confirming the empire's existence but unreliable on its causes — which the essay must keep in mind when borrowing his evidence.]

Yet the imperialist description, while largely accurate, needs its qualifications — and the documents supply them. Charles Francis Adams Jr., writing to a fellow anti-imperialist in 1898, appealed to American precedent: in Mexico and Venezuela, he argued, the United States had insisted that nations "stand on its own legs" rather than be kept "everlastingly in 'leading strings,'" and abandoning that tradition for the Philippines would betray what was "strictly American" (Doc. 1). Adams lost the political fight, but his letter proves anti-imperialism was not a foreign critique — it was an American tradition with deep roots. The 1848 Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo complicates the timeline further: the United States had been expanding by conquest for half a century before 1890, paying Mexico $15 million for California and the Southwest after defeating it in war (Doc. 3) — which suggests the 1890s were less a break than a new phase of an old habit. And even at empire's height, its defenders described it as tutelage: Filipinos would get "Self Govt." and "Education" (Doc. 4), protectorates were temporary, and the Philippines did move toward independence. The empire was real, but it was an empire embarrassed by its own name.

The extent, then, is substantial but bounded. [Complexity +1: the essay qualifies its verdict on multiple fronts — (1) a genuine anti-imperialist tradition persisted *inside* the United States (Doc. 1), so "imperialist" was always contested, not consensual; (2) much American power worked informally (economic leverage, protectorates) rather than through formal colonies, so counting only colonies understates — and overstates — different things; (3) the empire was justified as temporary tutelage, which shaped its actual administration and its eventual partial dismantling. The essay thus argues *for* the imperialist label while explaining exactly where the label strains.] From 1890 to 1945 the United States ruled an overseas empire, celebrated it in its newspapers, counted its subjects in its census — and argued with itself the entire time about whether it was doing any such thing.
