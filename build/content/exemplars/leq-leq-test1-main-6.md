**Prompt:** Assess how far social movements reshaped American society between 1945 and 1980.

## The essay

The movements of 1945–1980 did not erupt from nowhere. They unfolded inside postwar affluence and Cold War liberalism: America claimed to lead the "free world" against communism while denying basic rights to Black citizens at home, and the dislocations of World War II — millions of Black veterans, women drawn into wartime work — had already cracked the old order. [Contextualization +1: postwar affluence, the Cold War, and WWII's dislocations are broader developments beyond the movements themselves that explain the conditions they arose from.]

Social movements fundamentally reshaped postwar American society: the civil rights movement dismantled legal segregation, the women's movement expanded economic and political equality, and the counterculture challenged traditional norms — though each faced backlash that limited change, and the reshaping was profound but incomplete. [Thesis +1: a historically defensible claim about extent ("fundamentally reshaped… profound but incomplete") that establishes a line of reasoning across three movements, with the qualification that becomes complexity, rather than restating the prompt.]

The civil rights movement was the era's engine. The Montgomery bus boycott of 1955–56, sparked by Rosa Parks's arrest, showed that disciplined mass protest could defeat segregation in a major Southern city — and it made Martin Luther King Jr. a national figure. [Evidence +1 (identified): two specifics — the Montgomery boycott (1955–56) and Parks.] A decade later the movement converted protest into law: the Civil Rights Act of 1964 banned segregation in public accommodations and employment discrimination, and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 enfranchised Black Southerners, ending the Jim Crow system as a legal regime. [Evidence identified: the CRA (1964) and VRA (1965).] This matters because the movement did not merely change attitudes; it rewrote the relationship between citizens and the federal government, establishing Washington as the enforcer of equality — a precedent every later movement would invoke. [Evidence +1 (support): the examples are tied directly to the thesis claim about reshaping society — protest converted into federal law, creating a template.]

The women's movement applied that template to gender. The National Organization for Women, founded in 1966, demanded legal and economic equality; Title IX of 1972 barred sex discrimination in federally funded education, transforming schools and college athletics; and *Roe v. Wade* (1973) constitutionalized reproductive rights. [Evidence identified: NOW (1966), Title IX (1972), *Roe* (1973).] These were structural changes, not symbolic ones: Title IX measurably expanded women's access to higher education and professional careers within a decade. [Evidence tied to the argument: the effects are specified, not just named.] Other movements widened the era's challenge: the Stonewall uprising of 1969 launched the gay rights movement, and Cesar Chavez's United Farm Workers — through the Delano grape strike beginning in 1965 and the national boycott — forced the country to see Mexican-American labor. [Evidence identified and tied: Stonewall (1969), Delano (1965).]

But the honest measure of "how far" must include the backlash and the incompleteness. Richard Nixon's 1969 appeal to the "silent majority" and Ronald Reagan's election in 1980 built winning coalitions partly out of white resistance to the movements' gains — busing, affirmative action, and the Equal Rights Amendment all stalled or reversed. [Evidence identified: the "silent majority" (1969), Reagan (1980).] The Supreme Court's *Bakke* decision of 1978 limited affirmative action even as it preserved it, and the ERA — passed by Congress in 1972 — died unratified in 1982. [Evidence identified: *Bakke* (1978), the ERA's failure.] The movements changed what Americans expected government to do, but they did not change who held power as thoroughly as their rhetoric promised: wage gaps persisted, and de facto segregation outlived de jure segregation. [Analysis & Reasoning +1: continuity and change structure the argument throughout — legal transformation measured against persistent continuities — and causation links protest to legislation to backlash.]

The reshaping was real — no one in 1980 lived under the legal order of 1945 — but it was contested at every step, and the contest itself became part of the legacy: modern American politics is still organized around the coalitions these movements and their opponents built. [Complexity +1: the essay argues with its own counterevidence — backlash, *Bakke*, the ERA's defeat — and qualifies the thesis (profound but incomplete; de facto segregation persisted), going well beyond a one-sided argument through contradiction and qualification.]
