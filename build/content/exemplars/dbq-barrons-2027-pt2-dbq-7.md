## Essay

In both 1754–1800 and 1875–1925, Americans fiercely contested who could become American — nativists warning of cultural dilution against defenders of an expansive, welcoming nation — but the debate changed fundamentally between the two periods. The early argument turned on cultural assimilation and political loyalty to a fragile republic; the later argument absorbed industrial-era fears about wages and jobs plus a new racial "science" of permanent inferiority. The result was a transformation in scale: from pamphlets and naturalization debates to a federal system of categorical exclusion. Change outweighs continuity in basis, scale, and consequence — though the nativist impulse itself never disappeared. [Thesis +1: evaluates *extent* directly ("change outweighs continuity"), establishes a line of reasoning (basis → scale → consequence), and previews the qualification that keeps it from being one-sided.]

Each debate grew from the anxieties of its moment. In the 1790s the United States was a new republic watching the French Revolution's wars tear Europe apart; the Quasi-War with France made foreign radicalism feel like a domestic threat, and Congress responded with the Alien and Sedition Acts of 1798, lengthening the wait for citizenship and criminalizing criticism of the government. A century later the setting was unrecognizable: industrialization had drawn millions of immigrants into crowded cities and factory labor markets, where organized workers feared that newcomers would undercut wages — and the First World War and the Red Scare then fused nativism with fears of radicalism all over again. [Contextualization +1: broader context for *both* periods — wartime insecurity early, industrialization later — explaining why each debate took the shape it did. Evidence beyond documents +1 (first piece): the Alien and Sedition Acts of 1798 — a specific fact outside the documents showing the early debate producing federal restriction.]

The early debate was framed around whether newcomers could be absorbed into the nation. Benjamin Franklin, writing as an English Pennsylvanian watching German immigrants "swarm" into his colony, demanded to know why Pennsylvania should "become a Colony of Aliens, who will shortly be so numerous as to Germanize us instead of our Anglifying them" (Doc. 1). His worry was cultural: language, manners, customs. Against him stood the celebrants of openness. Hector St. John de Crèvecoeur, a French immigrant turned American farmer, asked "What then is the American, this new man?" and answered that in America "individuals of all nations are melted into a new race of men" (Doc. 2). And when Congress debated naturalization in 1794, Federalist Theodore Sedgwick warned against cheapening citizenship for "the discontented, the ambitious, and the avaricious of every country" while Europe's cruel war raged — framing immigration as a question of political loyalty and national security (Doc. 3).

[Sourcing +1 — Doc. 1: Franklin writes as a colonial Englishman alarmed at Germans "herding together" in *his* Pennsylvania; his purpose is to warn fellow colonists, not to describe Germans fairly. His point of view — a member of the colony's English establishment watching his cultural dominance slip — shapes the document's anxious framing, and even his closing phrase about "Complexion" reveals that racial thinking colored the early debate too. The document is evidence of nativist *anxiety*, not of what German immigrants were actually like.]

The later debate kept the nativist-versus-welcomer structure but changed its content. The economic dimension was new: a labor journalist, Joseph McDonnell, had to argue against the "Chinese must go" cry by insisting the real enemy was "low wages" and "cheap labor," not foreigners — shaming the descendants of earlier immigrants for repeating Know-Nothing bigotry against the Chinese (Doc. 4). That the pro-immigration voice now came from *inside* the labor movement shows how thoroughly the later debate had been economized. Meanwhile the cultural welcome persisted in popular imagery: Joseph Keppler's 1880 cartoon pictured America as Noah's ark, carrying immigrant families of many nations to safety while storm clouds gathered over Europe (Doc. 5) — the visual heir of Crèvecoeur's melting pot.

But the later period also produced an exclusionism harder and more categorical than anything in the early republic. Madison Grant, writing with the authority of early-twentieth-century racial "science," warned that the "new" immigrants from southern and eastern Europe were "the weak, the broken, and the mentally crippled of all races," the "sweepings of their jails and asylums" unloaded on "careless, wealthy, and hospitable America" (Doc. 6). Frank Beard's 1885 cartoon "Columbia's Unwelcome Guests" made the same case visually, drawing newcomers as vermin streaming from sewers marked Italy, Russia, and Germany, tagged as anarchists, socialists, and Mafiosi (Doc. 7). Where Franklin had worried that Germans would not adopt English customs — something assimilation could fix — Grant declared whole peoples biologically unfit, something no assimilation could cure.

[Sourcing +1 — Doc. 6: Grant writes as a wealthy eugenicist addressing educated elites and policymakers; his purpose is to give restriction the authority of science. His audience shapes the document's pseudoscientific vocabulary — "Nordic race," "lowest stratum" — which is designed to sound like expertise rather than prejudice. Reading it requires asking *who* benefits from calling nativism science: the restrictionists who needed respectable cover for the quota laws.]

The consequences confirm the transformation. The early period's fears produced the short-lived Alien and Sedition Acts; the later period's produced the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 — the first federal law barring an entire nationality — and then the Emergency Quota Act of 1921 and the National Origins Act of 1924, which capped immigration by national origin for decades. [Evidence beyond documents +1 (further pieces): Chinese Exclusion and the 1920s quota acts — specific outside facts showing the later debate's consequences dwarfing the early period's. This is the point students lose most often; the fix is one or two concrete facts placed where they advance the argument.]

The change was therefore substantial but not total. [Complexity +1: the essay qualifies its extent judgment — nativist anxiety and pro-immigration idealism both *persisted* across the century (continuity), while the *basis* of exclusion shifted from culture and loyalty to race and economics, the *scale* shifted from pamphlets to a federal quota bureaucracy, and the *consequences* hardened from temporary restrictions to decades of categorical exclusion (change). It also notes the internal tension that neither era's debate fully resolved — McDonnell and Keppler show the welcomers never disappeared either.] Americans never stopped asking who gets to be American; they just started answering with quotas instead of pamphlets.
