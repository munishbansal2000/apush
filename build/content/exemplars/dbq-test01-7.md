# DBQ Exemplar — test-01 (5s24-ch03-dbq): Federalist Administrations, 1789–1801

**Prompt:** To what extent did the Federalist administrations of George Washington and John Adams promote national unity and advance the authority of the federal government in the period 1789–1801?

**Target score: 7/7.** (Bracketed notes are teacher margin annotations, not student text.)

The Constitution of 1787 was written because the Articles of Confederation had left the national government too weak to tax, to pay its war debts, or to put down uprisings like Shays' Rebellion in Massachusetts. When Washington took the oath of office in 1789, no one knew whether the new federal government would survive, let alone command real authority. [Contextualization +1: establishes the broader setting — the failure of the Articles and the uncertainty of the new republic — that gives the Federalists' actions their significance.] The Federalist administrations advanced the authority of the federal government to a great extent — through Hamilton's financial program, the doctrine of implied powers, and the first use of national military force against domestic resistance — but they promoted national unity only weakly, and at times actively damaged it, because those same centralizing policies provoked sectional resentment, partisan warfare, and the states'-rights doctrines of the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions. [Thesis +1: a historically defensible claim establishing a line of reasoning — it answers "to what extent" by distinguishing authority (great extent) from unity (weak or negative), organizing the essay's two tracks.]

Hamilton deliberately built federal power through finance. Defending the Bank in 1791, he argued that the Constitution's powers "ought to be construed liberally in advancement of the public good" (Doc 4) — the classic statement of implied powers, a doctrine designed to let the national government do whatever its ends required. [Sourcing +1 — Doc 4: Hamilton is writing to Washington to win approval for the Bank; his purpose is advocacy, not neutral constitutional interpretation. Recognizing the document as a deliberate strategy to expand national power is exactly what lets the writer use it for the "authority" side of the argument.] That even Jefferson, writing privately to Washington to urge a veto, warned that taking "a single step beyond the boundaries" of delegated powers opened a "boundless field of power" (Doc 3) shows contemporaries understood the stakes: the administration was choosing an expansive reading of federal authority, and its own cabinet was divided over it. Beyond the documents, Hamilton's 1790 Report on Public Credit — with its assumption of state debts, the very policy Virginia protested — created a funded national debt that tied creditors' fortunes to the federal government. [Evidence beyond the documents +1: a specific policy (assumption via the Report on Public Credit) not found in the documents, used to support the argument. This is a known student bleed point — the point requires a concrete fact outside the documents, not a vague gesture.]

Washington matched financial power with coercive power. When western Pennsylvanians resisted the whiskey excise, his 1794 proclamation called forth the militia to "suppress the combinations" and "cause the laws to be duly executed" (Doc 5) — the first use of national military force to enforce a federal tax. [Sourcing +1 — Doc 5: this is a presidential proclamation addressed to the nation; its purpose is to justify federal force, and its formal legal language is itself a performance of the authority it claims. The writer shows how the document's situation shapes its meaning.] The gap between this action and Washington's 1789 inaugural promise — that "no local prejudices or attachments, no separate views or party animosities" would misdirect the government (Doc 1) — measures how far practice fell from the Federalists' own stated ideal of unity. [Sourcing +1 — Doc 1: Washington speaks as the above-party first president to Congress and the nation; his purpose is to legitimize the new government and bind the states to it. The lofty unity rhetoric is a political strategy, which is why the writer can use it to show the distance between Federalist promise and Federalist practice. A third sourced document is good teaching even though only two are required.]

But the drive for authority damaged the unity it claimed to serve. The Sedition Act of 1798 made it a crime, punishable by up to two years in prison, to publish "false, scandalous, and malicious writing" against the government (Doc 6) — a Federalist Congress criminalizing criticism of itself. The specific context, beyond the documents, is the undeclared naval Quasi-War with France in 1798, which gave the Adams administration the emergency it used to justify the Act. Virginia had already warned that Hamilton's program meant "the prostration of agriculture at the feet of commerce" and threatened "American liberty" (Doc 2), a sectional protest from an agricultural state against policies it saw as serving northern commerce; and Kentucky answered the Sedition Act by declaring the states "not united on the principle of unlimited submission" and the Act "altogether void" (Doc 7) — the birth of the nullification doctrine that would haunt the republic for decades.

Yet the story is not simply that Federalists built power while destroying unity — that would be too neat. Their centralizing policies provoked a durable states'-rights tradition: Kentucky's claim that states could judge federal overreach armed future opponents of national authority, which means the Federalists advanced federal power and simultaneously generated the doctrine that would challenge it. [Complexity +1: the writer qualifies the argument instead of arguing one side throughout — the same policies that built authority produced the resistance that limited it. This is the other great student bleed point: complexity requires engaging a contradiction or qualifying the claim, not just listing more evidence.] Nor did unity fail only because of Federalist overreach. Washington's own Farewell Address had warned in 1796 against the "baneful effects of the spirit of party," yet partisan division hardened anyway — between Federalists and Republicans, North and South — suggesting the young republic's unity was fragile for reasons beyond any one administration's choices. The Federalists succeeded where survival mattered most: they left behind a government that could tax, borrow, and enforce its laws. But the unity they achieved was the unity of obedience, and it expired with Adams's defeat in the election of 1800.
