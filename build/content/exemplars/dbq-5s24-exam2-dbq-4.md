## Essay

The Supreme Court inhibited Progressive regulation of corporations more than it advanced it during the period 1885–1920. Again and again, the Court struck down or narrowed the laws that reformers passed to control big business, protecting corporations instead of the public. [Thesis +1: evaluates extent ("inhibited more than it advanced") with a line of reasoning — the Court repeatedly struck down or narrowed reform laws.]

By the late 1800s, huge trusts and corporations dominated the American economy — the Sugar Trust, Standard Oil, the railroads — and ordinary workers and consumers had little power against them. The Progressive movement demanded that government step in to regulate these businesses, but every reform had to survive the Supreme Court, which during this era was deeply sympathetic to property rights and hostile to government interference in business. [Contextualization +1: broader background — concentrated corporate power, the Progressive reform impulse, and a property-friendly Court — explaining why the Court mattered.]

The Court's inhibition began with the Sherman Antitrust Act itself. When the government tried to break up the Sugar Trust, the Court ruled that the trust's actions "related exclusively to the acquisition of the Philadelphia refineries" and "bore no direct relation to commerce between states," so the Sherman Act did not apply (Doc. 2). By defining manufacturing as something separate from interstate commerce, the Court gutted the main federal weapon against trusts just as the trust problem was at its worst.

The Court then gave corporations constitutional armor. In Smyth v. Ames, it declared that "corporations are persons" under the Fourteenth Amendment, and on that basis struck down a Nebraska law reducing railroad rates, ruling that the rate limits would deprive the railroads of "the compensation they were legally entitled to receive" (Doc. 3). An amendment passed to protect freed slaves was now being used to protect railroad profits from state regulation.

The most famous barrier was "liberty of contract." In Lochner v. New York (1905), the Court struck down a state law limiting bakers' working hours, calling it "an illegal interference with the rights of individuals, both employers and employees, to make contracts regarding labor upon such terms as they may think best" (Doc. 4). This doctrine blocked Progressive labor laws for a generation, since almost any workplace regulation could be described as interfering with the freedom of contract.

The pattern continued past the period's supposed turning points. In 1918 the Court struck down the Keating-Owen Child Labor Act in Hammer v. Dagenhart, ruling that Congress could not use the commerce power to ban goods made with child labor — further proof that the Court kept limiting reform even late in the period. [Evidence beyond documents +1: Hammer v. Dagenhart (1918) — a specific outside decision reinforcing the inhibition argument.]

In conclusion, the Supreme Court was more of an obstacle than an ally to Progressive regulation: it narrowed the Sherman Act, constitutionalized corporate rights, and invented doctrines like liberty of contract that reformers could not get past.
