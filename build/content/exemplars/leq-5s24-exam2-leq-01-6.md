# LEQ Exemplar — Target Score: 6/6

**Test:** test-01 · **LEQ id:** 5s24-exam2-leq-01
**Prompt:** Evaluate the extent to which reform movements played an important role in shaping American society from 1820 to 1860.

## The essay

The market revolution of the early nineteenth century — canals, railroads, and factories — made many Americans richer and more mobile, but it also unsettled older patterns of work, family, and community. [Contextualization +1: the essay opens with the market revolution and transportation changes, a broader development beyond the reform topic itself that explains the conditions reform arose from.]

Out of that unsettled world came the reform movements of 1820–1860. They played an important role in shaping American society, building lasting institutions like public schools and a new moral vocabulary around slavery and women's rights — though their reach was bounded: reform was mostly a Northern phenomenon, its victories were partial, and its greatest triumph, abolition, came only through civil war rather than persuasion. [Thesis +1: a historically defensible claim ("important role" with defined limits) that establishes a line of reasoning — institutions built, moral vocabulary created, limits acknowledged — rather than restating the prompt.]

The most consequential reform was abolitionism. William Lloyd Garrison's *Liberator*, founded in 1831, demanded immediate emancipation and turned slavery from a distant Southern institution into a national moral crisis. [Evidence +1 (identified): a specific publication with a specific year.] Frederick Douglass's *Narrative* (1845) and the Underground Railroad gave the movement Black leadership and concrete action, turning abstract principle into escapes that forced Northerners to confront the Fugitive Slave Act. [Evidence: two more specifics identified — the *Narrative* (1845) and the Underground Railroad.] This matters because abolitionism did not merely protest slavery; it created the moral framework — that slavery was a sin, not just an economic system — that the Republican Party and eventually the Union war effort would inherit. [Evidence +1 (support): the examples are tied directly to the thesis claim that reform built a lasting moral vocabulary, not just named.]

Women's rights and temperance show the same pattern of real but limited change. The Seneca Falls Convention of 1848 produced the Declaration of Sentiments, modeled on the Declaration of Independence, claiming equality for women — yet no state granted women's suffrage before the Civil War. [Evidence identified and tied to the argument about limits.] Temperance, organized through the American Temperance Society (1826) and culminating in Maine's 1851 prohibition law, reduced alcohol consumption and gave women a public moral role — yet drinking persisted, and the gains were mostly regional. [Evidence identified and tied to the argument.] Together these movements show reform's signature method: translating evangelical moral urgency into organized, public action, even where the victories fell short of the rhetoric. [Supports the argument rather than listing.]

Reform also built institutions that outlasted the era. Horace Mann's common-school movement in Massachusetts made tax-supported public education a Northern norm, on the theory that a republic needed literate, virtuous citizens. Utopian communities like Brook Farm (1841) and the Oneida Community (1848) tested radical alternatives to market society — and mostly failed, which is itself evidence of reform's limits: Americans wanted moral improvement without abandoning the market economy that was enriching them. [Analysis & Reasoning +1: causation structures the whole essay — the market revolution's disruptions caused reform's rise; reform's moral framework and institutions caused later political and social outcomes.]

In the end, reform movements genuinely reshaped antebellum America — they put abolition on the national agenda, invented modern pressure-group politics, and built schools that still stand. But the honest accounting must include the other side: reformers never persuaded the South, their own movements split over tactics, and the "important role" they played was largely as a rehearsal — the moral and organizational groundwork for changes that only war and Reconstruction would complete. [Complexity +1: the essay qualifies its own claim — reform's reach bounded geographically (Northern), its signature victory achieved by war rather than persuasion, and its internal divisions acknowledged — going beyond a one-sided argument through qualification and contradiction.]

**Teaching note on complexity:** This is the point students miss most. Complexity does not require a separate "however" paragraph — here it is woven into the thesis ("though their reach was bounded") and the conclusion, where the essay admits evidence that cuts against its own claim (reform failed to persuade the South; its triumph required war). Teach students to argue *with* the counterevidence, not around it.
