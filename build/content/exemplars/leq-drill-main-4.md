# LEQ Exemplar — leq-drill-main (test-08) — target score 4/6

**Prompt:** Assess how far the abolitionist movement fostered change in America between 1830 and 1860.
**Period:** U4 · **Themes:** SOC, PCE · **Reasoning:** Causation
**Score earned: 4/6** — Thesis 1 · Contextualization 1 · Evidence 1 · Analysis & Reasoning 1

---

## Essay

Before the 1830s, opposition to slavery in America was mostly gradual. Some northern states had passed gradual emancipation laws, and the American Colonization Society wanted to send freed enslaved people to Africa. The Second Great Awakening encouraged Christians to fight sin in society, which led some reformers to take up the cause of abolition. The abolitionist movement of the 1830s was different because it demanded immediate emancipation. Between 1830 and 1860, the abolitionist movement fostered significant change in America by spreading antislavery ideas and pushing slavery into national politics, although it did not end slavery before the Civil War. [Thesis +1: defensible claim answering "how far," with a line of reasoning.] [Contextualization +1: contrasts immediatism with earlier gradualism and colonization, and connects the movement to the Second Great Awakening — the broader setting that explains why abolitionism emerged when it did.]

One example is William Lloyd Garrison, who started *The Liberator* in 1831 and called for immediate emancipation. Another example is the American Anti-Slavery Society, which organized petition campaigns, leading to the gag rule of 1836. Frederick Douglass wrote his *Narrative* in 1845 about his life as an enslaved person. The Underground Railroad helped enslaved people escape to the North. Politically, abolitionism led to the Liberty Party, the Free Soil Party, and the Republican Party. These developments caused slavery to become a major national issue and increased sectional tensions between the North and the South. [Evidence +1 (identification): Garrison/*Liberator*, the AASS and gag rule, Douglass's *Narrative*, the Underground Railroad, three antislavery parties — well over 2 specific examples.] [Evidence +0 (support): the paragraph is a catalog, not an argument. No example gets a sentence explaining *how* it fostered change — the gag rule is never connected to the claim about slavery corrupting politics, Douglass's *Narrative* is never connected to the claim about spreading ideas, and the parties are listed without showing how abolitionist energy moved into them.] [Analysis & Reasoning +1 (causation frame): present — "led to," "caused slavery to become a major national issue." The essay thinks in causes and effects throughout.]

In conclusion, the abolitionist movement fostered a great deal of change between 1830 and 1860 by spreading antislavery sentiment and reshaping politics, even though slavery itself continued until the Civil War. [Complexity 0: the conclusion restates the thesis. The essay never asks the uncomfortable questions its own material raises — why did a movement that changed politics fail to change most white minds? Why did attacking slavery make the South defend it harder? One such question, answered in two sentences, would earn the point.]

---

## Why this is a 4, not a 6

This student knows the content cold — the fact inventory here is nearly complete. Both lost points are about *using* knowledge rather than having it. The support point wants one "so what" sentence per example; complexity wants the student to notice that their own evidence contains a tension (moral radicalism vs. limited audience, attacking slavery vs. hardening it). Strong students at this level don't need more facts — they need the pause after each paragraph: "what does this prove, and what doesn't it prove?"
