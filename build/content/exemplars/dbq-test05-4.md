# DBQ Exemplar — test-05 (princeton-25e/dbq-test3): Slavery and National Unity, 1844–1861

**Prompt:** Evaluate the extent to which slavery challenged American national unity in the period from 1844 to 1861.

**Target score: 4/7.** (Bracketed notes are teacher margin annotations, not student text.)

The Missouri Compromise of 1820 had kept the slavery question contained for two decades by drawing a line at 36°30′: slavery could expand south of it, but not north. The annexation of Texas in 1845 and the Mexican War broke that containment, forcing the nation to decide whether slavery would spread into the vast new western territories. [Contextualization +1: situates the prompt in the Missouri Compromise and the territorial consequences of expansion — genuine broader context.] Slavery challenged American national unity to a great extent between 1844 and 1861: it divided Congress and the political parties along sectional lines, provoked a Supreme Court decision that inflamed the North, and finally broke the national party system in the election of 1860. [Thesis +1: a defensible claim about extent with a line of reasoning (Congress, the Court, the party system).]

The fight began over the territories. Congressman David Wilmot insisted the issue was "not whether slavery shall exist unmolested where it now is, but whether it shall be carried to new and distant regions, now free, where the footprint of a slave cannot be found" (Doc 3) — a Northern Democrat demanding that the lands won from Mexico remain free. The Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854 then repealed the Missouri Compromise line entirely, opening Kansas and Nebraska to slavery under popular sovereignty; the map of its results shows the nation geographically divided by the slavery question (Doc 4). Instead of settling anything, the Act nationalized the conflict. [Evidence +1 (first point): the content of three documents — Docs 3, 4, and 5 below — is accurately described and used.]

The Supreme Court's attempt to end the debate made things worse. In Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857), Chief Justice Taney declared that "the right of property in a slave is distinctly and expressly affirmed in the Constitution" and that Congress had no special power over slave property (Doc 5). Rather than settling the issue, the decision outraged the North, which read it as the Court imposing slavery on the whole nation. The political system then collapsed under the strain: the Democratic Party split into northern and southern wings that nominated rival candidates in the 1860 election, and within months of Lincoln's victory, South Carolina seceded. The violence had already begun years earlier in Kansas, where pro- and anti-slavery settlers fought a guerrilla war — "Bleeding Kansas" — over whether the territory would enter the Union slave or free. [Evidence beyond the documents +1: Bleeding Kansas is a specific fact outside the documents, used to support the argument.]

In the end, slavery challenged every institution that held the Union together — Congress, the courts, and the parties — and by 1861 the challenge had become secession and civil war.
