# LEQ Exemplar — 5s24-exam1-leq-02 (test-09) — target score 6/6

**Prompt:** Evaluate the extent to which differing economies shaped differing social structures in the English colonies in North America.
**Period:** U2 · **Themes:** WXT, SOC · **Reasoning:** Comparison
**Score earned: 6/6** — Thesis 1 · Contextualization 1 · Evidence 2 · Analysis & Reasoning 2

## Essay

England did not plant one colony in North America — it planted several, for profit, and the pursuit of profit ran into very different land. Joint-stock companies and royal proprietors founded colonies to enrich the mother country under mercantilist ideas, but the Chesapeake's tobacco soil, New England's thin rocky farms, and the Middle colonies' fertile wheat lands made entirely different economies possible. Those economies, in turn, built different societies. Differing economies decisively shaped differing social structures: the plantation South developed a rigid hierarchy topped by a planter elite and grounded in enslaved labor, New England developed a town-centered society of independent farming families with a narrower gap between rich and poor, and the Middle colonies developed the most diverse and commercially fluid society of the three — though all three shared English legal norms and patriarchal households that the economy never erased. [Thesis +1: defensible claim answering "to what extent," with a line of reasoning — three distinct social orders traced to economic bases, plus a stated limit.] [Contextualization +1: situates the colonies in the broader context — English mercantilist motives, joint-stock colonization, and the geographic differences that made different staples viable.]

The contrast is sharpest between South and North. In the Chesapeake, tobacco demanded large tracts of land and a large bound workforce. Early planters relied on indentured servants, but after Bacon's Rebellion in 1676 showed how dangerous a class of landless freedmen could be, planters shifted decisively toward enslaved African labor, which could be held permanently and whose children inherited bondage. The result was a society with a small planter elite dominating politics — Virginia's House of Burgesses, founded in 1619, became their instrument — a thin middle of small farmers, and a large enslaved population at the bottom; in South Carolina's rice Lowcountry, enslaved Africans had outnumbered whites since 1708, reaching about sixty percent of the population by 1715. New England, by contrast, could grow no staple for export: its economy rested on mixed family farming, fishing, timber, and trade. Land was distributed in towns, families worked their own farms, and town meetings gave ordinary male church members a direct political voice unknown in the South. There were merchants and ministers at the top, but no planter aristocracy — and far fewer enslaved people, since the economy had little use for gang labor. [Evidence +1 (identification): tobacco/rice plantation economy, the post-1676 shift to slavery, the House of Burgesses, New England town meetings and mixed farming — 2+ specific examples.] [Evidence +1 (support): each example is tied to the comparison the thesis promises — the labor shift *explains* the southern hierarchy, town meetings *show* the flatter New England order. The economies are not just described; they are shown producing the social structures.] [Analysis & Reasoning +1 (comparison frame): the paragraph is built as a direct comparison — "in the Chesapeake... by contrast... New England" — with similarities and differences organized around the economic cause.]

The Middle colonies show the pattern a third way. Pennsylvania, New York, and New Jersey combined fertile farmland — wheat and grain for export — with religious tolerance that drew Germans, Scots-Irish, Dutch, and others. Philadelphia grew into a major port and commercial center, supporting a society of merchants, artisans, and independent farmers more ethnically and religiously mixed than either New England or the South. Labor was mixed too: free workers, indentured servants, and enslaved people worked side by side, because no single staple dictated a single labor system. Yet the limits of the economic argument matter: slavery existed in *every* colonial region, including New York City; women everywhere lived under English coverture law regardless of the local economy; and even in New England, merchants grown rich on Atlantic trade formed their own elite, so the "egalitarian" North had hierarchy too. Economies shaped colonial societies powerfully, but within a shared English framework — and with internal variations — that economics alone cannot explain. [Evidence +1 (identification): Middle-colony wheat economy, Philadelphia commerce, ethnic/religious diversity, mixed labor systems — 2+ specific examples.] [Evidence +1 (support): the examples support the thesis's third case and its stated limit — diversity is traced to the mixed economy, and the counter-examples (slavery in the North, New England merchant elites) support the qualification.] [Complexity +1: the essay qualifies its own argument twice — shared English institutions persisted across all regions, and each region contained internal exceptions to its type. The comparison is not flattened into three caricatures; difference and similarity are weighed together.]

## Complexity, demystified

Comparison prompts tempt students into a list: "the South was like this, the North was like that." This essay earns complexity by doing what comparison is actually for — finding the *limits* of the pattern. Two moves: (1) **the shared framework** — naming what did *not* differ (English law, patriarchy, slavery's presence everywhere) keeps the differences honest; (2) **variation within the type** — the richest New Englanders were merchants, not farmers, which complicates the "egalitarian North" story. Complexity in a comparison essay is almost always some version of "the categories are real, but they leak."
