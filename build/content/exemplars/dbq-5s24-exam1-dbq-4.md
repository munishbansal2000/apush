## Essay

American foreign policy in Latin America between 1899 and 1917 had major effects on the region. It increased American power and control over Latin American countries through military intervention and economic influence, turning independent republics into places where the United States could step in whenever it wanted. [Thesis +1: a historically defensible claim — U.S. policy expanded American control — with a line of reasoning (military intervention + economic influence as the mechanisms).]

After the Spanish-American War in 1898, the United States became a world power with new interests in the Caribbean and Latin America. American businesses wanted to invest in the region, and the American government wanted to keep European powers from gaining influence there. This background helps explain why the United States began intervening so often in Latin American affairs during this period. [Contextualization +1: broader background — the 1898 war, business investment, keeping Europe out — relevant to the prompt.]

One major effect was that Cuba lost much of its real independence. The Platt Amendment, which Cuba had to accept before American troops would leave in 1903, gave the United States the right to intervene in Cuba for "the preservation of Cuban independence" and for the protection of "life, property, and individual liberty" (Doc. 1). This meant that Cuba was only independent as long as the United States approved of its government — American intervention was always a possibility.

President Roosevelt provided the justification for this kind of intervention. In his 1904 message to Congress, he argued that "chronic wrongdoing" in the Western Hemisphere might require "intervention by some civilized nation," and that the Monroe Doctrine could lead the United States "to exercise an international police power" (Doc. 3). This Roosevelt Corollary announced to the world that the United States claimed the right to act as the policeman of Latin America, which encouraged further interventions.

President Taft extended American control through economic means rather than military force. He described his policy of dollar diplomacy as "substituting dollars for bullets" in order to increase American trade (Doc. 5). Under this policy, American banks made loans to Latin American governments and took control of their customs houses to make sure the loans were repaid, which gave the United States economic leverage over those countries.

The Panama Canal shows how committed the United States was to controlling the region. The canal, which opened to traffic in 1914, was built in a zone the United States controlled in Panama, and it greatly increased American naval and commercial power in Latin America. [Evidence beyond documents +1: the 1914 canal opening — a specific fact outside the documents that supports the argument about effects. Note this is the point students most often leave on the table: one concrete outside fact, connected to the argument, is all it takes.]

In conclusion, American foreign policy from 1899 to 1917 made the United States the dominant power in Latin America. Through the Platt Amendment, the Roosevelt Corollary, and dollar diplomacy, Washington gained the ability to intervene militarily and economically whenever it chose. [Complexity 0: the essay argues a single side — American control grew — from start to finish. It never qualifies the claim (were there limits? did anyone resist?), never addresses a contradiction (for example, that Wilson criticized these very policies), and never weighs one effect against another. A qualifying sentence or two — acknowledging limits or competing effects — is the usual route to this point.]

[Sourcing 0: the essay accurately describes what Docs. 1, 3, and 5 say, but it never steps back to explain how any author's point of view, purpose, audience, or historical situation shapes the document's content — for instance, that Roosevelt was *justifying* a policy to Congress, or that Taft was *defending* his own record. Describing content earns evidence credit; explaining *why the document says it that way* earns sourcing credit. Two such explanations are needed, and this essay has zero.]

[Evidence, second point 0: only three documents are used. The rubric's second evidence point requires supporting the argument with at least six documents — a common, fixable miss: the student knew more documents but stopped at three.]
