**Prompt:** From the French and Indian War through the Civil War, Britain's North American colonies and then the United States fought a series of military conflicts. Explain how participation in these conflicts influenced American national identity in the period from 1754 to 1877.

## The essay

For most of the eighteenth century, Britain and France fought for control of North America, and that imperial rivalry is the essential backdrop: the French and Indian War was the climax of their struggle, and Britain's enormous war debt afterward is what drove Parliament to tax and regulate the colonies in the first place. [Contextualization +1: the Anglo-French imperial struggle and British war debt are broader developments beyond the conflicts themselves that explain the conditions for identity change.]

Military conflicts from 1754 to 1877 repeatedly remade American national identity, but unevenly: the French and Indian War and the Revolution turned colonists from Britons into Americans — a change a substantial Loyalist minority never accepted — while the Civil War completed the transformation by binding the nation to emancipation and turning "the United States" from a plural compact of states into a singular nation. [Thesis +1: a historically defensible claim that establishes a line of reasoning — identity forged in stages, contested at each stage — rather than restating the prompt.]

The French and Indian War gave colonists shared military experience fighting alongside British regulars, but its aftermath did more for identity than the fighting. The Proclamation of 1763 and the Stamp Act of 1765 told colonists who had just bled for the empire that Parliament could restrict their movement and tax them without their consent. [Evidence +1 (identified): two specifics — the Proclamation of 1763 and the Stamp Act of 1765.] The insult was the point: colonists had imagined themselves as Britons entitled to British liberties, and postwar policy taught them they were subjects of a different, lesser kind. That lesson is what made independence thinkable. [Evidence +1 (support): both examples are tied directly to the argument — postwar policy, not just the war, is what severed the British identity.]

The Revolution then had to invent the "American" to replace the "Briton." The Declaration of Independence of 1776 addressed "one people" dissolving their political bands, giving the new identity a founding text. [Evidence identified: the Declaration, 1776.] But the change was contested, not universal: a substantial minority of colonists remained Loyalists, and the Revolution's own violence against them — tarring, exile, confiscation — shows that American identity was imposed on some as well as chosen by others. [Evidence tied to the argument: Loyalist persistence is used to qualify the claim, not just named.] The War of 1812 then cemented the identity the Revolution had invented. Francis Scott Key's "Star-Spangled Banner" (1814) gave the nation an anthem born in battle, the Federalist Party collapsed after the Hartford Convention, and the "Era of Good Feelings" proclaimed a nationalism that, however exaggerated, showed Americans now imagined one country rather than thirteen jealous states. [Evidence identified and tied to the nationalism claim.]

The Mexican-American War (1846–1848) and the Civil War show that conflict could divide identity as well as forge it. Victory over Mexico reopened the question the Revolution had dodged — whether the nation was a white republic or a multiracial democracy — because the Wilmot Proviso of 1846 tried to bar slavery from the conquered territory and split the country along sectional lines. [Evidence identified and tied: the Proviso shows identity contested.] The Civil War settled the question by force. The Emancipation Proclamation (1863) and Lincoln's Gettysburg Address, with its promise of a "new birth of freedom," recast the war — and the nation — as a test of whether the Declaration's promise of equality could be made real. [Evidence identified: 1863, Emancipation and Gettysburg.] After 1865 Americans increasingly said "the United States *is*" rather than "the United States *are*" — a grammatical shift that captures the political one, from a compact of states to a single nation. [Analysis & Reasoning +1: causation and change-over-time structure the argument — each conflict is shown causing a specific transformation in identity, and the essay tracks change across the full period.]

The full arc, then, is not a simple story of wars uniting Americans. Conflicts gave Americans shared sacrifices to remember and enemies to define themselves against, but they also exposed who was excluded from the identity being forged — Loyalists in 1776, Mexicans and enslaved people in 1848, and Black Southerners again when Reconstruction's promises collapsed by 1877. [Complexity +1: the essay argues with its own counterevidence — wars divided as well as united — and qualifies the thesis (identity forged but contested, promises made and broken), going well beyond a one-sided argument through contradiction and qualification.]
