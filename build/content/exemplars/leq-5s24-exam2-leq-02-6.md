# LEQ Exemplar — 5s24-exam2-leq-02 (test-06) — target score 6/6

**Prompt:** Evaluate the extent to which differing ideas about the nature of government shaped the emergence of political parties in the 1780s and 1790s.
**Period:** U3 · **Themes:** PCE, NAT · **Reasoning:** Causation
**Score earned: 6/6** — Thesis 1 · Contextualization 1 · Evidence 2 · Analysis & Reasoning 2

---

## Essay

In the years after the Revolution, Americans had won independence but had not settled what their government should actually be. The Articles of Confederation had failed to hold the states together, and even after the Constitution was ratified in 1788, the bruising ratification fight showed that Americans disagreed deeply about how much power a national government should hold. Out of that disagreement, the first political parties emerged in the 1790s. Differing ideas about the nature of government were the main force that created the Federalist and Democratic-Republican parties: Alexander Hamilton's vision of an energetic national government that would actively build the economy clashed with Thomas Jefferson's vision of a limited federal government serving an agrarian republic. Foreign crises and personal rivalries sped the split up, but it was ideas about government that gave the parties their shape and their staying power. [Thesis +1: a historically defensible claim that answers "to what extent" and sets up a line of reasoning — ideas as the primary cause, with foreign crises and rivalries as accelerators, not rivals to the argument.] [Contextualization +1: situates the prompt in the broader context of post-Revolution uncertainty, the failed Articles, and the ratification debate over federal power.]

The clearest place the two visions collided was Hamilton's financial program. As Treasury secretary, Hamilton proposed that the federal government assume the states' war debts, create a national bank, and use tariffs to encourage manufacturing, as laid out in his 1791 Report on Manufactures. Hamilton read the Constitution's "necessary and proper" clause broadly: if building the economy served the nation, the government had the power to act. Jefferson and James Madison answered with strict construction — the federal government could do only what the Constitution specifically listed. When Congress chartered the Bank of the United States in 1791, Madison argued in Congress that a bank was nowhere authorized, while Hamilton's defense persuaded Washington to sign. The Bank fight showed that the parties were not just about personalities. Federalists organized around the belief that national power should be used energetically; Democratic-Republicans organized around the belief that it should be restrained, and each side built newspapers, political clubs, and congressional voting blocs to defend its reading of the Constitution. [Evidence +1 (identification): names 2+ specific examples — assumption/Bank debate, strict vs. loose construction, the Report on Manufactures.] [Evidence +1 (support): each example is tied back to the claim — the debate is framed as a clash of ideas about government power producing party organization, not just a policy disagreement.] [Analysis & Reasoning +1 (causation frame): the paragraph traces how constitutional ideas caused parties to organize — "showed that... Federalists organized around the belief," building clubs and voting blocs.]

Foreign policy tested the two visions and hardened them into parties. When Britain and revolutionary France went to war in 1793, Federalists favored Britain, America's main trading partner, and negotiated the Jay Treaty of 1794, while Jeffersonians sympathized with France as a fellow republic and denounced the treaty as a betrayal. The divide was about the nature of government too: Federalists trusted a strong executive to steer foreign affairs, while Jeffersonians feared Federalist diplomacy was dragging the republic toward monarchy. That fear peaked with the Alien and Sedition Acts of 1798, through which the Federalist Congress tried to silence Republican newspaper editors. Jefferson and Madison answered with the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions, arguing that states could judge federal overreach — a theory of limited government pushed to its logical extreme. Ideas about government had now produced not just disagreement but a constitutional crisis, and the election of 1800, in which Jefferson's Republicans took power peacefully from the Federalists, proved the parties were permanent institutions, not temporary factions. [Evidence +1 (identification): Jay Treaty split, Alien and Sedition Acts, Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions — 2+ specific examples.] [Evidence +1 (support): each is tied to the argument — foreign-policy differences are explicitly linked to competing ideas about executive power and government's proper role, and the paragraph closes by showing the ideas produced lasting party institutions.] [Analysis & Reasoning +1 (causation): "tested the two visions and hardened them into parties" — causation structures the paragraph.]

None of this means ideas acted alone, and the parties themselves were not perfectly consistent. The personal hatred between Hamilton and Jefferson, and the shock of the French Revolution, gave the split its bitterness and its timing — without them, the parties might have formed more slowly. More importantly, each side bent its own principles: the Federalists who preached energetic republican government used the Sedition Act to jail editors, contradicting their claim to defend liberty, while Jeffersonians who preached strict construction invented the states'-rights nullification theory — itself a bold new claim about government power — to fight back. The parties were born from ideas about government, but they were also shaped by events and by the compromises both sides made with their own ideals. [Complexity +1: qualifies the argument (ideas primary, but events set the timing), identifies contradiction (each side bent its principles), and weighs multiple causes in an integrated way — not a tacked-on "on the other hand" paragraph.]

## Complexity, demystified

The complexity point is not a bonus paragraph — it is a way of thinking that runs through the essay. This essay earns it three ways at once: (1) **qualification** — "ideas were the main force, but events set the timing"; (2) **contradiction** — each party violated its own stated principles; (3) **multiple causation** — ideas, personalities, and foreign shocks all weighed, and the essay says which mattered most. Any *one* of these, developed in a sentence or two, can earn the point. What never earns it: restating the thesis in different words, or listing a second cause without saying how the causes relate.
