## Essay

Between 1899 and 1917 the United States repeatedly intervened in Latin America, and the effects were both consistent and contradictory. Washington secured the Panama Canal, protected American investments, and claimed the right to police the hemisphere — yet each success bred resentment and resistance that limited American control. Three presidents with three different slogans — Roosevelt's big stick, Taft's dollar diplomacy, Wilson's moral diplomacy — all ended up using force or the threat of force south of the border. The effect of American policy was therefore to make the United States the dominant power in the region while simultaneously making Latin Americans more determined to resist that dominance. [Thesis +1: historically defensible claim establishing a line of reasoning — it answers "what were the effects" with a two-part argument (dominance achieved, dominance resisted) and previews the three-administration structure the essay will follow.]

This pattern grew out of the Spanish-American War of 1898, which left the United States with new island possessions and a new sense of itself as a world power. An industrializing America hungered for markets, raw materials, and outlets for investment, and the Caribbean and Central America — close, politically unstable, and indebted to European creditors — looked like both an opportunity and a vulnerability. The Monroe Doctrine of 1823 had long warned Europe out of the hemisphere; after 1898 Washington began deciding what that warning permitted the United States itself to do. [Contextualization +1: situates the prompt in broader developments — the 1898 war, industrial expansion, and the Monroe Doctrine — explaining *why* the U.S. turned toward intervention in this period.]

Roosevelt gave the new activism its doctrine. In 1904 he told Congress that "chronic wrongdoing" in the hemisphere might require "intervention by some civilized nation," and that adherence to the Monroe Doctrine could lead the United States "to exercise an international police power" (Doc. 3). The Platt Amendment made the doctrine concrete in Cuba: imposed as the price of American withdrawal in 1903, it granted Washington the right to intervene "for the preservation of Cuban independence" and the protection of "life, property, and individual liberty" (Doc. 1). The effect was to convert formal independence into a protectorate — Cuba was free except when the United States decided it was not.

[Sourcing +1 — Doc. 3: Roosevelt is announcing policy to Congress as a presidential message, so his purpose is to legitimize intervention *in advance* and win political buy-in. His language of "civilized nation" versus "chronic wrongdoing" is not neutral description; it frames Latin American instability as a moral failing that requires American correction. Because the document is persuasion aimed at lawmakers and the public, its claims about American motives must be read as justification, not confession — which is exactly why it matters: it shows how intervention was *sold*.]

The canal project showed how far Washington would go to manufacture the conditions it wanted. Days after a U.S.-backed revolt detached Panama from Colombia, the United States signed the Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty, taking "in perpetuity, the use, occupation and control" of the canal zone — negotiated with a French agent representing Panama before Panama possessed a functioning government of its own (Doc. 2). The canal opened to traffic in August 1914, cutting the voyage between the Atlantic and Pacific by thousands of miles and anchoring American naval and commercial power in the region: a genuine engineering triumph that was also a monument to intervention. [Evidence beyond documents +1: the canal's 1914 opening is a specific fact outside the documents, directly supporting the argument about the *effects* of U.S. policy.] A contemporary cartoon captured how the policy looked to skeptics at home: Uncle Sam feasting at a banquet table heaped with dishes labeled "Cuba," "Puerto Rico," "Panama," and "Nicaragua," while a figure labeled "U.S. investor" carves the roast and waiters labeled "U.S. Marines" stand ready (Doc. 4). The image argues that prosperity at home was being fed by dominance abroad.

Taft made the economic logic explicit. His "dollar diplomacy," he told Congress, "sought to respond to modern ideas of commercial intercourse" by "substituting dollars for bullets" to increase American trade (Doc. 5). American banks took over customs collection and floated loans across the Caribbean, tying governments to Wall Street. The Anti-Imperialist League saw exactly where this led: Erving Winslow warned that authorizing Washington to "secure the collection and disbursement of the revenue in the interest of American capitalists" in Honduras and Nicaragua risked "further interferences and ultimate control" (Doc. 6). He was describing the mechanism by which financial leverage became political control — and insisting that Latin Americans possessed "their independent right to govern (or misgovern) their own countries."

[Sourcing +1 — Doc. 5: Taft is defending his own administration's record in an annual message to Congress; his purpose is self-justification, so his framing of dollar diplomacy as peaceful, modern statecraft presents the policy in its best possible light. A reader should not take his benign description at face value — the document reveals how the administration *wanted* the policy seen, which itself shows the policy needed defending.]

Even Wilson, who had condemned dollar diplomacy as immoral, reached for the same tools. After the Tampico incident he asked Congress to let him use "the armed forces of the United States" against the Mexican leader Huerta to vindicate American "rights and dignity" (Doc. 7), and American troops occupied Veracruz in 1914. When Pancho Villa raided Columbus, New Mexico, in 1916, Wilson sent General Pershing's expedition deep into Mexico — nearly a year of pursuit that never caught Villa. Mexico, the largest and proudest of the region's republics, absorbed American blows without surrendering its revolution. [Evidence beyond documents — second piece: the 1916 Punitive Expedition, reinforcing the "resistance limited control" half of the argument.]

The effects were therefore real but bounded. [Complexity +1: the essay qualifies its own argument rather than arguing one side throughout — American power genuinely expanded (canal, protectorates, investments secured), *but* Latin American resistance repeatedly limited what Washington could control (Mexico defied intervention; the policy generated the nationalism it sought to manage). It also confronts a contradiction head-on: Wilson's "moral" diplomacy produced a military occupation, which complicates any simple story about changing presidential philosophies.] The United States got its canal, its coaling stations, and its loan repayments; it did not get obedient neighbors. Each intervention taught Latin American nationalists the same lesson Winslow tried to teach Americans: that protection, once accepted, becomes control. By 1917 the hemisphere stood more firmly under Washington's shadow than in 1899 — and more resentful of the shadow.

[Evidence 2/2: the essay uses the content of all 7 documents (Docs. 1–7), and each is deployed in support of the argument rather than merely summarized — Docs. 1–3 establish the protectorate system and its doctrine, Doc. 4 and Doc. 6 supply the economic-interest and critical counter-voices, Doc. 5 the dollar-diplomacy mechanism, Doc. 7 the continuity of intervention under Wilson.]
