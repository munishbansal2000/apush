# DBQ Exemplar — test-03 (barrons-2027-ch02-dbq): World War I and Change, 1915–1935

**Prompt:** Evaluate the extent to which American participation in World War I fostered political, economic, and social change in the United States in the period from 1915 to 1935.

**Target score: 7/7.** (Bracketed notes are teacher margin annotations, not student text.)

When the United States declared war in April 1917, it ended nearly three years of neutrality and stepped into a total war that would reach into factories, draft boards, and newspapers. The war arrived at the tail end of the Progressive Era, when reformers had already stretched federal power over railroads, food, and drugs — but nothing on the scale that mobilization would demand. [Contextualization +1: situates the war within Progressive Era precedents and the neutrality-to-intervention arc, establishing what "change" would be measured against.] The war fostered real but uneven and partly reversible change: it dramatically expanded federal power — criminalizing dissent and directing industry — and it accelerated democratic claims, as Black Americans turned Wilson's rhetoric against domestic racism and women's wartime service propelled suffrage. Yet the postwar years reversed much of this through nativist backlash and a deliberate return to normalcy. The war's most lasting legacy was therefore the precedent of an activist federal state, not a permanent social transformation. [Thesis +1: a historically defensible claim establishing a line of reasoning about extent — real but uneven and partly reversed, with the most durable change identified.]

The war's political change was coercive as well as inspiring. Weeks after the declaration of war, Congress amended the Espionage Act to punish anyone who would "willfully utter, print, write, or publish any disloyal, profane, scurrilous, or abusive language" about the government or the military (Doc 1) — dissent itself became a federal crime. Enforcement was not only statutory. A Wisconsin newspaper reported, without a hint of condemnation, that Adolph Anton had been dragged from his home and "given a coat of tar and feathers for alleged pro-German sentiments" (Doc 3). [Sourcing +1 — Doc 3: a local newspaper reporting vigilante violence in a flat, matter-of-fact tone. The writer uses the document's tone and purpose — to report, not to condemn — as evidence: the casualness reveals how community violence enforced wartime conformity, the social dimension of the war's political change.] The Supreme Court ratified this suppression in Schenck v. United States (1919), upholding Espionage Act convictions under the new "clear and present danger" test. [Evidence beyond the documents +1: a specific Supreme Court decision outside the documents, used to support the argument — a known student bleed point, handled here with a concrete fact rather than a vague gesture.]

The same war that crushed dissent also armed democratic claims. The federal government seized unprecedented control of the economy: the War Industries Board, chaired by Bernard Baruch, directed the iron and steel supply through "strict conservation" and federally managed "expansion of existing sources" (Doc 4) — private industry taking direction from Washington, a rehearsal for later twentieth-century economic intervention. And Wilson's own war rhetoric became a lever in other hands. Black editor William Charles Morris's open letter, "Mr. President, Why Not Make America Safe for Democracy?", turned the president's crusade back on the nation, demanding democracy at home for Black Americans facing lynching and disfranchisement. [Sourcing +1 — Doc 2: Morris writes as a Black newspaper editor in an open letter addressed to the president; his purpose is to wield Wilson's democratic war aims as a weapon for civil rights. The writer shows how the author's position and the letter's form shape its meaning — the war's ideals matter here because its opponents could quote them.] Wilson himself was converted by the war's logic: appearing personally before the Senate in September 1918, he asked whether the nation could "ask and take the utmost that our women can give — service and sacrifice of every kind — and still say we do not see what title that gives them to stand by our sides" (Doc 5). [Sourcing +1 — Doc 5: a wartime president personally lobbying senators for the suffrage amendment. The writer notes that even Wilson needed women's war service as his argument — the document's purpose reveals how the conflict accelerated the Nineteenth Amendment, ratified in 1920. A third sourced document is good teaching even though only two are required.]

But the postwar years showed how reversible wartime change could be. A 1919 cartoon, drawn months after the armistice amid the postwar Red Scare, depicted the United States as unable to "digest" undesirable newcomers — the melting pot in reverse, aimed at immigrants and radicals (Doc 6). [Sourcing +1 — Doc 6: the cartoon's historical situation — the months after the armistice, amid nativism and the Red Scare — explains its meaning. The writer uses situation to show the postwar reversal: wartime unity curdling into suspicion of foreigners and dissenters.] Warren Harding, campaigning for president in May 1920, promised a business audience "not nostrums, but normalcy; not revolution, but restoration" and warned that "excess of government offer no substitute for quality of citizenship" (Doc 7). [Sourcing +1 — Doc 7: Harding speaks as a Republican candidate to businessmen weary of war-era controls; his purpose is to promise a retreat from wartime activism. The writer connects the audience and purpose to the argument: normalcy was a deliberate political choice to dismantle the war's changes, not a natural fading.] The Great Migration continued, and the Nineteenth Amendment survived — but the wartime promises of equality were largely deferred.

The complexity is that the war changed different things in different ways, and the political change outlasted the social. [Complexity +1: the writer qualifies the thesis rather than arguing one side — distinguishing political/economic change (more durable, precedent-setting) from social change (real but reversed), and noting that the war simultaneously expanded democracy's promise and the state's power to crush dissent. This is the complexity bleed point handled correctly: genuine qualification, not more evidence.] Federal power over speech and industry was the change that stuck, because later crises — the Depression above all — would reach for the same tools. The democratic claims the war accelerated were real, but for Black Americans especially they remained claims, not victories, until decades later. To say the war "fostered change" is therefore true and misleading at once: it fostered the instruments of change far more reliably than the changes themselves.
