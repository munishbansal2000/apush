## The essay

England came late to colonizing North America, and it did so through private companies and religious exiles rather than a state-directed empire like Spain's. That background matters because it meant the colonies' characters were set by whoever organized them: in the 1630s, religious turmoil under Charles I pushed thousands of Puritans across the Atlantic in the Great Migration, while Virginia had been founded a generation earlier by investors chasing profit. [Contextualization +1: England's company-driven colonization and the religious upheavals behind the Great Migration are broader developments beyond the two colonies that explain why they differed.]

Massachusetts Bay and Virginia differed fundamentally in purpose and social order — a Puritan religious refuge built on towns and families versus a profit-driven plantation colony built on bound labor — though the difference should not be overstated: both maintained English legal traditions and a strong English identity, and both expanded at Native peoples' expense. [Thesis +1: a historically defensible claim that establishes a line of reasoning — purpose and social order as the axes of difference — and already signals the qualification that will become complexity, rather than restating the prompt.]

The difference began with why each colony existed. Virginia was a joint-stock venture: Jamestown was founded in 1607 to find gold and a passage to Asia, and it nearly failed until John Rolfe's tobacco experiments of the 1610s gave it a profitable crop. [Evidence +1 (identified): two specifics — Jamestown (1607) and Rolfe's tobacco (1610s).] Massachusetts Bay was a religious mission: John Winthrop's 1630 sermon calling the colony a "city upon a hill" told Puritan migrants they were building a godly model society, not a business. [Evidence identified: Winthrop's sermon, 1630.] That difference in purpose produced a difference in who came. Virginia attracted mostly young, single men seeking wealth; Massachusetts Bay attracted whole families seeking a permanent religious home — which is why New England developed stable towns with churches and schools almost immediately, including Harvard College in 1636, while Virginia's early decades were marked by high mortality and social instability. [Evidence +1 (support): the examples are tied directly to the argument — purpose explains migration patterns, which explain social structure. Harvard (1636) is used as evidence of the town-and-family order, not just named.]

Labor made the social orders diverge most sharply. Virginia's headright system granted land to anyone who paid for a migrant's passage, which entrenched indentured servitude — and after Bacon's Rebellion in 1676 frightened planters with the prospect of armed, landless freedmen, the colony shifted decisively toward enslaved African labor, building a planter aristocracy atop a Black majority workforce. [Evidence identified: headright system, Bacon's Rebellion (1676).] New England, with its rocky soil and mixed subsistence farming, never developed plantation agriculture; its labor came from family members and town neighbors, producing a more egalitarian — though still hierarchical — society of independent farmers. [Evidence tied to the comparison: the environmental and economic reason for the labor difference is explained.] Even politics reflected the contrast: Virginia's House of Burgesses (1619) represented property-holding planters, while Massachusetts town meetings gave ordinary male church members a direct voice — broader participation, but only within a religiously uniform community that banished dissenters like Roger Williams and Anne Hutchinson. [Evidence identified: Burgesses (1619); support: the Williams/Hutchinson detail qualifies the "broader participation" claim, showing the essay arguing with nuance.]

Yet the contrast has limits, and the honest comparison must admit them. Both colonies kept English common law, English property rights, and an English identity their colonists were proud of. Both expanded by displacing Native peoples — Virginia through the Anglo-Powhatan wars, New England through the Pequot War of 1636–37 and King Philip's War. And both relied on bound labor in some form; New England had indentured servants too, just fewer. [Analysis & Reasoning +1: comparison structures the entire essay — every paragraph weighs the two colonies against each other on purpose, migration, labor, and politics — rather than describing each in isolation.]

So the colonies differed profoundly in why they were founded and how their societies were organized, but they were recognizably products of the same English colonial world: the difference was in the social order built on top of shared foundations, not in the foundations themselves. [Complexity +1: the essay qualifies its own thesis — the differences were real but bounded by shared Englishness, shared displacement of Natives, and shared use of bound labor — and it acknowledges the strongest counterevidence (Virginia also had representative government; New England also persecuted dissenters). Qualification plus contradiction, woven through rather than bolted on.]

**Teaching note on complexity:** On a comparison prompt, the easiest complexity move is the one modeled here: after establishing the difference, spend one paragraph on what was *shared*. "Both were English" is not a retreat from the thesis — it is the qualification that makes the thesis credible. Examiners reward the student who can hold difference and similarity in the same argument.
