## Essay

In the early 1700s, religion in the British colonies was dominated by established churches, but many colonists felt that religious life had become formal and uninspired. The colonies were also very separate from each other, with little contact between regions. The First Great Awakening was a series of religious revivals in the 1730s and 1740s that changed colonial religion. The Awakening transformed American identity to a significant extent by creating a shared religious experience across the colonies and encouraging colonists to think for themselves, although it did not unite the colonies politically. [Thesis +1: defensible claim answering "how far," with a line of reasoning — religious transformation, political limits.] [Contextualization +1: sets the pre-Awakening scene — established churches, formalism, colonial separateness — giving the change something to be measured against.]

One example is George Whitefield, an English preacher who toured the colonies in 1739–1741 and drew huge crowds. Another example is Jonathan Edwards, who preached "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God" in 1741. The revivals also caused a split between Old Lights and New Lights. New colleges were founded, such as the College of New Jersey in 1746. The Awakening encouraged individual religious judgment. Over time, colonial religion changed while colonial governments stayed the same. [Evidence +1 (identification): Whitefield's tours, Edwards's sermon, the Old Light/New Light split, the College of New Jersey — 2+ specific examples named.] [Evidence +0 (support): the examples are listed, not used. No sentence explains *how* Whitefield's crowds created a shared identity, or *how* the Old/New Light split shows both transformation and limits — the paragraph reads as a list of facts adjacent to the thesis rather than evidence for it.] [Analysis & Reasoning +1 (continuity-and-change frame): the essay does organize around change over time — "colonial religion changed while colonial governments stayed the same."]

In conclusion, the First Great Awakening transformed American national identity to a significant extent in religious life but not in political life. [Complexity 0: the conclusion restates the thesis without qualifying, complicating, or weighing anything. The essay already *contains* the raw material for complexity — a movement that united and divided simultaneously — but never notices the tension. One sentence ("the same revivals that gave colonists a shared experience also split their churches") would have opened the point.]

## Why this is a 4, not a 6

A knowledgeable student who writes like a tour guide: "here is a fact, here is another fact." The fix is a one-sentence habit after each example — "this matters to my argument because..." The 6-point version of this essay would keep every fact here and add only the connective tissue: Whitefield's crowds as *the first shared intercolonial experience*, the schism as *the limit of the transformation*. Facts don't argue; the sentences between them do.
