# DBQ Exemplar — test-02 (barrons-2027-pt1-dbq): Johnson vs Congress, 1865–1869

**Prompt:** Between 1865 and 1869, President Andrew Johnson clashed with congressional Republicans over Reconstruction in the South. Evaluate the relative importance of the causes of this conflict.

**Target score: 7/7.** (Bracketed notes are teacher margin annotations, not student text.)

Lincoln's assassination in April 1865 elevated Andrew Johnson — a Southern Democrat from Tennessee who had owned slaves — to the presidency just as the war ended. The Thirteenth Amendment, ratified that December, ended slavery but said nothing about what freedom would mean. Congress had already shown it wanted harsher terms than Lincoln planned: the Wade-Davis Bill of 1864, which Lincoln pocket-vetoed, would have made readmission much harder for southern states. Johnson therefore inherited not just the presidency but an unresolved argument over who would control Reconstruction. [Contextualization +1: situates the conflict in Lincoln's death, the limits of the Thirteenth Amendment, and the earlier Wade-Davis fight — the broader setting that made the Johnson–Congress collision likely.] The conflict was caused above all by a fundamental disagreement over what emancipation required: the southern Black Codes convinced congressional Republicans that freedom was meaningless without federal protection of Black civil and political rights — a demand that freedpeople themselves pressed on Congress — while Johnson's commitment to rapid, lenient restoration led him to shield the southern states that wrote those codes. Partisan calculations about a new Black electorate sharpened the fight, but they were secondary: the moral and constitutional stakes drove it. [Thesis +1: a historically defensible claim establishing a line of reasoning about relative importance — ranks the moral/constitutional cause above the partisan one.]

The Black Codes were the provocation. Months after emancipation, Mississippi made it a crime for "freedmen, free negroes and mulattoes" to be found "with no lawful employment or business" or "unlawful assembling themselves together" (Doc 2) — vagrancy law as a tool to criminalize Black freedom itself. Louisiana went further, binding laborers to contracts they could not leave "until the fulfillment of his contract," under ten-hour days and punishment for "impudence, swearing, or indecent language" toward an employer (Doc 3). Northern readers did not have to imagine how these statutes worked in practice: Frank Leslie's Illustrated Newspaper showed a Black freedman being auctioned on a platform in Monticello, Florida, sold into labor to satisfy a vagrancy fine (Doc 4). [Sourcing +1 — Doc 4: this is a Northern illustrated newspaper depicting southern injustice for a Northern middle-class audience; its purpose is to dramatize the Black Codes into visceral outrage that builds political pressure for congressional action. The writer treats it as evidence of how the North was mobilized, not as a neutral record — and notes, honestly, that a Northern paper's depiction may heighten the drama even as it illustrates practices the codes themselves authorized.] To Republicans, this looked like slavery reborn under another name, and it radicalized them against Johnson's leniency.

The Republican response drew directly on what freedpeople themselves demanded. The South Carolina Colored People's Convention petitioned Congress in November 1865 for "the same laws which govern white men," jury rights, schools, and homesteads — "that we be dealt with as others, in equity and justice" (Doc 5). [Sourcing +1 — Doc 5: formerly enslaved South Carolinians organizing politically months after emancipation, petitioning Congress directly. The writer uses the document's point of view to establish Black political agency — freedpeople were not passive recipients of Reconstruction but active claimants whose demands the Radical Republicans took up. This shapes the argument: the push for federal protection came from below as well as from Congress.] Thaddeus Stevens gave that demand its moral formulation in Congress: "We have broken the material shackles of four million slaves," but freedom without equal law was a fraud — "every man, no matter what his race or colour... has an equal right to justice, honesty, and fair play" (Doc 7). Against this stood Johnson, who claimed the mantle of Lincoln's "malice toward none... let us strive on... to bind up the nation's wounds" (Doc 1). [Sourcing +1 — Doc 1: Lincoln speaks as a war president anticipating victory, setting a tone of reconciliation; Johnson invoked this precedent to justify rapid, lenient restoration. The writer shows how the document's situation — a leniency framed for a nation still at war — became Johnson's shield for defending the very legislatures writing the Black Codes. A third sourced document is good teaching even though only two are required.]

The concrete fights, beyond the documents, show how irreconcilable this became: in 1866 Johnson vetoed the Freedmen's Bureau Bill and the Civil Rights Act, and Congress overrode the Civil Rights veto — the first major override of a presidential veto in American history. [Evidence beyond the documents +1: specific veto fights outside the documents, used to support the argument. A known student bleed point — the point needs a concrete fact, not a vague reference to "laws they passed."] Congress then wrote its answer into the Constitution with the Fourteenth Amendment, ratified in 1868, and the conflict climaxed in Johnson's impeachment that same year after he defied the Tenure of Office Act.

Partisanship was real but secondary. Samuel Tilden, the Democrats' national leader, charged in 1868 that Republicans meant "to make the black race the governing power in those States" to deliver "twenty senators and fifty representatives practically appointed by itself in Washington" (Doc 6). [Sourcing +1 — Doc 6: Tilden speaks as a partisan opponent of Reconstruction to Democratic supporters; his purpose is to paint Republican policy as a cynical power grab. The writer treats the claim as partisan argument to be weighed, not as established fact — which is exactly what lets the essay evaluate its relative importance fairly.] The charge had a grain of truth — Republicans did stand to gain politically from Black suffrage — but the sequence matters: Republicans radicalized in response to the Black Codes of 1865 (Docs 2, 3), before Black voting existed to exploit. [Complexity +1: the writer weighs the partisan cause against the moral/constitutional one and qualifies the thesis — acknowledging the grain of truth in Tilden's charge while showing, from the documents' chronology, that substance drove the conflict and partisanship intensified it. This is the complexity bleed point handled correctly: relative importance argued with evidence, not asserted.] Johnson, for his part, was not purely cynical either — his leniency had a constitutional theory of rapid restoration — but by defending the Black-Code South he made Congress's case for federal intervention himself. The conflict's deepest cause was therefore the meaning of emancipation; the fight over who would profit from it politically was the accelerant, not the spark.
