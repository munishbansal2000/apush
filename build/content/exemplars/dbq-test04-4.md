# DBQ Exemplar — test-04 (princeton-25e/dbq-drill): Fears of Communism, 1940–1959

**Prompt:** Evaluate the extent to which fears of communism affected American society from 1940 to 1959.

**Target score: 4/7.** (Bracketed notes are teacher margin annotations, not student text.)

The United States had feared domestic radicals before — the First Red Scare of 1919–20, with the Palmer Raids against suspected anarchists and communists, showed how wartime and postwar anxiety could turn into government repression. The scare of the 1940s and 1950s grew from a more dangerous soil: the Soviet Union, America's wartime ally, became its Cold War enemy, and the Truman Doctrine of 1947 committed the United States to resisting communism everywhere. [Contextualization +1: situates the prompt in the First Red Scare and the Cold War's origins — genuine broader context.] Fears of communism affected American society to a great extent between 1940 and 1959: they reshaped national politics around accusations of disloyalty, constricted civil liberties, and reached into everyday life, although the fear was partly grounded in real Soviet spying. [Thesis +1: a defensible claim about extent with a line of reasoning (politics, liberties, daily life) and a nod toward qualification.]

The fear had a real foundation. A decoded 1944 KGB telegram from New York to Moscow shows a Soviet agent reporting success in recruiting sources inside the American atomic energy project — the Rosenberg spy network, in the cable's own operational language (Doc 1). Soviet espionage in the United States was not invented. But politicians inflated the threat beyond recognition. Senator Joseph McCarthy claimed in February 1950 that he held a list of 205 communists working in the State Department (Doc 6), a charge he could never substantiate — yet it made him the most famous anti-communist in America and terrified the federal workforce. Even President Truman, himself a Cold Warrior, warned in 1951 that Americans had grown so frightened of being called communists that some were afraid to sign their names to quotations from the Declaration of Independence (Doc 3). [Evidence +1 (first point): the content of three documents — Docs 1, 6, and 3 — is accurately described and used.]

The scare's effects went beyond Washington. The Rosenbergs were executed for atomic espionage in June 1953, the most dramatic proof the government could offer that the threat was real — and a warning to anyone tempted toward disloyalty. [Evidence beyond the documents +1: the Rosenberg executions are a specific fact outside the documents, used to support the argument.] By the mid-1950s, however, the fever was breaking: Edward R. Murrow's 1954 broadcasts and the televised Army–McCarthy hearings turned public opinion against McCarthy, and the Senate censured him that December.

In short, fear of communism touched nearly every part of American life in these years — politics, work, schools, and culture. It was not baseless, but it grew far beyond what the evidence justified, and it left a Cold War political culture that outlasted the scare itself.
