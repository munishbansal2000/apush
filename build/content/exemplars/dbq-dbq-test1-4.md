## Essay

American foreign policy changed a great deal between 1914 and 1917. The United States went from being strictly neutral in the European war to being on the edge of war with Germany, as each German action pushed America closer to the Allies. [Thesis +1: evaluates extent ("changed a great deal") with a line of reasoning — German actions driving an American shift from neutrality toward confrontation.]

At the start of the war, most Americans wanted nothing to do with Europe's fighting. The United States had a long tradition of staying out of European alliances, and millions of Americans had roots in the countries now at war with each other, so taking sides could have divided the country. This is why neutrality was the natural starting point in 1914. [Contextualization +1: broader background — the non-entanglement tradition and the divided immigrant population — explaining the 1914 starting point.]

President Wilson's August 1914 message captured that starting point. He urged Americans to act "in the true spirit of neutrality, which is the spirit of impartiality and fairness and friendliness to all concerned," and described the United States as "the one great nation at peace," ready to help mediate an end to the fighting (Doc. 1). This was a clear statement that America would not take sides.

Germany's actions soon made that position harder to hold. Before the Lusitania sailed in 1915, the German Embassy published a notice in American newspapers warning travelers that British ships were "liable to destruction" in the war zone and that passengers sailed "at their own risk" (Doc. 5). When Germany sank the Lusitania on May 7, 1915, killing nearly 1,200 people including 128 Americans, many Americans began to see Germany as a direct threat. [Evidence beyond documents +1: the Lusitania sinking — specific outside facts (date, death toll, American dead) supporting the argument that German escalation drove the change.]

By 1916 Wilson's tone had completely changed. During the Sussex crisis he told Congress that unless Germany abandoned submarine warfare against passenger ships, the United States would have "no choice but to sever diplomatic relations with the Government of the German Empire altogether" (Doc. 7). A president who had once offered impartial mediation was now delivering ultimatums — clear evidence that American policy had shifted from neutrality toward confrontation.

In conclusion, American foreign policy changed substantially between 1914 and 1917, moving step by step from neutrality to the brink of war as Germany's submarine campaign forced Wilson's hand.
