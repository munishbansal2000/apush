## Essay

American foreign policy changed fundamentally between 1914 and 1917, moving from genuine — if imperfect — neutrality to open confrontation with Germany. Yet the change was gradual and partly disguised: the *language* of neutrality persisted long after the *practice* had tilted toward the Allies, and the decisive push came less from any American plan than from Germany's own escalation. By 1917 the United States stood on the edge of war, still speaking the vocabulary of 1914. [Thesis +1: evaluates *extent* ("fundamentally... yet gradual and partly disguised"), establishes a line of reasoning (rhetoric vs. practice; German escalation as driver), and previews the qualification that earns complexity later.]

The starting point was a deep American tradition. Since Washington's Farewell Address, the United States had avoided entangling alliances with Europe, and in 1914 that tradition felt like common sense: the American population included millions of German, Irish, British, and French immigrants with divided sympathies, so taking sides risked tearing the country apart. At the same time, Britain's navy dominated the Atlantic, which meant American trade would flow to the Allies whether Washington willed it or not — and Allied war orders were already pulling the American economy into the conflict. [Contextualization +1: broader context — the non-entanglement tradition, the divided immigrant population, British sea power, and the economic pull of Allied orders — explaining both why neutrality was the starting point and why it was fragile.]

Wilson opened with a classic statement of that tradition. In August 1914 he called on Americans to act "in the true spirit of neutrality, which is the spirit of impartiality and fairness and friendliness to all concerned," presenting the United States as "the one great nation at peace," ready to mediate rather than fight (Doc. 1). But neutrality was contested from the start. Harvard professor Hugo Munsterberg wrote to Wilson complaining that American policy in practice favored Britain: wireless news was censored while British cables passed freely, and the "unlimited sale of ammunition to the belligerents" overwhelmingly armed the Allies (Doc. 2).

[Sourcing +1 — Doc. 2: Munsterberg writes as a German-American intellectual sympathetic to Germany, addressing the president directly. His point of view shapes the letter into a catalog of grievances — every example is chosen to prove pro-British bias. That does not make him wrong (the ammunition sales really did favor the Allies), but it means the document is evidence that neutrality was *perceived as* and *functioned as* partial, as seen through pro-German eyes. A hostile witness to neutrality's purity is still a useful witness, if read for what he is.]

The administration's defense revealed how thin the line had become. Secretary of State Bryan answered charges of partiality point by point, insisting there was "no power in the Executive to prevent the sale of ammunition" and that British advantages at sea were simply "the business of a belligerent," not America's doing (Doc. 4). Meanwhile the pressures mounted on both sides: Lansing recalled that Britain's blockade seized American ships and bled their owners through endless port detentions (Doc. 3) — yet American trade with the Allies only grew, because Britain controlled the sea lanes.

[Sourcing +1 — Doc. 4: Bryan writes as the administration's chief diplomat answering a Senate committee; his purpose is rebuttal — to defuse accusations of pro-British favoritism. The letter's legalistic tone ("no Hague convention which deals with absolute or conditional contraband") is the sound of a government defending a position it cannot quite admit: that "neutrality" had become a legal fiction covering an economic alliance with the Allies. The document's defensiveness is itself evidence of how contested the policy was.]

Germany's escalation then did what Allied pressure could not: it turned an economic tilt into a political crisis. Days before the Lusitania sailed, the German Embassy placed a notice in American newspapers warning travelers that British vessels were "liable to destruction" in the war zone and sailed "at their own risk" (Doc. 5) — a warning that reads, in hindsight, like premeditation. When the Lusitania was torpedoed on May 7, 1915, killing nearly 1,200 people including 128 Americans, the American public learned from the customs inspector's report that the ship had carried over 5,400 cases of ammunition consigned to the British government (Doc. 6). The episode captured the whole dilemma: American commerce with Britain was both the engine of prosperity and the reason Americans kept dying. [Evidence beyond documents +1 (first piece): the Lusitania sinking — date, death toll, American dead — specific outside facts that deepen Doc. 6 and drive the argument.]

By 1916 the vocabulary of neutrality was being used to deliver ultimatums. After the Sussex incident Wilson told Congress that unless Germany abandoned submarine warfare against passenger and freight vessels, the United States would "sever diplomatic relations with the Government of the German Empire altogether," casting America as "the responsible spokesmen of the rights of humanity" (Doc. 7). A president who in 1914 had offered impartial mediation was now threatening a break in relations — the endpoint of the change. When Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare on February 1, 1917, and the Zimmermann Telegram proposing a German-Mexican alliance surfaced weeks later, Congress declared war on April 6, 1917. [Evidence beyond documents +1 (further pieces): unrestricted submarine warfare's resumption, the Zimmermann Telegram, and the war declaration — the outside facts that complete the arc.]

The change was therefore real, but it was not a single clean break. [Complexity +1: the essay qualifies its extent judgment on three fronts — (1) neutrality was never pure, since trade and finance tied the U.S. to the Allies from 1914; (2) the *rhetoric* of neutrality outlasted the *practice*, which complicates any simple "neutral-to-belligerent" story; (3) German actions, more than American decisions, forced each step, so the change was partly something that happened *to* American policy rather than something Washington chose. It thus explains both change and its limits instead of arguing one side.] In 1914 the United States claimed to stand above the war; by 1917 it stood inside it, still insisting it fought for humanity rather than for allies — the old exceptionalism surviving inside the new belligerence.

[Evidence 2/2: all 7 documents support the argument — Doc. 1 the neutrality baseline, Docs. 2–4 the contested, increasingly fictional neutrality of 1914–15, Docs. 5–6 the German escalation and its entanglement with American commerce, Doc. 7 the endpoint.]
