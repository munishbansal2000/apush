# DBQ Exemplar — test-04 (princeton-25e/dbq-drill): Fears of Communism, 1940–1959

**Prompt:** Evaluate the extent to which fears of communism affected American society from 1940 to 1959.

**Target score: 7/7.** (Bracketed notes are teacher margin annotations, not student text.)

America had feared radicals before. The First Red Scare of 1919–20 — with its Palmer Raids and nativist cartoons like the 1919 image of an immigrant anarchist, knife and bomb in hand, sneaking up behind the Statue of Liberty (Doc 5) — showed how quickly fear of foreign radicalism could turn inward against immigrants and dissenters. The 1940–59 scare grew from newer soil: the wartime alliance with the Soviet Union collapsed into the Cold War, and the Truman Doctrine of 1947 committed the United States to containing communism worldwide. Anti-communism was now not a passing panic but a permanent feature of American politics. [Contextualization +1: situates the prompt in both the earlier Red Scare and the Cold War's origins, establishing continuity and what was new.] Fears of communism affected American society deeply between 1940 and 1959 — reshaping national politics, constricting civil liberties, and reaching into classrooms and popular culture. But the fear was neither baseless nor boundless: it was grounded in real Soviet espionage, exaggerated and exploited for political gain, and eventually checked by public pushback. Its extent was therefore great in political life, real but thinner in daily life, and self-limiting by the mid-1950s. [Thesis +1: a historically defensible claim establishing a line of reasoning about extent — deep but qualified three ways (grounded, exploited, checked).]

Some of the fear was grounded in fact. A decoded 1944 KGB telegram from New York to Moscow reports an agent's success in recruiting sources inside the American atomic energy project — the Rosenberg network, described in the cable's own operational language (Doc 1). [Sourcing +1 — Doc 1: this is an intercepted operational report from a Soviet intelligence officer to his headquarters, not propaganda written for Americans. The writer uses the document's point of view and purpose to establish that Soviet espionage in the United States was real — which is exactly what lets the essay argue the fear was "grounded" rather than purely hysterical.] Julius and Ethel Rosenberg would be executed for atomic espionage in June 1953, the most dramatic proof that the threat was not invented. But real espionage became the seedbed for reckless accusation. Senator Joseph McCarthy told a Wheeling, West Virginia audience in February 1950 that he held a list of 205 communists working in the State Department (Doc 6). [Sourcing +1 — Doc 6: McCarthy speaks as an ambitious senator addressing a Republican women's club, with the press carrying his words nationwide; his purpose is to gain national attention and political power. The writer shows how the speaker's situation explains the charge's recklessness — the "list" was a bid for fame, which is why it cannot be read as evidence of actual infiltration.] Representative John F. Kennedy showed how the fear worked as a partisan weapon: in January 1949 he blamed the Truman administration for the "disaster" of the communist victory in China and demanded a harder anti-communist line (Doc 2) — "losing China" becoming a charge no Democrat could afford to ignore.

The fear constricted American freedom itself, and the documents show it operating at every level. President Truman warned in 1951 that Americans had grown so frightened of being called communists that some were afraid to sign their names to quotations from the Declaration of Independence (Doc 3). [Sourcing +1 — Doc 3: a sitting president decrying the scare's excesses while defending his own anti-communism. The writer captures Truman's dual role — Cold Warrior and critic of the witch hunt at once — which is what makes the document evidence of the fear's height rather than just one more partisan attack. A third sourced document is good teaching even though only two are required.] The scare reached into children's daily lives as a business: a 1955 advertisement in Civil Defense magazine marketed metal identification tags for schoolchildren to wear during atomic-bomb evacuation drills, so that parents could identify their children after an attack (Doc 4). [Sourcing +1 — Doc 4: a commercial advertiser selling a product to schools and parents. The writer uses the document's commercial purpose to show fear commodified and embedded in the classroom — the scare as a market, not just a politics.] Beyond the documents, the House Un-American Activities Committee's 1947 Hollywood hearings blacklisted screenwriters and actors — the Hollywood Ten — for refusing to answer questions about their politics, showing how the fear policed culture as well as government. [Evidence beyond the documents +1: a specific fact outside the documents (HUAC, the Hollywood Ten blacklist), used to support the argument — a known student bleed point, handled with a concrete fact.]

Yet the scare met resistance, and that resistance defines its limits. Edward R. Murrow told his television audience in 1954 that McCarthy confused dissent with disloyalty, that "accusation is not proof," and that the senator's methods threatened the very freedoms Americans claimed to defend (Doc 7). [Sourcing +1 — Doc 7: a respected broadcast journalist speaking to a national television audience. The writer uses Murrow's establishment position to mark a turning point: when mainstream media pushed back, McCarthy's influence began to collapse — evidence that the scare had limits.] The televised Army–McCarthy hearings that same year exposed McCarthy's bullying to millions of viewers, and the Senate censured him in December 1954. The First Red Scare imagery of Doc 5, echoed four decades later, shows the continuity — but the Second Red Scare was larger, longer, and armed with the atomic age.

The complexity is that the fear's extent cannot be captured by a single verdict. [Complexity +1: the writer qualifies the thesis three ways instead of arguing one side — the fear was grounded in real espionage yet exaggerated for gain; it reshaped politics deeply but daily life more thinly; it was checked from within by figures like Truman and Murrow. This is the complexity bleed point handled correctly: genuine qualification of the extent claim, not additional evidence.] It was most powerful where it fused with institutions — Congress, the courts, the blacklist — and weakest where Americans pushed back. To say it "affected American society" is true; to say how much requires saying where, and for how long. By the late 1950s the fever had broken, but the Cold War consensus it built — that anti-communism was the price of political legitimacy — would shape American politics for another generation.
