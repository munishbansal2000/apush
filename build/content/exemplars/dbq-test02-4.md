# DBQ Exemplar — test-02 (barrons-2027-pt1-dbq): Johnson vs Congress, 1865–1869

**Prompt:** Between 1865 and 1869, President Andrew Johnson clashed with congressional Republicans over Reconstruction in the South. Evaluate the relative importance of the causes of this conflict.

**Target score: 4/7.** (Bracketed notes are teacher margin annotations, not student text.)

When Lincoln was assassinated in April 1865, Andrew Johnson — a Southern Democrat from Tennessee — became president at the exact moment the nation had to decide what to do with the defeated South. The Thirteenth Amendment ended slavery in December 1865, but it did not say what rights freedpeople would have, and Congress and the president disagreed sharply about who should decide. [Contextualization +1: places the conflict in Lincoln's assassination and the unfinished meaning of emancipation — genuine broader context.] The most important cause of the conflict was the southern Black Codes, which convinced Republicans that Johnson's lenient Reconstruction was allowing slavery to return in a new form; the demands of freedpeople themselves and the moral arguments of Radical leaders were also important, while partisan advantage mattered less. [Thesis +1: a defensible claim ranking the causes — Black Codes first, partisan motives last.]

The Black Codes showed Republicans that southern legislatures could not be trusted with Reconstruction. Mississippi's 1865 code declared that freedpeople found "with no lawful employment or business" would "be deemed vagrants," subject to fines and imprisonment (Doc 2) — a law that effectively criminalized Black unemployment. Louisiana's labor regulations bound workers to contracts they could not leave, under ten-hour summer days, with "impudence" toward an employer punished as disobedience (Doc 3). These were not the acts of states preparing for a free-labor South; they were an attempt to restore white control of Black labor, and they made Johnson's rapid restoration of those same legislatures look like a betrayal of emancipation. [Evidence +1 (first point): the content of three documents — Docs 2, 3, and 5 below — is accurately described and used.]

Freedpeople themselves pushed Congress to act. The South Carolina Colored People's Convention petitioned Congress in November 1865 asking for "the same laws which govern white men," jury rights, schools, and homesteads (Doc 5). This gave Radical Republicans in Congress both a moral cause and a political constituency to organize around. The conflict turned into open warfare in 1866, when Johnson vetoed the Civil Rights Act and Congress overrode his veto — the first time Congress had overridden a presidential veto on such major legislation — proving that neither side would back down. [Evidence beyond the documents +1: the 1866 Civil Rights Act veto and override is a specific fact outside the documents, used to support the argument.]

In short, the Black Codes radicalized Congress, freedpeople's demands gave the Radicals their cause, and Johnson's defense of the southern states made the collision unavoidable. Partisan motives played a role, but the heart of the fight was what freedom would mean.
