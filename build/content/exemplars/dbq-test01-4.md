# DBQ Exemplar — test-01 (5s24-ch03-dbq): Federalist Administrations, 1789–1801

**Prompt:** To what extent did the Federalist administrations of George Washington and John Adams promote national unity and advance the authority of the federal government in the period 1789–1801?

**Target score: 4/7.** (Bracketed notes are teacher margin annotations, not student text.)

After the Revolution, the Articles of Confederation gave the national government almost no power. It could not levy taxes or pay the war debt, and Shays' Rebellion in 1786 showed it could not even put down an uprising in Massachusetts. The Constitution of 1787 created a stronger federal government, and Washington's election in 1789 was the first test of whether it would actually work. [Contextualization +1: connects the prompt to the failure of the Articles and the ratification of the Constitution — genuine broader context, not just restating the prompt.] The Federalist administrations of Washington and Adams promoted national unity and advanced the authority of the federal government to a large extent by strengthening the nation's finances, enforcing its laws, and defending the government during a time of international crisis, although some of their measures were controversial. [Thesis +1: a defensible claim with a line of reasoning (finances, law enforcement, crisis defense).]

Washington set out to unite the country behind the new government. In his first inaugural address he promised that "no local prejudices or attachments, no separate views or party animosities" would misdirect Congress, and that national policy would rest on "the pure and immutable principles of private morality" (Doc 1). This was a president asking Americans to put the nation above their states and factions. Hamilton then gave the government the tools to act: his 1791 opinion on the Bank argued that constitutional powers "ought to be construed liberally in advancement of the public good" (Doc 4), which justified a national bank and a broad reading of federal power. [Evidence +1 (first point): the content of three documents — Docs 1, 4, and 6 below — is accurately described and used to address the prompt.]

The Federalists also showed that federal law would be obeyed. The Sedition Act of 1798 punished anyone who would "write, print, utter, or publish, any false, scandalous, and malicious writing" against the government with fines and up to two years in prison (Doc 6). This was a strong assertion of federal authority — the government would not tolerate attacks meant to undermine it. The context was the undeclared naval Quasi-War with France in 1798, a genuine international crisis in which French ships were seizing American merchant vessels; the Adams administration saw domestic criticism as a danger the young nation could not afford. [Evidence beyond the documents +1: the Quasi-War is a specific fact outside the documents, used to support the argument.]

In the end, the Federalist years left the United States with a stronger national government than the one Washington inherited — a funded debt, a national bank, and the demonstrated willingness to enforce federal law. Their policies were controversial, but they gave the republic the authority it needed to survive its first decade.
