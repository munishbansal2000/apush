# LEQ Exemplar — leq-test3-spare-a (test-07) — target score 6/6

**Prompt:** Assess how far the First Great Awakening transformed American national identity between 1700 and 1765.
**Period:** U2 · **Themes:** SOC, NAT · **Reasoning:** Continuity & Change
**Score earned: 6/6** — Thesis 1 · Contextualization 1 · Evidence 2 · Analysis & Reasoning 2

---

## Essay

In the early 1700s, the British colonies in North America were religiously divided and politically disconnected from one another. Congregational churches dominated New England and the Anglican Church held legal establishment in the South, but by the second and third generations many colonists felt the old churches had grown cold and formal, while educated elites were drifting toward Enlightenment rationalism. The colonies otherwise had little shared life: a farmer in Pennsylvania and a merchant in Massachusetts had almost no common experience. Into this landscape came the First Great Awakening of the 1730s and 1740s, a wave of emotional religious revivals. The Awakening significantly transformed colonial religious culture and gave colonists their first widely shared intercolonial experience — but it did not create a political national identity, because colonial governments, British authority, and most colonists' loyalty to the Crown continued unchanged. [Thesis +1: historically defensible claim that answers "how far" and establishes a line of reasoning — transformation in religion and intercolonial connection, continuity in politics.] [Contextualization +1: describes the broader religious and social landscape before the revivals — established churches, declining fervor, Enlightenment drift, colonial isolation — so the change the essay will describe has something to change *from*.]

The revivals changed how ordinary colonists experienced religion, and they did it across colonial borders at the same time. The English preacher George Whitefield toured the colonies in 1739–1741, drawing enormous crowds in city after city, and colonial preachers like Jonathan Edwards — whose 1741 sermon "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God" terrified and electrified listeners — spread the same message of personal conversion through a direct, emotional relationship with God. For the first time, colonists from New Hampshire to Georgia were reading about, discussing, and participating in the same event. The revivals also taught colonists to judge religious authority for themselves: if an unconverted minister could not save souls, as the revivalists insisted, then ordinary believers had the right to question their leaders. That habit of questioning established authority would matter long after the revivals faded. [Evidence +1 (identification): Whitefield's 1739–41 tours, Edwards's 1741 sermon, the doctrine of personal conversion and challenged ministerial authority — 2+ specific examples.] [Evidence +1 (support): each example is tied to the argument — Whitefield shows the *intercolonial* dimension of the transformation, and the challenge to ministers shows the *change in religious culture* the thesis claims.] [Analysis & Reasoning +1 (continuity-and-change frame): the paragraph is organized around what changed — cross-colony shared experience and the basis of religious authority — against the implied continuity of the old order.]

The transformation had clear limits, and in some ways the Awakening divided as much as it united. The revivals split churches into "New Lights," who embraced the emotional preaching, and "Old Lights," who condemned it as disorder — a schism that produced lasting new denominations, especially among Baptists, and new colleges such as the College of New Jersey, founded in 1746 by New Light Presbyterians. Yet while church life was transformed, political life was not: colonial assemblies kept operating as before, royal governors kept their authority, and colonists continued to think of themselves as British subjects whose liberties were protected by Parliament. The Awakening changed who colonists believed could speak for God; it did not change who they believed should govern them. That is the measure of its transformation: profound in religious culture, real in creating shared colonial experience, but stopping short of anything like a political nation. [Evidence +1 (identification): Old Light/New Light schism, Baptist growth, the College of New Jersey (1746) — 2+ specific examples.] [Evidence +1 (support): the examples directly support the thesis's limit — they show religious transformation alongside political continuity.] [Complexity +1: the essay holds change and continuity together in one judgment — the Awakening *united and divided at once* (shared experience vs. church schism), and transformed religious authority while political structures persisted. The contradiction is integrated into the argument, not appended to it.]

## Complexity, demystified

This essay earns complexity through **change-within-continuity** — the single most reliable complexity move for "how far" and "to what extent" prompts. The structure is: the transformation was real *and* it stopped at a boundary. Notice the final paragraph does not say "on the other hand, some historians disagree" — it says the same event (the Awakening) produced opposite effects (unity and division), and the same period saw deep change in one sphere (religion) and persistence in another (politics). One well-placed sentence of that shape — "it changed X but not Y" — is often the entire point.
