# LEQ Exemplar — leq-drill-main (test-08) — target score 6/6

**Prompt:** Assess how far the abolitionist movement fostered change in America between 1830 and 1860.
**Period:** U4 · **Themes:** SOC, PCE · **Reasoning:** Causation
**Score earned: 6/6** — Thesis 1 · Contextualization 1 · Evidence 2 · Analysis & Reasoning 2

---

## Essay

Before 1830, most organized opposition to slavery in America was gradual and cautious: northern states had passed gradual emancipation laws, and the American Colonization Society, founded in 1816, proposed freeing enslaved people only to ship them to Africa — a plan that asked nothing of white prejudice. Meanwhile the Second Great Awakening was spreading the perfectionist belief that Christians had a duty to purge sin from society, and the Market Revolution's printing presses and railroads were giving reformers a national audience for the first time. Into this moment stepped a new, uncompromising abolitionism. Between 1830 and 1860, the abolitionist movement fostered profound change: it forced slavery onto the national political agenda and reshaped the party system — but it changed politics far more than it changed white racial attitudes, and it freed almost no enslaved people directly before the Civil War. [Thesis +1: defensible claim answering "how far," with a clear line of reasoning — political transformation, limited moral and practical reach.] [Contextualization +1: places immediatism against what came before (gradualism, colonization) and within the broader forces (Second Great Awakening, print culture) that made the movement possible.]

The movement's first great change was moral and rhetorical: it made compromise with slavery shameful. When William Lloyd Garrison launched *The Liberator* in 1831, he demanded immediate emancipation and denounced the Constitution's compromises with slavery, declaring "I will be as harsh as truth, and as uncompromising as justice... AND I WILL BE HEARD." The American Anti-Slavery Society, founded in 1833, flooded Congress with antislavery petitions until the House answered with the 1836 "gag rule" tabling them unread — which only proved the abolitionists' point that slavery was corrupting republican government itself. Black abolitionists gave the movement its most devastating evidence: Frederick Douglass's *Narrative* (1845) put a first-person account of slavery's brutality into northern parlors, making an abstraction into a person. None of this freed anyone directly, but it destroyed the comfortable northern fiction that slavery was a distant southern problem. [Evidence +1 (identification): Garrison's *Liberator* (1831), the AASS petition campaign and gag rule (1836), Douglass's *Narrative* (1845) — 2+ specific examples.] [Evidence +1 (support): each example is tied to the argument — Garrison shows the moral absolutism, the gag rule shows slavery's corruption of national politics, Douglass shows how evidence was made personal; all support the claim about forcing slavery onto the agenda.] [Analysis & Reasoning +1 (causation frame): the paragraph traces cause and effect — immediatist rhetoric *caused* the gag-rule backlash, which *proved* the movement's deeper claim.]

The second great change was political. Moral suasion alone could not touch slavery, so abolitionists built political instruments: the Liberty Party in 1840, the Free Soil Party in 1848, and finally the Republican Party in the mid-1850s, which carried abolitionist energy into a coalition powerful enough to win the presidency in 1860. The Underground Railroad, meanwhile, turned antislavery principle into illegal action, aiding enslaved people escaping to the North in defiance of federal law. Southern reaction measured the movement's impact: where an earlier generation had called slavery a "necessary evil," John C. Calhoun now proclaimed it a "positive good" (1837), and mobs murdered the abolitionist editor Elijah Lovejoy in Illinois in 1837. Abolitionism had not ended slavery, but it had ended the national silence about it — and in doing so, it helped destroy the old party system and build the sectional crisis that led to war. [Evidence +1 (identification): Liberty/Free Soil/Republican parties, the Underground Railroad, Calhoun's "positive good," Lovejoy's murder — 2+ specific examples.] [Evidence +1 (support): tied to the claim — the parties show political reshaping, the southern reaction shows the movement forcing a national confrontation.] [Analysis & Reasoning +1 (causation): "helped destroy the old party system and build the sectional crisis" — the paragraph is built as a causal chain from movement to political transformation.]

Yet the movement's limits are part of its story, and they sharpen rather than weaken the assessment. Abolitionism's very absolutism narrowed its audience: most northerners in 1860 remained indifferent or openly racist, voted Republican to keep slavery out of the West rather than to help Black people, and would have rejected Garrison's demand for immediate emancipation. And the movement provoked a counter-change — its pressure drove the South from apology to aggressive defense of slavery, hardening the very institution it attacked. So the fair measure is this: abolitionism transformed American *politics* — the agenda, the parties, the sectional divide — while transforming far fewer American *minds*, and freeing almost no one until the war it helped bring about. A movement can be historically decisive without being immediately successful, and abolitionism is the proof. [Complexity +1: integrates qualification (politics changed more than minds), contradiction (the movement hardened proslavery ideology even as it attacked slavery), and a paradox held honestly — decisive yet unsuccessful. The limits are woven into the assessment, not bolted on.]

## Complexity, demystified

This essay's complexity move is **the honest paradox**: a cause can succeed and fail at the same time, in different spheres. The structure — "it transformed X while barely touching Y, and it provoked the opposite of what it wanted in Z" — lets the writer assess *extent* precisely, which is exactly what "assess how far" prompts reward. Students often think admitting limits weakens their thesis; here the limits *are* the thesis. The point was lost by more 2025 test-takers than any other (0.15/1) precisely because most essays pick "it changed a lot" or "it changed a little" and defend one side. The 6-point habit is refusing to pick.
