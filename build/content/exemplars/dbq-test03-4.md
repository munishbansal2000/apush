# DBQ Exemplar — test-03 (barrons-2027-ch02-dbq): World War I and Change, 1915–1935

**Prompt:** Evaluate the extent to which American participation in World War I fostered political, economic, and social change in the United States in the period from 1915 to 1935.

**Target score: 4/7.** (Bracketed notes are teacher margin annotations, not student text.)

When the United States entered World War I in 1917, it had been neutral for nearly three years while Europe tore itself apart. The war came at the end of the Progressive Era, when the federal government had already begun regulating railroads, food, and drugs — but mobilizing for total war would demand far more from Washington than progressivism ever had. [Contextualization +1: connects the prompt to neutrality, the Progressive Era, and the scale of mobilization — genuine broader context.] American participation in the war fostered significant political and social change: it greatly expanded the power of the federal government over speech and dissent, and it accelerated the women's suffrage movement, although some of the wartime changes faded after the war ended. [Thesis +1: a defensible claim about extent with a line of reasoning (expanded federal power, accelerated suffrage, partial reversal).]

The war's most dramatic political change was the federal government's new power to punish dissent. The 1917 amendment to the Espionage Act made it a crime to "willfully utter, print, write, or publish any disloyal, profane, scurrilous, or abusive language" about the government, the Constitution, or the military (Doc 1). For the first time, criticizing the government during wartime could land an American in federal prison. At the same time, the war gave suffragists their decisive argument: when President Wilson personally addressed the Senate in September 1918, he asked how the nation could "ask and take the utmost that our women can give — service and sacrifice of every kind — and still say we do not see what title that gives them to stand by our sides" in guiding the nation's affairs (Doc 5). Women's wartime service had earned them what petitions alone could not. [Evidence +1 (first point): the content of three documents — Docs 1, 5, and 7 below — is accurately described and used.]

But the war's changes did not all survive peacetime. Warren Harding, campaigning for president in May 1920, promised "not nostrums, but normalcy; not revolution, but restoration," and warned that "excess of government" could not substitute for good citizenship (Doc 7). His victory showed that voters wanted a retreat from wartime controls. The suffrage victory endured — the Nineteenth Amendment was ratified in 1920, giving women the vote nationwide — but much of the wartime expansion of federal power was deliberately dismantled. [Evidence beyond the documents +1: the Nineteenth Amendment's 1920 ratification is a specific fact outside the documents, used to support the argument.]

In the end, the war changed America substantially while it lasted: a stronger federal government, a successful suffrage movement, and a new sense of America's role in the world. The 1920s pulled back from the wartime peak, but the country that emerged was not the same one that had declared neutrality in 1914.
