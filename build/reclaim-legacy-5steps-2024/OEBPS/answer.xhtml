Day 1

As the website points out, in A.D. 1250 Cahokia was larger than London. The Mississippians developed a highly sophisticated urban culture that thrived centuries before the arrival of the Europeans. The mounds builders help illustrate the wide variety of cultures and levels of development among Native American peoples.

Day 2

The proper chronological order is Polo, Columbus, Balboa. Marco Polo was a Venetian merchant who traveled to China in the late 1200s. When Polo’s account of his voyage was published many years later in 1477, it spurred European desires to find a way to the rich markets of the Far East. Looking for Asia by sailing west across the Atlantic, Columbus instead discovered a New World in 1492. This opened up the Americas to European exploration and settlement. Balboa crossed the isthmus of Panama in 1513 and saw the Pacific Ocean.

Day 3

Portuguese mariners were exploring the Atlantic coastline of Africa in the 1480s. In 1488 Bartolomeu Dias discovered the Cape of Good Hope. Columbus first reached the western hemisphere in 1492. Hernando Cortès captured the Aztec capital of Tenochtitlan in 1521. This broke the power of the Aztec empire and secured his conquest of Mexico. Francisco Pizarro landed a small army in Peru in 1531; by 1533 he had completed the conquest of the Inca Empire. Francisco Vasquez de Coronado explored the American Southwest between 1540 and 1542. He unsuccessfully sought rumored cities of gold.

Day 4

Prescott’s The History of the Conquest of Mexico combines excellent scholarship with a nineteenth-century Romantic sensibility that makes it a dramatic and colorful narrative. Prescott admires Cortès and sees him as a great Romantic hero, bending events to his will. However, as a nineteenth-century man, Prescott deplores the conquistador’s cruelty and religious fanaticism. Ultimately Prescott sees Cortès as a brilliant product of a harsh time.

Day 5

The proper chronological order is Crusades, astrolabe, encomienda. The Crusades were religious wars waged by Christian Europeans in the Middle Ages. The Europeans hoped to stop Islamic territorial advances and to win back control of the Holy Land. The astrolabe was a navigational device that helped European mariners calculate their latitude. It helped make possible the fifteenth- and sixteenth-century voyages of discovery. The encomienda system emerged during the sixteenth-century Spanish conquests in Central and South America. Native Americans were forced to work for Spanish colonists in exchange for “protection.”

Day 6

Bartolomé de Las Casas was a Dominican friar who opposed the mistreatment of Native Americans and in person and in print defended their humanity and rights. While Las Casas had some influence on government policy, his attitudes had little impact on most Spanish settlers. Aside from converting Native Americans to Christianity, the settlers were intent on using conquered peoples as a source of labor.

Day 7

The Castillo de San Marcos is an imposing edifice that tells someone looking at it that the Spanish came to stay in America. The Castillo also tells the observer that the Spanish settlers lived in a dangerous world. The Castillo was attacked several times over the years. The Castillo is the oldest surviving fort in North America.

Day 8

The English settled Jamestown in 1607. This was the first successful English colony in America. Samuel de Champlain founded the French settlement of Quebec in 1608. The Virginia House of Burgesses, the first colonial legislature, began meeting in 1619. The Pilgrims landed at Plymouth in 1620. The Puritans established the Massachusetts Bay Colony in 1629. The first settlers arrived in Maryland in 1634. Maryland was intended as a refuge for English Catholics. William Penn founded Pennsylvania in 1681. Penn, a Quaker, established religious freedom for all Christians in Pennsylvania.

Day 9

All Europeans, whatever their nationality, regarded the Native Americans as heathen barbarians. The Spanish attempted to convert the Native Americans to Christianity, and then integrate them as workers in the colonial economic and social structure. Examples would be the encomiendas of early Spanish settlers and the missions established by the Franciscans and other religious orders in the Southwest and California. The French also wanted to convert the Native Americans to Catholicism. Jesuit missionaries went to live among the Native Americans. Because the French were mostly interested in fur trading, they did not require much land from the Native peoples they encountered, and so caused less disruption to their ways of life. The English were interested in converting the Native Americans to Protestant Christianity, but went at this less systematically than did the Spanish and French. Because the English needed land for their growing agricultural population, they engaged in many wars with local Native Americans, notably the brutal Pequot War of 1634–1638.

Day 10

The proper chronological order is Cartier, Hudson, and La Salle. Jacques Cartier made three voyages to Canada in the 1530s and 1540s, exploring the Gulf of St. Lawrence and the St. Lawrence River. His voyages helped lay the foundation for New France. Henry Hudson was an English sea captain working for the Dutch East India Company. In 1609, Hudson explored Manhattan and the river that would be named after him. In 1610–1611, he discovered what is now called Hudson Bay in Canada. Hudson’s first voyage opened the way for Dutch settlement in North America. Robert La Salle was a French explorer who sailed down the Mississippi River to the Gulf of Mexico in the 1680s. He established French outposts along the way and claimed the Mississippi River Valley for France, naming it Louisiana after King Louis XIV.

Day 11

The first Thanksgiving took place in November 1621, when the Pilgrims, with some Wampanoag Indian guests, celebrated a good harvest after the hardships that they had suffered in their first year in America. Thanksgiving did not become a national holiday until 1863, following a proclamation by President Abraham Lincoln. Now, in addition to being a time for Americans to reflect on their blessings, Thanksgiving is a time for family gatherings, parades, and football.

Day 12

Roger Williams was a Puritan minister and theologian who came to believe that church and state should be separated. His criticism of the Puritan establishment in Massachusetts led to the colony’s authorities ordering his banishment in 1635. In 1636, Williams and some followers established a new settlement at Providence in what became the colony of Rhode Island. Settlers here were assured freedom of conscience. Williams helped lay the foundation of the American tradition of religious liberty and the separation of church and state.

Day 13

The proper chronological order is John Smith, Squanto, Anne Hutchinson. John Smith was the military man whose leadership contributed to the survival of the English colony at Jamestown, Virginia. He claimed that after being captured by Native American members of the Powhatan Confederacy, his life was saved by Pocahontas, the daughter of their chief. Squanto was a member of the Patuxet tribe. He learned English after being captured by English explorers. Squanto was able to return home and helped the Pilgrims at Plymouth adjust to the new world, teaching them Native American methods of agriculture. Anne Hutchinson and her family settled in the Massachusetts Bay Colony in 1634. Here Hutchinson criticized the ministerial establishment of the colony and questioned their teachings on divine grace. Hutchinson was banished from the colony in 1638, and she moved to Rhode Island.

Day 14

Roman Catholicism was the Christian church of Europe before the Reformation. The clergy of the Church were organized hierarchically, with the Pope at their head. Roman Catholicism emphasized regular attendance at the Mass and other liturgies, sacraments, and the performance of good works as a means to salvation. King Henry VIII created the Church of England when he broke with the Catholic Church in the 1530s. The Church of England looked to the king, rather than the Pope, as its head. The Church of England maintained the episcopal structure of the Catholic Church and kept the Mass and other sacraments. Puritans were followers of John Calvin and held the doctrine of predestination. They wanted to “purify” the Church of England of its “Catholic” elements. The Church of England became the established church in Virginia and most of the southern colonies. Puritans settled in Massachusetts and initially dominated in the New England colonies. In the seventeenth century, Catholics were persecuted in England. The aristocratic Calvert family founded Maryland in the 1630s as a refuge for English Catholics.

Day 15

King William’s War began in 1688 and raged until 1697. In Europe it was known as the War of the League of Augsburg. Queen Anne’s War began in 1702 and ended in 1713. In Europe it was known as the War of the Spanish Succession. The religious revival of the Great Awakening began in the 1720s and lasted into the 1740s. The Stono Rebellion, a slave uprising in South Carolina, took place in 1739. King George’s War began in 1744 and ended in 1748. It was the American phase of the European War of the Austrian Succession, which ran from 1740–1748.

Day 16

Arthur Miller saw the Salem Witch Trials and the Second Red Scare as comparable because he believed both were hysterical reactions against imaginary threats. He opposed the investigations of the House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC) into the political beliefs of filmmakers, writers, and actors. Miller has his protagonist condemn himself for not speaking out earlier because he criticized HUAC witnesses who named names of former Communist associates to save their own careers. He, himself, was cited for contempt of Congress in 1957 for refusing to do this.

Day 17

The proper chronological order is the English Civil War, the Glorious Revolution, and the Acts of Union between England and Scotland. The English Civil War lasted from 1642 to 1651 and resulted in the execution of King Charles I and the establishment of a Puritan dictatorship under Oliver Cromwell. The Glorious Revolution took place in 1688, when the Catholic King James II was overthrown by his Protestant daughter Mary and her husband, the Dutch stadtholder William III. As the price of obtaining the kingdom, William and Mary agreed to the 1689 Bill of Rights, which limited the power of the monarchy and codified the rights of Parliament; this established a constitutional monarchy in England. The Acts of Union were passed by the English Parliament in 1706 and the Scottish Parliament in 1707, and unified the two countries into Great Britain, with one British Parliament.

Day 18

The Byrd family’s mansion of Westover was built around 1750. It is a magnificent example of Georgian architecture. Westover testifies to the wealth of some Virginia planters. It also testifies to the high level of culture of some planters; the Byrd library was once one of the largest in America.

Day 19

Mercantilism was an economic theory that encouraged governments to increase their share of limited global wealth by maintaining a favorable balance of trade. This was accomplished by regulations that encouraged exports while restricting imports from foreign countries with high tariffs. Colonies provided raw materials and staple products that were turned into finished goods by the mother country. Examples of legislation furthering mercantilism were the Navigation acts of the 1660s and the Molasses Act of 1733. Proponents of free trade believe that general prosperity is encouraged by the abolition of restrictions on exports and imports of goods. The great eighteenth-century British advocate of free trade was Adam Smith in his book The Wealth of Nations (1776).

Day 20

Bacon was telling his congregation of African American slaves that they should accept their station in life. They were to perform their duties willingly and without complaint. They were to do this because their station in life was the will of God and to object would be to defy God’s authority. This sermon demonstrates that in the eighteenth century some colonists in Maryland believed that the institution of slavery was sanctioned by God and reflected a divine order in the world. Bacon sees society divided into defined classes, much like European aristocrats. Interestingly, though the Reverend Bacon obviously saw African Americans as social inferiors, he did think it important that they embrace Christianity.

Day 21

The proper chronological order is Nathaniel Bacon, Sir Edmund Andros, and Jonathan Edwards. Nathaniel Bacon was a Virginia planter who led a rebellion against the Royal Governor William Berkeley in 1676. Bacon was protesting the inability of Berkeley to protect settlers on the frontier from Native American attacks. After initial success, Bacon died and Berkeley reasserted control over Virginia. Sir Edmund Andros was an English colonial administrator who in the seventeenth century served as governor of New York and other colonies. In 1686, King James II appointed Andros governor of the Dominion of New England, which combined New York, New Jersey, and the New England colonies. When word of the Glorious Revolution reached the colonies, Andros was overthrown and the Dominion dissolved. Jonathan Edwards was a minister who played a leading role in the Great Awakening. He delivered his famous sermon “Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God” in 1741.

Day 22

The Albany Congress was convened in 1754 to address western settlement and the issues this raised with Native Americans. The Stamp Act Congress was held in 1765 to protest the Stamp Act and lobby for its repeal. The Boston Massacre took place in 1770 as British sentries fired into a crowd of unruly American colonists. The Boston Tea Party took place in 1773 when colonists dressed as Native Americans dumped East India Company tea into Boston Harbor. The First Continental Congress gathered in 1774 to coordinate resistance to the Intolerable Acts passed by Parliament after the Boston Tea Party.

Day 23

In 1754, Governor Robert Dinwiddie of Virginia sent George Washington, a young militia officer, to evict the French from Fort Duquesne, which they had built on the site of present day Pittsburgh. Washington was overwhelmed and defeated. He served bravely with General Edward Braddock in 1755 and helped rally the survivors of that general’s defeat. Following this, Washington commanded Virginia troops on the frontier and became one of the most experienced American military commanders. The Marquis de Montcalm was the commander of French forces in Canada during the French and Indian War. He won important victories with the capture of Fort William Henry in 1757 and the successful defense of Fort Carillon (later Ticonderoga) in 1758. He was defeated and killed at the fall of Quebec in 1759. William Pitt became a leader of the British government in 1757. He devised an ambitious strategy to expand the British Empire. He sent large military reinforcements and capable commanders to North America, leading to the complete defeat of the French in Canada.

Day 24

In the eighteenth century, there were few roads in America. The easiest and fastest way to get around was by water. Lake Champlain was important because it provided an invasion route to and from Canada. Forts on the lake helped control this strategic waterway for the French, British, and later, the Americans. The French Fort Carillon was renamed Ticonderoga after it was captured by the British in 1759. It illustrates the enormously sophisticated nature of eighteenth-century fortifications, which were designed to bog attackers down in long and costly sieges.

Day 25

The proper chronological order is Sons of Liberty, Committees of Correspondence, minutemen. All these were groups of Americans resisting the policies of the British government. The Sons of Liberty were organized by Sam Adams in 1765 to resist the Stamp Act. The Sons of Liberty remained active after the Stamp Act crisis. Adams helped found a Massachusetts Committee of Correspondence in 1772 to express opposition to British actions and share ideas on methods of resistance. Soon other Committees of Correspondence emerged across the colonies. Minutemen were members of the militia charged with being ready for action at brief notice. In 1774, the Massachusetts Provincial Congress, which refused to recognize the military government imposed by the British, called on the militia to form companies of minutemen. The minutemen played an important role in the opening battles of the Revolutionary War at Lexington and Concord.

Day 26

Daniel Leonard was a wealthy and successful lawyer in Massachusetts. He opposed the agitation against Parliament and British policy, and skillfully criticized it in his “Letters,” which were published in a newspaper between December 1774 and April 1775. His writings remind us that many American colonists remained loyal to the British government. During the Revolutionary War perhaps as many as 20 percent of Americans were Loyalist in their sympathies.

Day 27

Traditionally in English history, kings had to go to parliament to raise taxes, establishing the precedent that taxpayers had to have a say on taxes that affected them. The 1689 English Bill of Rights codified the principle that all taxes had to be approved by Parliament. During the long period of Salutary Neglect, the American colonists became accustomed to paying taxes voted by their legislatures. Once the British government began directly taxing the colonists with the Stamp Act (1765), it seemed to the colonists that their rights as Englishmen were being denied. They were being taxed without their legislative consent. The British government responded with the argument that American received “virtual representation” in Parliament; this assumed that members of Parliament represented all British subjects, not just the people of their electoral constituency. American critics of British policy did not find this persuasive. Increasingly influenced by the writings of thinkers like John Locke, they believed that “taxation without representation” violated their natural rights. This difference in outlooks led to the American Revolution.

Day 28

From its inception in the 1930s until 1979, Colonial Williamsburg focused on the stories of white colonists. Then it began to hire African American actors to portray slaves. In 1999, an effort to depict the darker side of slavery, such as a slave auction, led to protests. It can still be difficult to find African Americans willing to dress up and act like eighteenth-century slaves. The dilemma faced by the reenactors at Colonial Williamsburg reflects the difficulty of re-creating a history that is intensely painful for many people.

Day 29

The Second Continental Congress convened in May 1775 and was faced with carrying on the war that had just begun with Great Britain. The Declaration of Independence was signed on July 2, 1776, and formally announced on July 4. The Battle of Saratoga resulted in the surrender of an entire British army on October 17, 1777. The victory at Saratoga convinced the French government that it would be safe to intervene on the side of the American rebels. France and the United States signed a formal treaty of alliance on February 6, 1778. The Battle of Yorktown resulted in the surrender of another British army on October 19, 1781. This battle ensured that the United States would become independent and was the last major battle of the Revolutionary War.

Day 30

The proper chronological order is the Articles of Confederation, the Treaty of Paris, the Northwest Ordinances. The Articles of Confederation was a constitution for the United States that the Second Continental Congress submitted to the thirteen states on 1777 and which was finally ratified in 1781. The weakness of the central government under the Articles of Confederation led to the convening of the Constitutional Convention in 1787. The Treaty of Paris ending the Revolutionary War and recognizing the independence of the United States was signed on September 3, 1783. It was ratified by Congress on January 14, 1784. The Northwest Ordinances refer to the Land Ordinance of 1784, the Land Ordinance of 1785, and the Northwest Ordinance of 1787. Collectively they set forth procedures for governance and land sales in the western territories. The Northwest Ordinance created the Northwest Territory and stated that once a territory reached a population of 60,000 it could apply for statehood. The Northwest Ordinance also prohibited slavery in the Northwest Territory, making the Ohio River a boundary between slave and free states.

Day 31

During the first years of the Revolutionary War, Benedict Arnold was one of the bravest and most effective combat generals in the Continental Army. He led a heroic march to Canada to Quebec in 1775 and was wounded leading an assault on that British-held city. The next year he prevented a British invasion of the United States from Canada by fighting a naval battle on Lake Champlain. He played a leading role in the victory at Saratoga in 1777 and once again was badly wounded. Despite his merit, a promotion for Arnold was held up for political reasons. He grew increasingly disillusioned with the American cause and more anxious to improve his financial situation. In 1780 he conspired with the British to surrender the vital strongpoint of West Point for a large sum of money. He hoped that this would result in the capture of the garrison and possibly George Washington. Arnold’s plot was discovered when his British contact was captured with incriminating documents in his boot. Arnold escaped and for the rest of the war served as a British officer. Had Arnold succeeded in his treasonous plot, the history of the Revolutionary War might have been very different. This is why he is the most infamous traitor in American history.

Day 32

Thomas Paine’s pamphlet Common Sense was published in January 1776 and made a powerful case for American independence from Great Britain. Common Sense was a publishing sensation. It had an immense circulation and to this day holds the record for being the best-selling title in American history. Paine’s arguments in his pamphlet helped prepare Americans for independence later in the year. Thomas Jefferson was a member of the Second Continental Congress. In June, 1776, he was appointed to a five-member committee charged with writing a declaration of independence. The committee selected Jefferson to compose the first draft. In his draft, Jefferson justified American independence with an appeal to the natural rights described by the English philosopher John Locke, and a long list of the ways that Great Britain had abused the colonies. After some revisions made by the committee and Congress, the Declaration of Independence was released to the world on July 4, 1776.

Day 33

Independence Hall played a pivotal role in American history. It was where Congress met for much of the Revolutionary War. It was the place where George Washington was appointed the commander of the Continental Army, and the Declaration of Independence was signed. In 1787, it was the meeting place where delegates gathered to frame and sign the Constitution of the United States.

Day 34

Washington in this letter is arguing that the United States needs a strong government for the new nation to become great. Without a strong government the United States will be treated contemptuously by the European powers and might even fall apart. This perspective is important because it reveals the concerns about the weakness of the Articles of Confederation that would eventually result in the calling of the Constitutional Convention. George Washington would chair the Constitutional Convention.

Day 35

In both 1775 at Lexington, and in 1786 during Shays’ Rebellion, Massachusetts farmers were resisting taxation that they believed was unfair. In 1775, the farmers were standing up to the British government and “no taxation without representation.” In 1786, the rebelling farmers were protesting the tax policies of their own state legislature, which was dominated by merchant interests. The farmers at Lexington helped spark the start of the Revolutionary War. Shays’ Rebellion convinced many Americans that the government under the Articles of Confederation had become too weak. This spurred the convening of the Constitutional Convention.

Day 36

The Virginia Plan, proposing a bicameral national legislature with all representatives allocated by proportional representation, was submitted to the Constitutional Convention in 1787. The French Revolution began in 1789, at the same time as the new American government under the Constitution was getting established. Secretary of the Treasury Alexander Hamilton proposed to stabilize the finances of the United States by having the federal government assume all state and Revolutionary War debts in his Report on the Public Credit in 1790. The Whiskey Rebellion began in 1794 as farmers in western Pennsylvania protested a new federal tax on distilled spirits. For many farmers far from urban markets, distilling grain was the only practical way to get their products to a market. The XYZ Affair occurred in 1798 when American diplomats refused to pay bribes to the emissaries of French Foreign Minister Talleyrand.

Day 37

The proper chronological order is The Federalist Papers, the Bill of Rights, the Kentucky and Virginia Resolves. The Federalist Papers were a series of articles published by James Madison, Alexander Hamilton, and John Jay intended to persuade the people of New York to support the proposed Constitution. Over time, these articles became seen as a profound commentary on the Constitution. The Bill of Rights comprises the first ten amendments to the Constitution and places limits on the federal government, guaranteeing basic rights of the American people. Supporters of the Constitution promised a bill of rights to persuade doubters to support the Constitution. James Madison proposed most of these amendments to Congress in 1789, and the ten amendments of the Bill of Rights were ratified by 1791. The Kentucky and Virginia Resolves were responses to the 1798 Alien and Sedition Acts, which gave the president the power to deport “dangerous” immigrants and shut down “seditious” newspapers. Thomas Jefferson wrote the Kentucky Resolution, and James Madison was the author of the Virginia Resolution. These resolutions argued that states could declare federal laws unconstitutional.

Day 38

Mercy Otis Warren was the sister of James Otis and the wife of James Warren, both prominent leaders in the resistance to British policies. During the Revolution she produced various writings supportive of the Patriot cause. Warren later wrote one of the first histories of the American Revolution. Warren was an anti-Federalist who opposed the ratification of the new Constitution. In the quotation she criticizes the system that was set up to ratify the Constitution through special state conventions, with the affirmative vote of nine of these making the Constitution the law of the land. Warren believed that this was an inappropriate end-run around the sitting Congress and state legislatures. Warren’s work is a reminder that many people opposed the ratification of the Constitution, fearing that the strengthened federal government would become tyrannical.

Day 39

Sources like Martha Ballard’s diary, if studied carefully, can give historians valuable insights into the lives of ordinary men and women. Martha Ballard’s record of her daily activities is especially useful for shedding light on the lives of women on the American frontier in the late eighteenth century, providing important information on people whose lives were generally ignored by contemporary historians. The more we appreciate the way ordinary people lived in the past, the better we can understand the social, cultural, and intellectual context of more prominent figures.

Day 40

The proper chronological order is the Declaration of Neutrality, Jay’s Treaty, the Quasi-War with France. George Washington issued the Declaration of Neutrality in 1793. He hoped that the United States could avoid involvement in the war being waged between the Republic of France and Great Britain and its allies. With relations deteriorating between the United States and Great Britain, President Washington sent Chief Justice John Jay to London to negotiate with the British government. The resulting Jay’s Treaty was signed in 1794 and ratified in 1795. The British made few concessions to the United States, but tensions were eased between the two countries. Thomas Jefferson and his followers opposed the treaty. The Quasi-War broke out between the United States and France following the XYZ Affair. It was fought at sea. The newly revived U.S. Navy distinguished itself, winning a number of ship-to-ship battles. The war ended with the Convention of 1800, signed September 30, 1800. The Convention of 1800 also officially ended the Revolutionary War alliance between the United States and France.

Day 41

Thomas Jefferson took a strict constructionist view of the Constitution. He believed that the federal government could not take any action that was not explicitly spelled out in the Constitution. Hence he opposed Alexander Hamilton’s plan for a Bank of the United States, because there is nothing in the Constitution about a national bank. Alexander Hamilton took a loose constructionist view of the Constitution. He believed that a broader interpretation of the Constitution allowed the federal government to take actions not literally spelled out in the document. He held that the “necessary and proper” clause allowed the government to take steps necessary to carry out its enumerated tasks. Hence Hamilton believed that the federal government could create a Bank of the United States to help transact federal financial business. The constitutional differences between Jefferson and Hamilton helped create the first party system. Thomas Jefferson became the leader of the Democratic-Republicans. Hamilton was the guiding force behind the Federalists.

Day 42

Following the Revolutionary War, the British did not evacuate all their forts from what had become the northwestern territories of the United States. The British provided support to the Native Americans who were resisting American efforts to assert control over this region. In 1790 and 1791, two American armies were defeated by the Native Americans as they attempted to march into the Northwest Territory. President Washington appointed General Anthony Wayne commander of the Legion of the United States in 1792. After training his men for nearly two years, Wayne began moving into the territory controlled by the Native Americans, building forts along the way to protect his line of communication and supply. On August 20, 1794, Wayne decisively defeated the Native Americans at the Battle of Fallen Timbers, near present-day Toledo, Ohio. The retreating Native Americans hoped to find shelter in the nearby British Fort Miamis, but the fort’s commander dared not let them in. On August 3, 1795, Wayne and Native American leaders signed the Treaty of Greenville, which opened up much of Ohio to American settlement. The United States had asserted its authority in its western territory.

Day 43

The Louisiana Purchase occurred in 1803. President Thomas Jefferson purchased the Louisiana Territory from France for $15 million, doubling the size of the United States. The Twelfth Amendment was ratified in 1804. It was designed to avoid the electoral tie between Thomas Jefferson and his running mate Aaron Burr in 1800, and stipulated that voters in the Electoral College vote separately for the president and vice president. The Battle of Tippecanoe took place in 1811, when William Henry Harrison, governor of the Indiana Territory, led a force against the Native American village of Prophetstown established by Tecumseh and his brother the Prophet. The Native Americans were defeated and the village was destroyed. The Treaty of Ghent, which ended the War of 1812, was signed on December 24, 1814. The Second Bank of the United States was chartered in 1816. It was intended to further economic growth after the War of 1812.

Day 44

The proper chronological order is Embargo of 1807, Non-Intercourse Act, Hartford Convention. The Embargo Act was a response to years of British impressment of American sailors, interference with American trade, and the 1807 attack on USS Chesapeake by HMS Leopard. President Thomas Jefferson hoped that an embargo on American trade would persuade the warring European powers to respect American rights. The Embargo Act hurt American merchants and shippers and became very unpopular in New England and in coastal areas. The 1810 Non-Intercourse Act was an attempt to alleviate the situation by allowing trade with all countries but Great Britain and France. These policies failed to prevent war with Great Britain in 1812. The War of 1812 was very unpopular in Federalist-dominated New England. In 1814, a group of Federalists convened the Hartford Convention, where they discussed their grievances and brought up the nullification of federal laws and even, briefly, secession. The subsequent end of the war discredited this gathering.

Day 45

In the Marbury v. Madison decision, Marshall made the case that since the Constitution is the supreme law of the land, the courts could void laws that conflict with the Constitution. This established the principle of judicial review, giving the courts the power to review the constitutionality of laws. This ruling enhanced the prestige and power of the judiciary.

Day 46

Tecumseh was a Shawnee war leader and diplomat who in the early nineteenth century attempted to create a tribal confederacy to oppose further American settlement on Native American lands. He made it a principle that the land belonged to all Native Americans, and so no individual or group of individuals could sell parts of it to the U.S. government. Andrew Jackson was a supporter of American westward expansion. During the War of 1812, he inflicted severe defeats on the portions of the Creek tribe that supported the British. Later, as president, he pushed for the removal of eastern tribes to lands west of the Mississippi River. The defeat and death of Tecumseh in the War of 1812 and the ascendency of American leaders like Andrew Jackson, meant the end of Native American power in the eastern United States.

Day 47

Thomas Jefferson started building a house on the site of Monticello in 1768. He began extensive remodeling in 1794 that lasted until 1809. This did not stop him from making improvements on the house until his death. For Jefferson, Monticello was always a work in progress. The house illustrates Jefferson’s gifts as an architect and a designer. It reflects the gracious life of a well-to-do Virginia planter. The historical material on the website reminds viewers of the labors of the slaves who sustained this estate.

Day 48

The proper chronological order is Lewis and Clark Expedition, Battle of New Orleans, Era of Good Feelings. President Thomas Jefferson sent Captain Meriwether Lewis and Second Lieutenant William Clark on an expedition to explore the Louisiana Territory. Their journey lasted from May 1804 to September 1806. Lewis and Clark reached the Pacific Ocean, claimed the territory for the United States, and brought back a wealth of information about the geography and resources of the west. On January 8, 1815, General Andrew Jackson severely defeated a British army attempting to take control of the strategic city of New Orleans. The Battle of New Orleans foiled the last major British offensive of the War of 1812. This spectacular victory spurred American patriotism and confidence in the aftermath of the war. The Era of Good Feelings was the period during the presidency of James Monroe when postwar nationalism was the predominant sentiment in the United States. The first party system disappeared with the collapse of the Federalist Party, leaving the Democratic-Republicans in largely unchallenged control of the nation.

Day 49

Adams is making the point that most Americans worked very hard in the early nineteenth century. They made more money than people in Europe, but had to deal with harsher living conditions. This ties into the traditional view that if you work hard in America, you can make your fortune. One of the most famous manifestations of this were the books for children written by Horatio Alger in the late nineteenth century. In these books young boys avoided temptations, worked hard, and made their way up in the world.

Day 50

The Missouri Compromise was designed to keep a balance between free and slave states. In 1820, Missouri was admitted as a slave state, and Maine as a free state. The compromise, engineered by Henry Clay, drew a line through the Louisiana Territory; areas north of the line would become free states and those south of the line would become slave states. The Monroe Doctrine was issued in 1823, and warned European powers against imposing colonial rule on territories in the western hemisphere. The Erie Canal was completed in 1825. It provided a waterway between New York City and the Great Lakes, enabling western farmers to get their crops to market. The Removal Act of 1830 gave the president the power to purchase the lands of eastern Native American tribes and move them across the Mississippi River. The Nullification Crisis occurred in 1832 when South Carolina nullified federal tariffs. President Jackson threatened to use force against the state. Henry Clay defused the crisis by persuading Congress to lower the tariff rates.

Day 51

The proper chronological order is Bank War, Specie Circular, Panic of 1837. Henry Clay saw the Second Bank of the United States as a centerpiece of his American System. In 1832, as he prepared to run for the presidency, he attempted to have its charter renewed early by Congress. President Andrew Jackson opposed the bank as a tool of special interests. He vetoed the rechartering bill. The next year, in an attempt to kill the bank, Jackson withdrew all federal money from it and placed these funds in state and local banks, termed “Pet Banks” by his opponents. The Specie Circular was an executive order issued by President Jackson in 1836 requiring that federal land be paid for with gold or silver coins. This was an attempt to cut back on land speculation. The Panic of 1837 was a severe economic downturn in the United States that lasted into the 1840s. Although international financial dislocations contributed to the crash, Americans blamed the panic on credit problems caused by the Bank War and the Specie Circular.

Day 52

The Trail of Tears was the culmination of efforts by settlers to acquire the lands lived on by Native Americans in the Eastern United States. The Cherokee tribe fought back in court. In the 1831 Supreme Court decision Cherokee Nation v. Georgia, Chief Justice John Marshall ruled that the tribe had rights to the land that the state of Georgia was attempting to force them from. This Court decision had no effect because President Andrew Jackson was unwilling to enforce it. The Removal Act of 1830 gave the federal government the authority to negotiate the removal of tribes to lands west of the Mississippi River. Tribes began moving in 1831. The removal of the Cherokee took place in 1838. One-third of the tribe died of disease as they trekked west. The path of their journey became known as the Trail of Tears.

Day 53

Following in the tradition of Alexander Hamilton, Henry Clay believed that the government could play a role in promoting economic growth. His “American System” called for a high protective tariff to encourage the growth of American industry, a Bank of the United States to provide credit and facilitate business transactions, and government support for internal improvements like roads and canals to help businessmen and farmers get their goods to markets. Andrew Jackson followed the tradition of Thomas Jefferson. He was a believer in limited government, and opposed direct government assistance to business. He killed the Bank of the United States because he thought it a corrupt bastion for the privileged. He discouraged federal funding for internal improvement projects, fearing that they would become expensive supports for special interests. Clay’s ideas were adopted by the Whig Party, while Jackson’s economic perspective dominated the Democratic Party.

Day 54

In the election of 1840, the Whig Party was determined to win the presidency. Looking for their own Andrew Jackson, they nominated William Henry Harrison, another aging hero of the War of 1812. They came up with a brilliant campaign slogan that reminded voters of Harrison’s 1811 victory over the Indians, as well as his vice presidential running mate, John Tyler—“Tippecanoe and Tyler Too!” Instead of stressing a political program, the Whigs focused on selling the American people an image of the aristocratic Harrison as a man of the people. He was portrayed as a candidate willing to sit in front of a log cabin sipping hard cider. Copious quantities of hard cider were distributed at Harrison rallies. Groups of young Whigs rolled large balls across the countryside, supposedly until Harrison was elected. This helped popularize the expression “Keep the Ball Rolling.” The success of the Whig campaign in 1840 illustrates the power of image and marketing in American elections.

Day 55

Charles Grandison Finney was one of the greatest preachers of the Second Great Awakening. The Second Great Awakening was a Protestant religious revival movement that began in the 1790s and persisted through the 1830s. This revival emphasized a more personal and emotional religious experience. The intensification of religious feeling across the United States spurred reform movements such as those for temperance and the abolition of slavery.

Day 56

The proper chronological order is American Colonization Society, Nat Turner’s Rebellion, Gag Rule. The American Colonization Society was an antislavery society founded in 1817. Members of the society believed that blacks and whites could not live together peacefully in the United States and worked to send freed slaves to new homes in Africa. In 1831, Nat Turner led a bloody slave revolt in Virginia. Around 60 white men, women, and children were killed. The revolt was brutally suppressed. At least 100 slaves were killed as the authorities hunted down the rebels. Turner and 55 other slaves who had participated in the insurrection were executed. Beginning in 1831, abolitionists sent petitions to the House of Representatives opposing slavery. In 1836, Southern representatives took the lead in imposing a Gag rule that automatically tabled all antislavery petitions. The Gag rule lasted until it was overturned in 1844.

Day 57

The Battle of the Alamo took place in March 1836 as part of the Texan War of Independence. After a period of independence as the Lone Star Republic, Texas was admitted to the Union in 1845. The Mexican War began in 1846 as Mexican forces challenged the American claim for the southern border of Texas. California was part of the territory won from Mexico during the war. In 1848, gold was discovered in California, leading to a rush of prospecting and settlement. The desire of California to enter the Union as a free state led to the Compromise of 1850. The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 was part of the compromise and a Northern concession to the South.

Day 58

Manifest Destiny was a term coined by the Democratic journalist John O’Sullivan in 1845. It referred to the belief that it was the God-given mission of the United States to expand across the continent to the Pacific Ocean. Manifest Destiny appealed to both Northerners and Southerners. In the election of 1844, the Democratic presidential candidate James K. Polk called for the United States to acquire both slaveholding Texas and all of the Oregon Territory. A Democratic Congressman named David Wilmot proposed what came to be known as the Wilmot Proviso in 1846. He called for a prohibition of slavery in any territory conquered from Mexico during the Mexican War. He expressed the view of many Northerners that the United States should expand its territory, but that the expansion of slavery should be halted. The Wilmot Proviso began a debate over the nature of American expansionism and the issue of slavery in the western territories that would last into the 1850s.

Day 59

The Seneca Falls Women’s Rights Convention took place on July 19–20, 1848. It was attended by such well-known activists as Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott. The African American abolitionist Frederick Douglass was also one of the attendees. The convention issued a Declaration of Sentiments that called for the right of women to vote. The convention helped spur further discussion of women’s rights.

Day 60

Dred Scott was a Missouri slave who sued for his freedom because his master had taken him for a time to live in free parts of the United States. Chief Justice Roger Taney and the Supreme Court ruled that African Americans, whatever their legal status, could not be citizens. The Court also ruled that Congress could not prohibit slavery in the federal territories. This decision deepened sectional differences over the issue of slavery. Abolitionists were infuriated by the decision. Many Northerners were outraged at the prospect of slavery being allowed to expand throughout the western territories.

Day 61

The proper chronological order is Kansas-Nebraska Act, “Bleeding Kansas,” the Harpers Ferry Raid of John Brown. The 1854 Kansas-Nebraska Act was sponsored by Senator Stephen A. Douglas of Illinois as a way to rapidly organize these western territories and promote a transcontinental railroad based in Chicago. To win Southern support for his scheme, Douglas’s bill provided that the settlers of these territories could vote on whether they wanted slavery or not. This provision angered many Northerners because these territories were above the Missouri Compromise line, and slavery should have been prohibited there. There was never any doubt that Nebraska would be free of slavery. Kansas was a different story. Proslavery and antislavery settlers flooded into Kansas and engaged in a sometimes violent conflict over a constitution for the territory, giving the territory the name “Bleeding Kansas.” John Brown was a fanatic antislavery activist who engaged in this violence and eventually had to flee after murdering five proslavery settlers. On October 16, 1859, John Brown and a band of 18 followers attacked the federal arsenal at Harpers Ferry, Virginia. He hoped to start a slave insurrection. He and his men were soon overpowered by federal troops. Brown was hanged for treason.

Day 62

During the Texas Revolution, rebels occupied the Alamo mission in San Antonio. From February 23 to March 6, 1836, the Texas garrison of around 200 men was besieged by an army led by General Antonio López de Santa Anna that eventually numbered over 3,000. Among the defenders were such well-known frontiersmen as Jim Bowie and David Crockett. Santa Anna launched his final assault early on the morning of March 6. All the Texans were killed, and the Mexicans suffered around 600 casualties. The heroic defense of the Alamo inspired Texan resistance to the Mexicans. When Sam Houston’s Texan army defeated and captured Santa Anna at the Battle of San Jacinto on April 21, some of the Texans reportedly cried, “Remember the Alamo!” as they hunted fleeing Mexican soldiers. The Alamo has become a symbol of courage against great odds as well as Texan pride. The Alamo has also become a source of controversy as historians argue over the number of casualties and the fate of individual members of the garrison. Some Mexican accounts of the battle differ in important respects from the popular perception of the battle. While the broad outline of the Alamo battle is clear, its story demonstrates how difficult it is sometimes to reconstruct all the details of an event when many participants are dead and political passions color memories.

Day 63

Stephen Douglas ran for reelection to the U.S. Senate as a Democrat in 1858. His Republican opponent was Abraham Lincoln. During the political campaign, Douglas and Lincoln debated at seven locations in Illinois. Douglas defended his belief in popular sovereignty, holding that the citizens of a territory should be able to decide whether they wanted slavery or not. In the wake of the Supreme Court’s 1857 Dred Scott v. Sandford decision, he proposed his Freeport Doctrine, arguing that even if it was technically illegal to exclude slavery from a territory, the residents of that territory could keep slavery out by legislating onerous regulations that would discourage slave owners. Abraham Lincoln opposed the extension of slavery into the territories under any circumstances. Before the Seventeenth Amendment was adopted in 1913, voters were not able to directly vote for and therefore elect a U.S. Senator. Instead, it was the state legislature that elected the senators from their state. Therefore, Douglas won the Senate campaign because the Democrats kept control of the Illinois legislature. Douglas’s Freeport Doctrine angered Southern Democrats, split the party, and crippled his presidential candidacy in 1860. Though he lost the Senate race, Lincoln attained national prominence and won the Republican nomination and the presidency in 1860.

Day 64

South Carolina responded to the election of Abraham Lincoln as president by seceding from the Union in December 1860. The 1861 Crittenden Plan was Senator John Crittenden of Kentucky’s effort to save the Union through a compromise. He called for the federal government to guarantee the future of slavery where it already existed and for a return to the Missouri Compromise line in the west, with it being extended all the way to the Pacific Ocean. Abraham Lincoln issued the Preliminary Emancipation on September 22, 1862, following the Union victory at Antietam. It warned the Confederate States that if they did not return to the Union, he would issue an Emancipation Proclamation freeing the slaves in rebellious states on January 1, 1863. The New York City Draft Riots took place in July 1863 as Irish immigrants and other working men violently protested the imposition a federal military draft. Sherman’s March to the Sea took place from November 15 to December 21, 1864, as General William Tecumseh Sherman marched an army across Georgia from Atlanta to Savannah, living off the land and demonstrating the weakness of the Confederate government.

Day 65

The proper chronological order is Fort Sumter, Battle of Gettysburg, Appomattox. Fort Sumter was a federal fort in Charleston Harbor. The Confederate decision to bombard the fort into surrender started the fighting in the Civil War. The Battle of Gettysburg took place on July 1–3, 1863. The Federal Army of the Potomac under General George Meade defeated General Robert E. Lee’s Confederate Army of Northern Virginia, ending Lee’s invasion of Pennsylvania. This was the bloodiest battle of the Civil War; never again would Lee be able to launch an offensive into the North. Appomattox, Virginia, was the place where Federal forces under General Ulysses S. Grant cornered Lee’s retreating army and forced Lee to surrender on April 9, 1865. Within two months, all other major Confederate forces surrendered.

Day 66

Civil War reenacting is very popular because it allows people to re-create aspects of one of the most dramatic and important events in American history. Civil War reenactors take pride in their historical heritage, and honor the fighters on both the Northern and Southern sides. While men can dress as soldiers, many women take part in Civil war reenactments, wearing 1860s costumes. Reenactors often put on historical demonstrations at Civil War battlefields and other locations. Many have acted as extras in movies. Some question whether it is appropriate to dress as Confederate soldiers and display the Confederate battle flag in a historical context, because the Confederate army was defending slave owners.

Day 67

Congressman Clement Vallandigham was a leading Northern Democratic opponent of the Civil War. Northern Democrats who opposed the war were known as Copperheads. President Abraham Lincoln took vigorous action against these Southern sympathizers. Over 14,000 were jailed without trial. President Lincoln suspended the writ of habeas corpus to keep some of these individuals in prison indefinitely. In 1863, Vallandigham was arrested and tried by a military court for treasonable utterances. He was convicted and banished to the Confederacy.

Day 68

The proper chronological order is Battle of Shiloh, Battle of Vicksburg, Fall of Atlanta. The Battle of Shiloh was a major battle that took place in the western theater of the war on April 6–7, 1862. General Ulysses S. Grant repulsed a Confederate attack in a hard-fought battle that badly mauled his army. General Grant began a long offensive against the Confederate strongpoint of Vicksburg, Mississippi, the last important Confederate outpost on the Mississippi River. After months of maneuvering and fighting, Grant put the city under siege on May 18, 1863. When Vicksburg surrendered on July 4, Federal forces controlled the entire span of the Mississippi River, splitting the Confederacy. In May 1864, General William Tecumseh Sherman launched a campaign to capture the important Confederate manufacturing and railroad center of Atlanta, Georgia. After months of hard fighting, the city surrendered on September 2. The fall of Atlanta encouraged Northern voters, persuading them that an end to the war was in sight, and ensured the reelection of Abraham Lincoln.

Day 69

General George McClellan wanted to fight a limited war. He wanted to restore the Union, but did not want to interfere with Southern institutions like slavery. As a general, he was very cautious and slow, and hoped to outmaneuver his enemies rather than engaging in bloody, head-on battles. General Ulysses S. Grant gradually came to realize that the war would be long and hard, and that the Confederates would have to be worn down before they admitted defeat. Although capable of brilliant maneuvers, Grant knew that the Confederate armies would have to be destroyed by hard fighting. In 1864–65, Grant never let his forces lose contact with Lee’s army in Virginia. The casualties were enormous, but he knew that he could afford losses better than Lee and that he was bringing the end of the war closer. Grant also allowed subordinates like William Tecumseh Sherman and Philip Sheridan to ravage enemy territory, waging psychological and economic war against Southern civilians that was a precursor of the total war of the twentieth century.

Day 70

President Abraham Lincoln was assassinated by the actor John Wilkes Booth on April 14, 1865, while attending the play Our American Cousin at Ford’s Theatre. Booth was a Southern sympathizer who headed a conspiracy to kill the leading members of the federal government. The other conspirators failed in their efforts to murder Vice President Andrew Johnson and Secretary of State William H. Seward. Being killed at the moment of victory made Lincoln a martyr in the eyes of Northerners. There was a national outpouring of grief as Lincoln’s body was taken to Springfield, Illinois, for burial. Many Americans came to believe that Lincoln would have done a much better job than his successor in guiding the United States through Reconstruction.

Day 71

Walt Whitman was shocked by the conditions of the hospital treating men wounded at the Battle of Fredericksburg, which took place December 11–15, 1862. The place that he visited was probably a triage point, treating the most badly wounded. Whitman realized that the people running the hospital were doing the best that they could. His experience near Fredericksburg inspired Whitman to travel to Washington, D.C., and volunteer as a nurse in Army hospitals. Whitman’s description is a vivid reminder of the hardships faced by wounded soldiers in a period before antibiotics. Whitman noticed the results of many amputations. For Civil War doctors, amputation was often the best way to save the life of a man with a shattered arm or leg likely to develop a deadly infection.

Day 72

President Abraham Lincoln proposed his Ten Percent Plan for reconstructing the Union in 1863. When ten percent of registered voters in a former Confederate state swore allegiance to the federal government, they could form a new state government. The Wade-Davis Bill was a Radical Republican measure passed by Congress in 1864. This bill made the reorganization of state governments in former Confederate states conditional on a majority of voters swearing that they had never been disloyal. Practically, this meant that large numbers of freed slaves would have to be added to voter rolls. The Thirteenth Amendment abolishing slavery everywhere in the United States was ratified in 1865. The Tenure of Office Act was passed by the Radical Republican–dominated Congress in 1867. It prohibited the president from summarily firing cabinet officers who had been confirmed by Congress. This measure was intended to protect Radical Republican–leaning cabinet officers like Secretary of War Edwin Stanton from being fired by President Andrew Johnson. President Johnson defied Congress by firing Stanton anyway. The House of Representatives voted articles of impeachment in 1868. Johnson survived his impeachment trial in the Senate by just one vote.

Day 73

The proper chronological order is Reconstruction Act, Fifteenth Amendment, Compromise of 1877. The Reconstruction Act was passed by the Radical Republican–dominated Congress in 1867. It divided the former Confederacy into five military districts, each governed by an army general. If former Confederate states were to form new governments, they had to give freedmen equal rights and allow them to vote. The Fifteenth Amendment furthered Radical Republican Reconstruction by ensuring that no one could be denied the right to vote “on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude.” The Compromise of 1877 was a deal worked out between Republicans and Democrats after the contested presidential election of 1876. The Republican candidate, Rutherford B. Hayes would be sworn in as president, but the remaining federal troops in the South would be withdrawn, leading to the collapse of the last Republican governments in the region. This marked the end of Reconstruction.

Day 74

D. W. Griffith based the latter part of The Birth of a Nation on The Clansman (1905), a racist novel by Thomas F. Dixon, Jr., which celebrated the Ku Klux Klan. The son of a former Confederate officer, Griffith was not sensitive to the appalling racism of his source material. Striving for historical accuracy, he had one of his film’s intertitles quote a history of the United States written by President Woodrow Wilson when he was still a university professor. Wilson, like most historians of the time, regarded Radical Reconstruction as a mistaken failure. In addition to its sympathy for the Klan, Griffith’s film reflected the racism of the Jim Crow era in other ways. Some of the African American characters are played by white actors in blackface. Though The Birth of a Nation was a huge box-office success, it roused a storm of criticism. Defensive about these attacks, Griffith released another epic film in 1916. Titled Intolerance, it ambitiously looked at religious and economic intolerance at four different periods of history. Ironically, for all his sympathy for the old South, Griffith regarded Abraham Lincoln as a hero and portrayed him as such in the first half of The Birth of a Nation. He later made a film about Lincoln’s life.

Day 75

President Andrew Johnson attempted to follow what he thought was Abraham Lincoln’s approach to Reconstruction. He wanted to allow the Southern states to be reorganized on lenient terms. Aside from ex-Confederate political and military leaders, and the largest plantation owners, he was willing to offer pardons to former rebels willing to swear their allegiance to the federal government. The Radical Republicans believed that Johnson’s easy terms would allow former Confederates to return to power. They were appalled by the Black Codes that Southern states were creating to control the freedmen. The Radical Republicans wanted to empower the former slaves. They established the Freedman’s Bureau to help them adjust to their new status. They believed that giving the freedmen the vote would enable them to protect themselves from the former Confederates, and would help create new Republican political majorities in the Southern states. The struggle between Johnson and the Radical Republicans led to the impeachment crisis in 1868. Although Radical Reconstruction was eventually dismantled, it provided a precedent for government action to promote the welfare of disadvantaged groups.

Day 76

Clara Barton’s congressional testimony reveals that there was a great deal of confusion among freedmen shortly after the Civil War. They were not getting good information from their former masters and were subject to conflicting rumors, such as the possibility of being re-enslaved because of the death of Abraham Lincoln. When Clara Barton arrived on a tour of Georgia, she was seen as a source of truthful information by the local freedmen. What she saw clearly revealed that the former slaves valued their freedom and worried about losing it. Barton’s report helped justify support for federal initiatives such as the Freedman’s Bureau, which provided assistance to the former slaves.

Day 77

The Tuskegee Institute was founded by Booker T. Washington in 1881. Washington was a former slave who believed that the best way forward for African Americans was through economic self-reliance. His school taught students practical skills in farming and the trades. Students built many of the early buildings at Tuskegee. The school also prepared teachers to take knowledge back to their fellow African Americans. Among the teachers at Tuskegee was George Washington Carver, who became famous for his work with peanuts and other agricultural alternatives to cotton. Tuskegee was one of a number of educational institutions created in the nineteenth century to serve African Americans.

Day 78

A carpetbagger was a Northerner who moved to the South after the Civil War. Many were looking for economic opportunity. Others took part in the politics of Reconstruction, playing an important role in the Republican party in the Southern states. Scalawags were Southerners who cooperated with the Republican Reconstruction governments. Both carpetbagger and scalawag were pejorative terms for most Southerners, and for many years after Reconstruction these Northern and Southern collaborators with the Reconstruction governments in the South were looked upon as questionable participants in a doomed social experiment. Since the time of the Civil Rights Movement of the 1950s and 1960s, the historical assessment of these people has changed, and they are now seen as important allies for African Americans in the struggle for equal rights for all during Reconstruction.

Day 79

The federal government encouraged Western settlement. The 1862 Homestead Act granted 160 acres of land to a settler willing to farm it for five years. The Battle of the Little Bighorn took place on June 25–26, 1876. A large force of Sioux and Northern Cheyenne defeated a detachment of U.S. cavalry commanded by Lieutenant Colonel George Armstrong Custer. Custer and 267 of his men were killed. This was the last great victory for Native Americans in the Plains Wars. The 1878 Timber and Stone Act made land deemed unsuitable for farming inexpensively available to businessmen interested in logging and mining. The 1887 Dawes Act was sponsored by reformers hoping to help Native Americans adjust to “civilized” life. Reservations were divided into plots of land for individual Native Americans so they would have land to farm. In the end, a lot of this land ended up being sold to speculators. The Ghost Dance was a religious movement that became popular among some Plains tribes in 1890. Ghost Dancers believed that their dancing would lead to the departure of the white man, the return of the buffalo, and a new era of peace and plenty. The attempt of a band of Ghost Dancers to leave the Sioux reservation led to the carnage at Wounded Knee.

Day 80

The proper chronological order is completion of the transcontinental railroad, Interstate Commerce Act, Sherman Antitrust Act. The Central Pacific and Union Pacific Railroads met at Promontory Summit, Utah, on May 10, 1869. The railroads became a major driver of Western settlement and were vital to the economic interests of Western farmers. Concerns that the railroads were abusing their power led to the 1887 Interstate Commerce Act, which gave the federal government some authority to regulate railroad rates. Fears that large businesses and monopolies were getting too powerful led to the 1890 Sherman Antitrust Act, which made business combinations “in restraint of trade” illegal.

Day 81

Mary E. Lease was a fiery spokeswoman for the Farmers’ Alliances and later the Populist Party. She expressed the concerns of Western farmers who were being squeezed by falling prices, and often found themselves unable to pay their mortgages. These farmers blamed their predicament on business monopolies and the gold standard, which contributed to a tighter money supply. Lease is also important as a feminist who spoke up for the right of women to vote and defend their interests in public debate.

Day 82

William Jennings Bryan was a former congressman from Nebraska who captured the 1896 Democratic presidential nomination by giving a speech at the convention in which he declared, “You shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold!” Bryan was a spokesman for the Western farmers who believed that their economic situation was being ruined by tariffs that made prices for goods higher, and the gold standard with its tight money policy supported by lawmakers in Washington. They demanded a lower tariff and the free coinage of silver to inflate the currency, raise agricultural prices, and reduce the value of mortgages. William McKinley was a former congressman and governor of Ohio. He defended the interests of industry and industrial workers. He supported a high tariff to protect American industries and manufacturing jobs. He supported the gold standard to avoid inflation that would hurt businesses and undermine the purchasing power of workers. In the election of 1896, McKinley’s base of support in the cities and the manufacturing centers of the East and Midwest proved decisive, and he won the election. The election of 1896 signaled a shift in power from rural to urban America.

Day 83

The proper chronological order is the first “long drive” of cattle, the invention of barbed wire, and the opening of “Buffalo Bill’s Wild West Show.” The first “long drive” of cattle took place in 1866, when Texas ranchers banded together and drove their herds to the railhead at Sedalia, Missouri. From there the cattle could be shipped to Chicago and Eastern markets. This began the great era of Western cattle drives that lasted for around twenty years. Joseph Glidden invented barbed wire in 1873. The ready availability of barbed wire allowed Western farmers to enclose their lands, marking the beginning of the end of the open range phase of cattle herding. William “Buffalo Bill” Cody was a former buffalo hunter and Army scout. He became famous when the writer Ned Buntline loosely based a dime novel on his exploits. Developing skills as a showman, Cody began his “Buffalo Bill’s Wild West Show” in 1883. Featuring real cowboys and Indians, as well as stars such as the sharpshooter Annie Oakley, Cody’s show was immensely popular for decades, and created an image of the “Wild West” that would endure in Western movies, television shows, and novels for a century and more.

Day 84

The Homestead National Monument was created in 1936 on the site of the farm of Daniel Freeman at Beatrice, Nebraska. Freeman was one of the first people to take advantage of the 1862 Homestead Act, submitting his claim a few minutes after midnight on January 1, 1863. The Homestead Act played an important part in the settlement of the West as many thousands of people took advantage of its provisions to obtain land. The Homestead Act helped make it possible for people of ordinary means to settle in the West. By 1900, 610,000 lots of land had been transferred to homesteaders. These farmers encountered many challenges on the Great Plains. Some became prosperous, while others failed. Writers such as Willa Cather and O. E. Rolvaag described the range of experiences faced by these settlers.

Day 85

For all of American history up through the late nineteenth century, there had been a frontier offering Americans a chance at a new life. Following the 1890 census, the Census Bureau announced that there no longer was a continuous American frontier. This occurred just as the United States was becoming a great industrial power, with more and more Americans living in cities. The rapid urbanization of the United States was worrisome to many Americans. Turner’s thesis aggravated these worries. People asked how American democracy could continue if the frontier that had nurtured it was disappearing. Would the American character remain the same in an urbanized and industrialized nation? The response of some Americans like Theodore Roosevelt was to conserve what was left of the American wilderness and to open up a new frontier through imperialism in the Philippines and elsewhere. The idea of the frontier remained compelling to Americans in the twentieth century. President John F. Kennedy labeled his program of domestic and foreign policies the “New Frontier.” At the same time, many people saw the exploration of space as opening a new frontier.

Day 86

The Knights of Labor was founded in 1869. The Knights of Labor was a union that was open to all workers. The 1882 Chinese Exclusion Act prohibited the immigration of all Chinese laborers. This act was a response to anti-Chinese feeling in California and elsewhere. The Haymarket Square bombing took place on May 4, 1886, in Chicago, Illinois. As police arrived to break up a labor rally, someone threw a bomb at them. Following the explosion the police shot into the crowd. Seven police and four workers were killed. Seven anarchists were accused of the crime. All were convicted. Five were sentenced to death. One committed suicide in prison, and the other four were hanged. Ellis Island was opened as an immigrant inspection station in 1892. It operated until 1954. Coxey’s Army was a march of unemployed workers organized by Jacob Coxey, an Ohio businessman. The march was intended to publicize hardships experienced by working men as a result of the depression that began in 1893. The march began in Ohio. Participants marched to Washington, D.C., where they dispersed after a speech by Coxey.

Day 87

Samuel Gompers was president of the American Federation of Labor, which was composed of unions serving skilled workers. In 1894, the American Railway Union went on strike to protest conditions at the Pullman Company, which made railroad sleeping cars. Union members refused to work on trains carrying Pullman cars. This caused chaos on the railroads. The federal government intervened against the strike. Attorney General Richard Olney encouraged federal judges like Judge Peter Grosscup to issue injunctions against the strikers. Federal troops helped put down the strike. Eugene V. Debs, the head of the American Railway Union, was sent to prison for six months for violating the injunction. In the passage, Gompers is chastising Judge Grosscup for taking action against the strikers. He accuses the judge of failing to appreciate the conditions that led workers to unionize and strike for better working conditions. Gompers argues that workers have to join unions because business owners treat them like disposable machines.

Day 88

The proper chronological order is Progress and Poverty, by Henry George; Looking Backward, by Edward Bellamy; How the Other Half Lives, by Jacob Riis. George’s Progress and Poverty (1879) attempted to explain why poverty persisted in a time of technological progress. George argued that landowners benefitted disproportionately from social progress, enabling them to charge higher rents, hurting wage-earners. He called for a single tax on land values to redress the balance. In his novel Looking Backward (1888), Bellamy envisioned a future where all Americans had peacefully joined together to create a socialist utopia. Riis in How the Other Half Lives (1890) graphically described the slums of the Lower Side in New York City and offered suggestions to improve the lives of the people living in the tenements.

Day 89

The Statue of Liberty, or Liberty Enlightening the World, was conceived of as a gift of the French people to the United States. It was designed by Frederic Auguste Bartholdi and was constructed by Gustave Eiffel. Americans paid for the statue’s pedestal, with the money being raised by subscription. The statue was dedicated October 28, 1886. The statue was originally intended to celebrate American freedom and the centennial of American independence. It gained new meaning as a symbol of welcome to immigrants fleeing hardship and tyranny abroad. To help raise money for the pedestal, Emma Lazarus wrote a poem entitled “The New Colossus,” which included the lines: “Give me your tired, your poor—Your huddled masses yearning to breathe free.” In 1903, a plaque bearing Lazarus’s poem was placed on the statue’s pedestal.

Day 90

The late nineteenth century in the United States is called the Gilded Age because it was a time of extremes. Businessmen and industrialists made enormous fortunes while many American lived in poverty. It was a time of intense political participation by many Americans but also a time of widespread political corruption, with political machines dominating many localities. Because of this, social critics could assert that the booming and bustling American society of the day was not experiencing a golden age, but instead was a period of a baser material, gilded to look golden. Historians debate the justice and accuracy of such a judgment of the period.

Day 91

Vertical integration and horizontal integration refer to ways in which late nineteenth-century companies structured their production. Vertical integration refers to a company controlling every phase of production from the acquisition of raw materials through manufacturing, to marketing and distribution. Horizontal integration involves a company controlling production by purchasing or merging with competitors. Andrew Carnegie’s Carnegie Steel is a famous example of vertical integration. John D. Rockefeller’s Standard Oil is a well-known example of horizontal integration. Eventually Carnegie engaged in horizontal integration by purchasing other steel companies, and Rockefeller pursued vertical integration through buying up oilfields.

Day 92

Lord Bryce in this passage is referring to the increasing prevalence of political machines in late nineteenth-century America. Most, though not all, political machines were located in large cities. Political machines were run by bosses, most of whom, as Bryce notes, preferred to operate behind the scenes, though some bosses held elective office. Political machines financed themselves through their control of public contracts, often receiving kickbacks from contractors as the price of business. Political machines kept the loyalty of voters by distributing jobs and favors. For immigrants in the big cities, often the only forms of welfare help available to them were the benefits that they received from machines in exchange for their votes. The most famous and long-lasting of these political organizations was Tammany Hall, the Democratic machine in New York City.

Day 93

The United States purchased Alaska from Russia in 1867. Unaware of Alaska’s potential resources, many critics termed the purchase “Seward’s Folly” after Secretary of State William H. Seward. Captain Alfred Thayer Mahan’s The Influence of Sea Power upon History was published in 1890. Its argument for the importance of a large navy persuaded Theodore Roosevelt and other American policymakers to push for increases in the size of the United States Navy. When Queen Liliuokalani of Hawaii attempted to revise the Hawaiian constitution to limit the powers of the influential American planters on the islands, they organized a successful coup in January 1893. The planters ruled a republic of Hawaii until the United States annexed Hawaii in 1898. The Spanish–American War was fought from April 21 to August 13, 1898. The United States forced Spain to relinquish Cuba and acquired Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines. The Platt Amendment was part of an Army appropriations bill in 1901. It prohibited Cuba from entering into agreements with other countries without American permission, and gave the United States the right to intervene in Cuban politics “when necessary.”

Day 94

The proper chronological order is Open Door Note, Boxer Rebellion, Great White Fleet. Secretary of State John Hay sent his first Open Door Note to the other great powers on September 6, 1899. He proposed that the powers allow all countries to trade in China without interference. This became the foundation of the U.S. Open Door policy in China. The Boxer Rebellion was an uprising of Chinese nationalists angered by the growing influence of foreign powers in China. In 1900, Boxer rebels and elements of the Chinese army besieged the Legation Quarter in the capital of Beijing. American forces joined an international army that rescued the besieged in Beijing and suppressed the uprising. The Great White Fleet was organized by President Theodore Roosevelt. He sent a powerful fleet of 16 battleships and their escorts, all painted white to show their peaceful intentions, on a goodwill tour around the world that lasted from December 16, 1907, to February 22, 1909. The Great White Fleet was a public relations success that also demonstrated American naval power.

Day 95

William Randolph Hearst was the son of George Hearst, who made a fortune mining and became a U.S. senator. Hearst developed an interest in newspapers. After running a newspaper in San Francisco, Hearst bought the New York Morning Journal in 1895. Here Hearst engaged in a circulation war with Joseph Pulitzer and his New York World. As they competed for readers, both editors pioneered what came to be known as “Yellow Journalism,” emphasizing sensationalism, human interest stories, and innovations like comic strips. As part of this competition, Hearst published lurid, and often inaccurate, stories about the Spanish war against revolutionaries in Cuba. Hearst and Pulitzer played a major role in convincing the American people that the United States should go to war with Spain.

Day 96

With victory in the Spanish-American War, Americans had to decide what to do about the Philippines after Commodore George Dewey won the Battle of Manila Bay. Supporters of a new American imperial role in the world believed that the United States should claim the Philippines. Theodore Roosevelt believed that the island chain offered the United States commercial advantages in the Asian market. He also saw the Philippines as a new frontier to replace the one that had closed in the West. Ultimately, President William McKinley decided to take the Philippines because he believed that if the United States walked away, other imperial powers like Japan and Germany would move in and claim the islands. Anti-imperialists like William Jennings Bryan, Andrew Carnegie, and Mark Twain believed that administering an imperial possession and a subject people would violate American traditions of liberty. Others worried that an empire would prove too expensive or worried about economic competition from colonized peoples with a lower standard of living. Bryan ran on an anti-imperialism platform against McKinley in the presidential election of 1900 and lost. In the Philippines, the United States had to fight a bloody war to suppress a Filipino insurrection that lasted from 1899 to 1902.

Day 97

The proper chronological order is acquisition of the Panama Canal Zone, Roosevelt Corollary, Dollar Diplomacy. The United States wanted to construct a canal through the isthmus of Panama connecting the Atlantic and Pacific oceans. In 1902, when a treaty purchasing a canal zone from Colombia fell through, President Theodore Roosevelt supported a revolution that made Panama independent. The United States quickly concluded a treaty with the new Panamanian government allowing the construction of a canal. In 1902–1903, Venezuela was blockaded by warships from Britain, Germany, and Italy to compel the government there to pay overdue debts. Disturbed by this European activity in the Western Hemisphere, in 1904 President Theodore Roosevelt announced his Roosevelt Corollary to the Monroe Doctrine; the United States would intervene in any Caribbean or Latin American country where disorder threatened to bring on European military action. This made the United States the “policeman” of the Western Hemisphere. President William Howard Taft, who came to office in 1909, hoped to replace military incursions with American economic investment designed to stabilize countries wracked with unrest. This policy came to be known as “Dollar Diplomacy.”

Day 98

Leonard Wood saw the role of the U.S. Army as supporting the Cuban people as they created civil government after years of rule by Spain. As governor of Cuba, Wood not only fostered the beginnings of democratic institutions, he also launched a public health campaign that greatly reduced the dangers posed by diseases like yellow fever. Wood is seen as a sort of Progressive reformer who operated overseas. Although broadly benevolent in their approach to the Cubans, the Americans put limits on Cuban sovereignty with the Platt Amendment in 1901, which set constraints on Cuban foreign policy and allowed the United States to intervene in Cuba when thought necessary. The Americans were prepared to give the Cubans many liberties, but not enough freedom to oppose vital American interests. After leaving in 1902, American troops returned in 1906 when the Cuban government fell apart. They stayed until 1909. The United States would not abandon its paternalistic policies toward Cuba until the presidencies of Herbert Hoover and Franklin D. Roosevelt.

Day 99

The film takes a very positive perspective on Theodore Roosevelt’s role in building the Panama Canal. Roosevelt is seen as completing Christopher Columbus’s mission of discovery and exploration, looking for a route to the Indies. The canal is seen as a triumph of American energy, enterprise, and engineering. The film sees the canal as a measure of the U.S. position at the forefront of progress. At the end of the film, President Calvin Coolidge pays tribute to Theodore Roosevelt for his work in making the canal possible.

Day 100

Vice President Theodore Roosevelt became president when William McKinley was assassinated in 1901. He was elected president in his own right in 1904, defeating the Democratic candidate Judge Alton B. Parker. The 1906 Hepburn Act gave the Interstate Commerce Commission greater regulatory authority over railroads, including the power to set maximum railroad rates. William Howard Taft was Theodore Roosevelt’s handpicked successor. He defeated third-time Democratic presidential candidate William Jennings Bryan in the election of 1908. The National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP) was founded in 1909 by a group of African American and white civil rights activists that included W.E.B. Du Bois. The NAACP quickly became the most influential civil rights organization in the United States. Woodrow Wilson was elected president in 1912. He was the first Democrat to be elected president since Grover Cleveland in 1892. He benefited from a split in the Republican Party. He defeated William Howard Taft, the Republican candidate; Theodore Roosevelt, the candidate of the Progressive (Bull Moose) Party; and Eugene V. Debs, the candidate of the Socialist Party.

Day 101

The proper chronological order is Pure Food and Drug Act, Federal Reserve Act, Clayton Antitrust Act. The 1906 Pure Food and Drug Act gave the U.S. government the authority to regulate foods and drugs for purity and safety. The act required manufacturers to list active ingredients on labels and gave the Food and Drug Administration the power to test products. Reformers like Dr. Harvey Wiley had been pushing for such a law, but the revelations of Upton Sinclair’s The Jungle (1906) hastened passage of the legislation. The 1913 Federal Reserve Act addressed the longstanding issue of creating a central banking system and a flexible currency for the United States. This legislation established a Federal Reserve System, with a Federal Reserve Board to act as a governing body and 12 regional Federal Reserve Banks. The Federal Reserve System issues Federal Reserve Notes to act as American currency. This is still the system in the United States. The 1914 Clayton Antitrust Act strengthened the laws against monopolistic and illegal practices by corporations. The Clayton Act exempted labor unions from these provisions, giving a boost to the labor movement.

Day 102

After serving three terms in the U.S. House of Representatives, Robert La Follette became concerned about the power wielded by business interests in his state of Wisconsin. He organized an insurgent group within the Wisconsin Republican party, and in 1900 was elected governor. In office, La Follette enacted a series of reforms, including greater regulation of railroad rates. In 1906, La Follette moved on to the U.S. Senate, where he remained until his death in 1925. La Follette embodied the Progressive Movement’s effort to exert greater public control over corporations. This was a battle that was waged on the state as well as national levels.

Day 103

Settlement houses were established by middle class reformers to provide services to the urban poor. Hull House helped recent immigrants adjust to life in the United States. Among the services Hull House provided were classes on language, domestic skills, and the arts. Hull House also sponsored clubs, concerts, and other recreational activities. Reformers living at Hull House also conducted studies of the neighborhood to aid in social planning and advocating for new public institutions such as a juvenile court.

Day 104

Muckrakers played an important role in publicizing corporate abuses, political malfeasance, and other social problems during the Progressive Era. They helped mobilize public opinion behind progressive reforms. Although muckrakers published in a variety of venues, some periodicals such as McClure’s Magazine became famous for their investigative journalism. Famous muckrakers include Ida M. Tarbell, who wrote a critical history of John D. Rockefeller’s Standard Oil, and Lincoln Steffens, who exposed the workings of urban political machines.

Day 105

The proper chronological order is Northern Securities Case, Ballinger-Pinchot Affair, and Sixteenth Amendment. The Northern Securities Company was organized in 1901 by James J. Hill, E. H. Harriman, and J. P. Morgan, and controlled a number of railroads. In 1902, President Theodore Roosevelt directed the Justice Department to sue for the dissolution of the company under the Sherman Antitrust Act. The directors of the Northern Securities Company fought back in court until 1904, when the Supreme Court ruled in favor of the government, and the company was dissolved. The Northern Securities Case was the first of a number of Progressive Era prosecutions of “trusts” that breathed life into the Sherman Antitrust Act. The 1909–1910 Ballinger-Pinchot Affair began with a dispute between Secretary of the Interior Richard Ballinger and Gifford Pinchot, the head of the U.S. Forest Service, over the opening of public land in Alaska to coal mining. President Taft ultimately sided with Ballinger and fired Pinchot, a close friend of Theodore Roosevelt. This well-publicized dispute contributed to the eventual political rupture between Roosevelt and Taft. The Sixteenth Amendment, ratified in 1913, gave Congress the power to levy an income tax. This taxing power enabled Congress to vastly increase federal spending over the course of the twentieth century.

Day 106

The disastrous Triangle Shirtwaist Fire shocked the nation in 1911. It had immediate consequences in New York. After an investigation, new workplace safety laws were enacted in New York. The New York legislature also passed a bill to shorten workers’ hours. These bills became models for other states. The tragedy also proved a spur to unionization. Many garment workers joined the growing International Ladies’ Garment Workers’ Union (ILGWU).

Day 107

Theodore Roosevelt began promulgating his New Nationalism in 1910, and it became the basis of his run for the presidency in 1912 as the candidate of the Progressive (Bull Moose) party. Roosevelt called for an expanded role for the federal government in regulating business and the economy. Roosevelt argued that government had to grow to match the power of the big corporations that were central to the economic future of the United States. He also believed that the government should take the lead in promoting greater social justice. Elements of the New Nationalism included a federal income tax, a federal securities commission, social insurance, the eight-hour day, and suffrage for women. Woodrow Wilson’s New Freedom rejected Roosevelt’s big-government solutions to social problems and his assumption that big corporations were inevitable. Wilson called for a reduction in the protective tariff, a more aggressive antitrust policy, and banking reform to reduce the power of Wall Street. Wilson won the election of 1912 when Roosevelt and President William H. Taft split the Republican vote. Once in office, Wilson moved closer to the regulatory vision of the New Nationalism with laws such as the Federal Trade Commission Act of 1914.

Day 108

World War I broke out in August 1914, following a diplomatic crisis precipitated by the assassination of the Austrian Archduke Franz Ferdinand. The 1916 Sussex Pledge was a reaction of the Imperial German government to American criticism of its campaign of unrestricted submarine warfare against the British and French. The Germans had recently sunk the Sussex, with the loss of 50 lives. The Germans promised not to sink passenger ships and to only sink enemy merchant ships after their crews had been allowed to escape in lifeboats. The breaking of the Sussex Pledge by the Germans in early 1917 led to American participation in the war. The 1917 Zimmerman Telegram was a message from the German foreign minister to the Mexican government. In the event of war with the United States, the Germans offered a military alliance to the Mexicans and stated that they would not object if the Mexicans reconquered lands lost in the Mexican War of the 1840s. The British intercepted this message and gave it to the U.S. government. Publication of the telegram angered Americans and helped prepare them for war with Germany. The Fourteen Points were a list of war objectives issued by President Woodrow Wilson in January, 1918. Among these objectives were freedom of the seas, national self-determination, and a league of nations. President Wilson saw the Fourteen Points as a basis for the postwar peace. The Treaty of Versailles was signed in 1919. While it did not embody all of Wilson’s Fourteen Points, it did include a League of Nations.

Day 109

The American Battle Monuments Commission faced many challenges. They had to relocate the bodies of American soldiers killed in many battles to these new cemeteries. In many cases, they could not identify the dead. They also had to arrange the cemeteries and memorials in a way that was tasteful and respectful of the dead. They also had to memorialize a war that meant many things to many people. Most scholars believe that the Commission did an excellent job balancing its different responsibilities.

Day 110

President Woodrow Wilson’s War Message to Congress envisioned a very idealistic war. The United States went to war because of the German submarine campaign and questions of the freedom of the seas. Wilson was interested in ensuring the freedom of the seas, but he also wanted to see a new, more democratic world emerge from the conflict. He embodied his ideas on a just peace in his Fourteen Points. Such an idealistic understanding of the war caused problems when the Treaty of Versailles failed to live up to all the ideals in the Fourteen Points. Many Americans became disillusioned with the outcome of the war. This was intensified when Wilson refused to compromise with the Senate majority over the League of Nations, leading to the failure of the Treaty of Versailles in the Senate. Postwar writers such as Ernest Hemingway questioned whether the idealistic rhetoric of Woodrow Wilson justified the bloody horrors experienced by soldiers in the war.

Day 111

The proper chronological order is Lusitania, Lever Food and Fuel Control Act, Meuse-Argonne Offensive. The Lusitania was a British passenger liner that was sunk by a German submarine off the coast of Ireland on May 7, 1915. 1,198 passengers and crew lost their lives, including 128 Americans. This sinking outraged American public opinion and led to a diplomatic crisis with Germany. With the Sussex pledge the following year, the Germans would temporarily back off from their full submarine campaign. The 1917 Lever Food and Fuel Control Act gave the president the power to regulate the production, transportation, and price of food and fuel during the war. Herbert Hoover was named the head of the U.S. Food Administration charged with implementing the production and allocation of food. This law reflected the total war mobilization of all American resources for the war effort. The Meuse-Argonne Offensive was the last and largest major offensive of the American Expeditionary Force in World War I. The offensive began on September 26, 1918, and continued to the end of the war on November 11. Over a million American soldiers took part and 26,000 lost their lives. The American attack cut crucial German supply lines and helped convince the Germans to call for an armistice.

Day 112

The National World War I Museum and Memorial exhibitions give a good sense of the vast scale of the Great War. The fighting was deeply influenced by technological advances such as machine guns and artillery on recoilless platforms that literally drove armies underground into trenches. Weapons such as poison gas, tanks, airplanes, and submarines were deployed to break the stalemate on the Western Front. Initially these proved indecisive and added to the casualties. World War I was a total war that required the full mobilization of a nation’s resources. During the war, the U.S. government took control of industrial production, food production, and transportation, running the railroads. Another consequence of this vast mobilization was the movement of women into jobs previously held by men. The significant contributions of women to the war effort helped pave the way for the passage of women’s suffrage in 1919.

Day 113

The 1918 Sedition Act was an expansion of the 1917 Espionage Act. It was designed to quell dissent and actions that might interfere with the war effort. It was also intended to end vigilante actions by citizens against supposed traitors by giving the government the power to prosecute them. The law reflected the widespread intolerance for dissenting viewpoints on the war. A notable figure prosecuted under the Sedition Act was the Socialist party leader Eugene V. Debs, who was sentenced to 10 years in prison for advocating resistance to the draft. The Sedition Act was repealed in December 1918.

Day 114

President Woodrow Wilson and supporters of the League of Nations believed that the international organization would prevent any more conflicts like World War I. Operating under the principle of collective security, member states would act to resolve international disputes and oppose unprovoked aggressions. Supporters of the League believed that it would eventually rectify problems created by the harsh terms imposed by the Treaty of Versailles on the defeated nations. “Reservationists” were concerned that the League of Nations threatened aspects of American sovereignty. They feared the League’s collective security provisions threatened congressional war powers. They also believed that the League posed problems for such traditional pillars of American foreign policy as the Monroe Doctrine. The “reservationists” were prepared to support the Treaty of Versailles and the League, but only if reservations about these concerns were attached to the document. This distinguished them from a handful of “irreconcilables” in the Senate who opposed any sort of American participation in the League. In the end, President Wilson refused to compromise, and the Treaty of Versailles never received enough votes, with or without reservations, to be ratified.

Day 115

A constitutional amendment to introduce national prohibition was passed by Congress in 1917 and was ratified by the states in 1919. The Volstead Act to enforce prohibition passed Congress on October 28, 1919. Prohibition went into effect on January 1, 1920. The Washington Disarmament Conference convened on November 12, 1921. It resulted in treaties that limited naval construction, maintained the territorial status-quo in the Pacific, and upheld the Open Door in China. The 1925 Scopes Trial resulted from a challenge to a Tennessee law that prohibited the teaching of evolution in that state’s schools. The presence of William Jennings Bryan on the prosecution team and Clarence Darrow on the defense team helped make the trial a national sensation. At the trial the law was upheld, but religious fundamentalism was criticized by the defense. Charles Lindbergh became an international hero when he became the first person to make a solo flight across the Atlantic Ocean in his plane the Spirit of St. Louis on May 20–21, 1927. After several bumps, the stock market crashed in October 1929. This set in motion an economic crisis that resulted in the Great Depression.

Day 116

The name “Jazz Age” is appropriate for the 1920s in part because this was a time when jazz music became very popular. Both African American musicians like Louis Armstrong and white jazz musicians such as the bandleader Paul Whiteman enjoyed a great deal of success. Serious composers like George Gershwin wrote music heavily influenced by jazz. The name “Jazz Age” is also appropriate because jazz music helped capture the cultural rebelliousness and excitement about change that was very prominent in the 1920s.

Day 117

The proper chronological order is Palmer Raids, National Origins Act, execution of Sacco and Vanzetti. Attorney General A. Mitchell Palmer launched the Palmer Raids between November 1919 and January 1920. These were a response to terrorist bombings. The raids rounded up thousands of left-wing radicals, and 500 were deported. This was a major moment in the first Red Scare. The National Origins Act was part of the larger Immigration Act of 1924. It was a response to concerns about high immigration that were intensified by the Red Scare. This limited immigration to 2 percent of the number of any ethnic group living in the United States in 1890. This greatly reduced immigration, especially from Southern and Eastern Europe. Nicola Sacco and Bartolomeo Vanzetti were two Italian-born anarchists who were convicted of a 1920 payroll robbery murder. Some observers believed that the men were innocent and were victims of a prejudiced trial during the Red Scare. After many protests, the men were executed in 1927.

Day 118

In this speech, Coolidge is making the point that decreases in government spending benefit ordinary Americans by reducing taxes, which allows them to keep more of their money. Like his predecessor Warren G. Harding, Coolidge believed in limited government. Both Harding and Coolidge cut government spending and lowered taxes. They agreed with Secretary of the Treasury Andrew Mellon that lowered tax rates would increase prosperity and raise actual tax revenues. The 1920s did see a period of dramatic economic growth in the country. Coolidge significantly reduced the national debt in his time in office.

Day 119

In May 1927, Charles Lindbergh became the first person to make a solo flight across the Atlantic in his plane The Spirit of St. Louis. Lindbergh was honored for his courage and skill in singlehandedly accomplishing a feat that had cost other pilots their lives. Lindbergh increased the esteem of his contemporaries through his modesty and unwillingness to financially capitalize on his achievement. Many Americans saw Lindbergh as a person who was carrying on the pathfinding tradition of the American pioneers, and in doing so demonstrating that traditional American virtues were possible in the modern era. Lindbergh was also seen as a harbinger of a new era of technological possibilities. His close identification with his plane made him seem a cutting-edge figure in a decade experiencing rapid technological change.

Day 120

Governor Alfred “Al” Smith of New York was the Democratic nominee for president in 1928. A native of New York City, and in his early career associated with the Tammany Hall political machine, Smith vehemently opposed Prohibition. Herbert Hoover, the Republican nominee, expressed some support for Prohibition, calling it an “experiment noble in purpose.” Hoover, famous as a philanthropist and energetic public servant, easily won the election. Smith’s desire to repeal Prohibition, along with his Roman Catholicism, hurt him with rural voters who were still a powerful political force. Smith did very well in the big cities and laid the groundwork for the coalition of urban, ethnic voters who would be a political mainstay of Franklin D. Roosevelt’s New Deal.

Day 121

Frederick Lewis Allen is describing changes in the behavior of young women in the 1920s. Women had received the vote in 1920, but one of the most conspicuous developments of the decade was the growing social freedom of women. Women shed their corsets and long, heavy dresses and embraced makeup. The “flapper,” with her bobbed hair, short dress, and mannish figure became the symbol of the age. As Allen points out, many young women joined young men in smoking and drinking. Some women took advantage of decreasing parental controls, and the freedom provided by the automobile, to partake in sexual experimentation. This behavior contributed to the reputation of the 1920s as the “Jazz Age.”

Day 122

The 1930 Hawley-Smoot Tariff was intended to protect American industries and jobs by making foreign goods more expensive. It had the unintended effect of leading other countries to increase their tariffs, hurting foreign trade and Americans who worked in industries that exported goods. The 1931 Scottsboro Boys Trial involved nine African American teenagers who were convicted of raping two white women on a train. The trial, held in segregated Alabama, was widely thought to be unfair. The young men are now believed to have been innocent. Created in 1932, the Reconstruction Finance Corporation (RFC) was an effort by President Herbert Hoover and Congress to combat the Great Depression. The RFC lent money to banks and other businesses. Prohibition ended in 1933. The Twenty-first Amendment repealing the Eighteenth Amendment was ratified on December 5, 1933. The Congress of Industrial Organizations (CIO) was organized in 1935. The CIO unionized many unskilled workers in large industries.

Day 123

The proper chronological order is National Industrial Recovery Act (NIRA), Social Security Act, Justice Reorganization Bill. The 1933 NIRA was a centerpiece of the First New Deal. The NIRA attempted to restore industrial prosperity by encouraging industries to set standards for prices and competition, while also protecting the rights of workers. The NIRA also set up a Public Works Administration (PWA) to provide work for the unemployed. The 1935 Social Security Act was a centerpiece of the Second New Deal. It helped lay the foundations for the modern American welfare state by creating a retirement plan for older Americans and unemployment insurance for workers who lost their jobs. The 1937 Justice Reorganization Bill was an attempt by President Franklin D. Roosevelt to increase the size of the Supreme Court so he could appoint justices favorable to the New Deal. A coalition of conservative Democrats, many of them from the South, and Republicans defeated this bill because it seemed to place too much power in Roosevelt’s hands.

Day 124

Mrs. Henderson’s letter is referring to the effects of the Dust Bowl. Beginning in 1934, a series of droughts in the 1930s dried out soil that had been exposed by years of farming in the Lower Plains states and produced immense clouds of dust. Many people lost their farms. By 1940, 3.5 million people moved away from the affected area. Many moved to California, where they were known as “Okies.” John Steinbeck chronicled the plight of such people in his novel The Grapes of Wrath (1939).

Day 125

It is not true that President Herbert Hoover did nothing to help people during the Great Depression. He actively used the power of the federal government to combat the depression. He cut taxes and launched public works to provide employment. He signed legislation such as the Hawley-Smoot Tariff and helped create the Reconstruction Finance Corporation (RFC) to pump money into the economy by lending money to banks and businesses. Hoover feared that too much government involvement in people’s lives would undermine American individualism. He worked hard to organize local private charities to provide relief for the unemployed. Franklin Roosevelt and his New Deal built on the foundation established by Hoover and agencies like the RFC. Roosevelt was willing to go further than Hoover in expanding the reach of government, and used government agencies to provide direct relief to the unemployed and poor. Roosevelt brought an experimental approach to fighting the depression, and created many different sorts of agencies, such as the Agricultural Adjustment Administration (AAA) and the Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA) in an effort to improve the situation.

Day 126

Although Franklin D. Roosevelt’s New Deal failed to end the Great Depression, he did build a New Deal Coalition that endured for many decades. Born to wealth, as his home indicates, FDR had a capacity to empathize and communicate with ordinary Americans. Some scholars attribute this to his long struggle with the polio that crippled his legs. FDR spoke directly to the American people in radio addresses that became known as his “Fireside Chats.” This way he became a reassuring presence in many people’s homes. FDR gave the impression that he cared about ordinary Americans. Although the New Deal did not solve the nation’s economic problems, it provided relief and a measure of security to millions.

Day 127

Huey P. Long served as governor of Louisiana from 1928–1932, and as U.S. senator from 1932–1935. Long built a political machine in Louisiana that made him the virtual dictator of the state. He remained very popular in Louisiana because he spoke up for the poor, built a string of charity hospitals, and expanded schools, with free textbooks for schoolchildren. As an ambitious senator, Long aimed for the presidency. He won favor with millions by promoting a “Share Our Wealth” program that would cap personal fortunes at $50 million and provide an annual income of $2,000–3,000 for every family. Many observers believed that Long was a dangerous demagogue. FDR’s Second New Deal was intended in part to curb his popular appeal. A Louisiana doctor who opposed Long’s actions assassinated him in September 1935.

Day 128

President Franklin D. Roosevelt and his New Deal provided increased legal support to the labor movement. The 1935 Wagner Act affirmed the right of workers to unionize and bargain collectively. The Wagner Act also outlawed certain labor practices and established the National Labor Relations Board (NLRB) to enforce the labor laws. This led to an effort to expand unionization. John L. Lewis, the president of the United Mine Workers (UMW), helped found the Congress of Industrial Workers (CIO) in 1935. The CIO worked to unionize unskilled workers in large industries. In 1936 and 1937 there was a wave of strikes in the automobile and steel industries. Workers in the automobile industry staged “sit-down” strikes, where they occupied manufacturing plants. This tactic seemed radical to many Americans. The intense labor strife in this period was also disturbing. This is why Lewis went on to the radio to reassure his listeners that the striking union members were not radicals influenced by communistic ideas, but could, if treated fairly, become mainstays of the American free enterprise system.

Day 129

President Franklin D. Roosevelt promoted recovery, relief, and reform in his New Deal Program. The First New Deal of 1933–1934 emphasized recovery and relief, with agencies such as the National Recovery Administration (NRA), Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC), and the Agricultural Adjustment Administration (AAA). Elements of reform in the First New Deal were the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) and the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). The Second New Deal, launched in 1935, was in part a response to challenges from leaders like Huey Long and his “Share Our Wealth” Plan and Francis Townshend with his “Townshend Plan” of old-age pensions. The Second New Deal emphasized reform though legislation like the Social Security Act, which provided aid to the aged, children, and the unemployed, and the Wagner Act, which provided government protections to organized labor.

Day 130

The Neutrality Act of 1935 was an effort to keep the United States out of a conflict like World War I. The law established a six-month moratorium on American trade in weapons or military supplies with warring countries. Following the signing of a nonaggression pact with the Soviet Union, Germany invaded Poland on September 1, 1939, beginning World War II. The Destroyers for Bases Agreement was concluded between the United States and Great Britain on September 2, 1940. The United States gave Great Britain 50 retired destroyers in exchange for bases in British territories in the Western Hemisphere. The British needed the ships to fight submarines, while the United States was interested in building up its defenses. Technically, the agreement was a violation of American neutrality. The 1941 Lend-Lease Act provided military supplies to countries resisting Nazi Germany. Promoted as a self-defense measure, the Lend-Lease Act was an expression of growing American opposition to the Axis Powers. Internment camps for Japanese were established in 1942, after the December 1941 bombing of Pearl Harbor. Japanese living on the West Coast of the United States, citizens and noncitizens alike, were regarded as a security threat and moved to internment camps. Although the Supreme Court upheld the constitutionality of this war measure in 1944, it is now regarded as an unnecessary violation of these people’s rights.

Day 131

The proper chronological order is Battle of Midway, Battle of Guadalcanal, and Battle of the Bulge. The Battle of Midway, fought June 4–7, 1942, turned the tide of the war in the Pacific Theater. In a battle dominated by aircraft carriers, the Americans sank four Japanese aircraft carriers while losing one aircraft carrier of their own. After this defeat, the Japanese were forced on the defensive. The Battle of Guadalcanal was fought from August 7, 1942, to February 9, 1943. The American landing on the island of Guadalcanal was the first American offensive of the Pacific War. In a series of battles on the island and in the waters surrounding it, American land, air, and naval forces inflicted heavy losses on the Japanese and forced them to fall back. The Battle of the Bulge, fought from December 16, 1944, to January 25, 1945, resulted from a desperate German offensive that aimed at inflicting such a defeat on the Allies that they would be forced to make a compromise peace. After being caught by surprise, American forces stopped the Germans and forced them back, inflicting losses the Germans could no longer afford.

Day 132

Senator Gerald P. Nye of North Dakota was an influential isolationist. From 1934 to 1936, Nye headed a Senate investigation into the munitions industry to see if prominent American businesses had helped lead the United States into World War I. He played an important role in passing the neutrality laws designed to keep the United States out of another war. Nye opposed American intervention in World War II. He was not alone. The America First organization, founded in 1940, became an influential force in American politics. Isolationists remained vocal until the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941.

Day 133

The World War II Valor in the Pacific National Monument lies above the resting place of the USS Arizona. During the December 7, 1941, Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor, the Arizona, an American battleship, was sunk with the loss of 1,177 men. In all, during the Japanese assault, 2,403 Americans were killed and 1,178 were wounded. The United States and Japan disagreed over Japanese conquests in China and Indochina. The Japanese attacked because they resented American economic sanctions, which cut them off from supplies of American oil and metals. The Japanese believed that they could capture sources of these resources from the British and Dutch possessions in Asia. To do this safely, they had to neutralize the American fleet. The Japanese surprise attack infuriated Americans. The Pearl Harbor attack failed to catch the American aircraft carriers, which were out of port, and failed to destroy Pearl Harbor as a functioning naval base. The United States would soon be able to launch a series of counterattacks that eventually destroyed the Japanese Empire.

Day 134

The proper chronological order is beginning of the draft, Revenue Act of 1942, G.I. Bill. In 1940, in response to the security threat posed by the outbreak of World War II and Axis victories, the United States launched a massive rearmament program to build up American defenses. Part of this program was the institution of a draft to build up the army. The Selective Training and Service Act of 1940 was the first peacetime draft in American history. The Revenue Act of 1942 was a wartime measure to help raise funds for the war. The number of Americans paying income tax was increased. The top tax rate on excess profits was raised to 90 percent. The Serviceman’s Readjustment Bill of 1944, popularly known as the G.I. Bill, provided benefits to returning members of the armed services. Benefits included low-interest loans to purchase homes and start businesses, as well as funds to help pay for education. These benefits helped many veterans successfully reenter civilian life and played a significant role in spurring postwar prosperity.

Day 135

Following the Allied breakout from the D-Day beachhead in July, 1944, a dispute arose over the best way to complete the rout of the German armies and end the war. The Allied armies moved so fast that they began to outrun available supplies, especially gasoline. The British General Bernard Montgomery argued for a narrow front thrust into Germany by his forces. He believed that this would disrupt the German defenses and bring on a German surrender. Such an attack would require giving him most of the available fuel, bringing American operations to a halt. General Dwight D. Eisenhower, the Supreme Commander of Allied forces in Europe, preferred a broad front strategy that would have all his armies advance. Debate over the narrow versus broad front strategies continues to this day. Proponents of the narrow front strategy argue that the broad front advance delayed victory, while proponents of the broad front strategy argue that a narrow thrust would have been overwhelmed by the Germans.

Day 136

By August 1945, Japan was isolated and objectively beaten. Despite this, the military rulers of Japan declared that they would fight on and were gathering large forces to resist an American invasion. The Americans were preparing Operation Olympic, an assault on the Japanese home islands. Given the ferocity of the Japanese resistance in the bloody battles of Iwo Jima and Okinawa, the Americans were expecting heavy casualties. With Germany defeated, troops in Europe, including Paul Fussell, were slated to be shipped to the Pacific and the climactic battle for Japan. Fussell’s reaction to the dropping of the atomic bombs was similar to that of many American soldiers—relief that they would not have to fight a harrowing battle that might have cost their lives. In his essay, Fussell responds to critics of the atomic bombing by pointing out that a rapid conclusion to the war in the Pacific not only prevented a bloody invasion of Japan that would have killed untold numbers of Americans and Japanese, but it also ended the many deaths from ongoing fighting and from Japanese cruelties in the lands that they occupied.

Day 137

The February 1945 Yalta Conference between President Franklin D. Roosevelt, British Prime Minister Winston Churchill, and Soviet Premier Josef Stalin discussed postwar conditions in Europe. Stalin promised free elections in Poland, a pledge he later broke. On March 5, 1946, Winston Churchill, now out of office, gave his “Iron Curtain” speech while visiting the United States. His vision of Europe divided between Communist and democratic nations was a rhetorical acknowledgment of the beginning of the cold war. The Federal Employee Loyalty program was introduced in 1947. It was a reaction to the recognition that Soviet spies had infiltrated the American government. Loyalty Review Boards attempted to root Communists and Communist sympathizers out of government employment. The People’s Republic of China was established on October 1, 1949, after the Communist forces of Mao Zedong won the Chinese Civil War. The Korean War began on June 25, 1950, when Communist North Korean forces invaded South Korea. The United States rapidly entered the conflict to support the South Koreans.

Day 138

The proper chronological order is Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, NATO. In early 1947, the British told the American government that they could no longer protect Greece and Turkey from Communist threats. In gathering Congressional support to take over for the British, President Harry Truman announced what came to be known as the “Truman Doctrine” on March 12, 1947. Truman promised American aid to any country being threatened by Communist aggression or internal subversion. The Marshall Plan was launched in 1948. Named after Secretary of State George Marshall, this was a plan to help restore Western European prosperity and weaken the appeal of Communism through American economic aid. The plan, which encouraged greater European economic integration, proved very successful. Because of concerns about the military threat posed by the Soviet Union, the United States, Canada, and 10 European nations formed the North Atlantic Treaty Organization in 1949. This was the first formal alliance for the United States since it ended its Revolutionary War alliance with France in 1800.

Day 139

George F. Kennan was an American diplomat with extensive experience in the Soviet Union by the mid-1940s. In his “Long Telegram” sent to Washington on February 22, 1946, and his 1947 Foreign Affairs article, initially published anonymously and signed “X,” Kennan formulated the policy of containment. Kennan’s superiors in the Truman Administration found his writings very useful in clarifying their thought and adopted and elaborated on his policy of containment. Kennan believed that Soviet expansionism should be contained through adroit diplomacy. Much to Kennan’s dismay, as time went on American administrations looked to military means of containment.

Day 140

Both President Harry Truman and Senator Joseph McCarthy believed that Soviet spies had infiltrated the American government. Revelations about Soviet spying, including in the atomic bomb program, became public in the late 1940s and early 1950s. President Truman responded to this with his Federal Employee Loyalty program, which established a Loyalty Review Board to sift security risks out of the federal bureaucracy. He believed that this program effectively addressed the problem. On February 9, 1950, Senator McCarthy achieved notoriety by giving a speech claiming that a large number of Communists were serving in the State Department. From that point, McCarthy became influential by accusing the government of harboring Communists and launching Senate investigations of government officials. This infuriated Truman, who regarded McCarthy as an irresponsible demagogue and political partisan. Following maneuvers by the Eisenhower Administration, McCarthy was censured by the Senate on December 2, 1954.

Day 141

The proper chronological order is Geneva Accords, Sputnik, the Soviet downing of a U-2 plane. The Geneva Accords were announced on July 21, 1954. They were the result of a conference called to determine the future of Indochina. While the conference was ongoing, on May 7, the French were forced to surrender the remains of an army at Dien Bien Phu. The Geneva Accords divided Vietnam into North Vietnam, controlled by Ho Chi Minh and his predominantly Communist forces and South Vietnam, controlled by anti-Communist Vietnamese. On October 4, 1957, the Soviet Union launched Sputnik, the first human-made satellite, into space. This technological and public-relations triumph led to dismay in the United States and a greater emphasis on math and science in American education. Anxious to avoid a costly arms race, President Dwight D. Eisenhower sponsored the development of the U-2 spy planes, which could do high-altitude reconnaissance flights over the Soviet Union and monitor Soviet missile installations. On May 1, 1960, the Soviets shot down a U-2 plane with a missile, capturing its pilot, Captain Francis Gary Powers. At first the Eisenhower Administration tried to deny that it had been spying in this way on the Soviet Union. When the Soviets produced Captain Powers, the Americans had to admit that they had been doing so. This incident led to the failure of a summit meeting between President Eisenhower and the Soviet premier Nikita Khrushchev, and a deterioration in relations between the United States and the Soviet Union.

Day 142

During the 1950s, the United States began deploying Intercontinental Ballistic Missiles (ICBMs) as part of its nuclear deterrent against the Soviet Union. In the mid-1950s development began on the solid-fuel Minuteman missile. ICBMs, capable of carrying increasingly destructive nuclear warheads, were the most deadly weapons ever created. Fears grew of a “missile gap” with the Soviet Union and became a political issue. The first Minuteman missiles went into service in 1962. The Minuteman Missile National Historic Site offers insight into the bases where, for decades, men and women remained on alert, ready to fire these ICBMs in the event of a nuclear confrontation with the Soviet Union.

Day 143

In this news conference President Dwight D. Eisenhower articulated the “Domino Theory.” This argues that, if one country in a region falls to Communism, other neighboring countries will succumb as well, like a line of falling dominoes. This formulation is important because it became an underpinning of American involvement in Indochina in the 1950s and 1960s. The United States supported South Vietnam in its struggle with the Viet Cong and North Vietnam in order to prevent Communism from spreading to other countries in East Asia.

Day 144

The disputes over the origins of the cold war took on greater significance than usual academic debates over the past because they often reflected deep political divisions. The cold war lasted over 40 years. It was still ongoing when many of these historical debates took place. The understanding of the past was thought to have relevance for present and future policy. Many of the “revisionists” were angered by American policy in Vietnam, and reflected the anti-establishment sentiments that emerged in the 1960s. “Post-revisionism” emerged in the aftermath of the tumult of the 1960s and reflected a more critical view of the Soviet Union that emerged in the increasingly conservative 1970s and 1980s.

Day 145

The 1947 Taft-Hartley Act was passed over President Harry Truman’s veto in a brief interval between 1947 and 1949 when the Republicans controlled Congress. The law placed some limits on union activity and required union leaders to declare that they were not Communists. Newly elected President Truman proposed an ambitious program of legislation that he called the Fair Deal in 1949. Among other things, Truman called for a universal health care law and the repeal of the Taft-Hartley Act. A coalition of Republicans and Southern Democrats prevented Truman from getting much Fair Deal legislation enacted. In 1952, Republican vice-presidential candidate Richard Nixon was accused of benefitting from an illegal campaign fund. Nixon went on television and gave a speech defending himself during which he referenced to a dog named Checkers that had been given to his daughter. The Checkers Speech was a great success, and Nixon saved his political career. The 1956 Interstate Highway Act was a recognition of the growing importance of automobiles in American life. It laid the foundations for the modern interstate highway system. Beat writer Jack Kerouac published his novel On the Road in 1957. The tale of two friends road-tripping across the country together, On the Road helped inspire the counterculture of the 1960s.

Day 146

In 1948, facing a strong challenge from the Republicans and New York Governor Thomas E. Dewey, President Harry Truman appealed to African American voters by proposing laws to extend greater civil rights protections to them. At the Democratic Convention, he supported a plank favoring greater civil rights for African Americans. This alienated many Southern Democrats. They formed the States’ Rights Democratic Party (Dixiecrats) and nominated South Carolina Governor Strom Thurmond for president. During the campaign, Thurmond defended segregation. Truman won the presidential election, but Thurmond carried four states with a total of 39 electoral votes.

Day 147

President Eisenhower’s Second Inaugural reflects a widespread American prosperity in the 1950s. Prosperity began after World War II, with unemployment falling to 2 percent in 1948. With many competitors hobbled by wartime damage, American industry dominated the world. In 1950, American industries produced 6.2 million automobiles, 6.2 million refrigerators, and 14.6 million radios. The American GNP increased 25 percent from 1953–1961. In 1954, stock prices finally regained the levels last seen in 1929. While poverty persisted in many parts of the United States, for many people the 1950s were indeed “happy days.”

Day 148

One reason for the Pleasantville view of women in the 1950s is that the popular culture of the time exalted the role of woman as homemaker. Many women’s magazines, movies, and television shows persistently portrayed women as happy in the home. A good example of this would be the hit sitcom Leave It to Beaver, which first aired in 1957. Critics of this role for women helped shape the current view in the 1960s. Betty Friedan in The Feminine Mystique (1963) led a reaction against the role of housewife for women. The women’s movement of the 1960s and 1970s, as well as economic change, sparked dramatic differences in women’s expectations for employment.

Day 149

William Levitt was a developer who created seven “Levittowns” in the postwar years. His developments utilized assembly-line construction techniques perfected during World War II to quickly produce communities of largely identical homes that were comfortable and reasonably priced. Levitt’s developments helped spur the rapid growth of the suburbs in the late 1940s and 1950s, as married couples looked for places to raise their families. Levittowns and other suburban developments enabled many Americans to own their own homes in a time of growing prosperity. These suburban communities also reflected the demographic effects of the postwar baby boom with their emphasis on yards and room for children.

Day 150

The proper chronological order is Brown v. Board of Education, Montgomery Bus Boycott, Little Rock Nine. The 1954 Brown v. Board of Education Supreme Court decision was a landmark in the African American struggle for civil rights. The Supreme Court ruled against segregation in schools by declaring that “separate but equal” was illegal. In 1955, Rosa Parks refused to surrender her seat on a Montgomery, Alabama, bus to a white man. She was arrested. This led to an African American boycott of the Montgomery bus system. One of the leaders of the boycott was a minister named Martin Luther King, Jr. The boycott eventually succeeded when the Supreme Court ruled that segregation on city buses was illegal. In 1957, Central High School in Little Rock, Arkansas, was about to be integrated by the admission of nine African American students. This was opposed by many white citizens and Arkansas Governor Orval Faubus, who ordered National Guardsmen to prohibit the Little Rock Nine from entering the school. President Dwight D. Eisenhower federalized the National Guard and deployed Army troops to escort the Little Rock Nine into Central High School.

Day 151

The news story from the St. Louis Post-Dispatch reflects a critical attitude toward rock and roll. The new music, being popularized by bands like Bill Haley and his Comets, is seen as encouraging violent behavior. Many adults in the 1950s believed that rock and roll was “devil’s music,” and associated it with immorality. Rock and roll was embraced by a baby-boom generation of young people who saw it as central to an emerging youth culture. Rooted in jazz, and joining African American and white musical styles, rock music appealed to a broad cross-section of American teenagers. Rock musicians like Chuck Berry and Elvis Presley bridged the racial divide.

Day 152

Sit-ins as a tactic for civil rights protesters began in 1960. African American students staged a sit-in at a segregated lunch counter at a Greensboro, North Carolina, Woolworth store. In an attempt to overthrow the Communist regime of Fidel Castro in Cuba, the United States landed a force of anti-Castro Cubans at the Bay of Pigs in Cuba on April 17, 1961. The invasion was a disaster and the American-backed force was defeated and captured. To provide greater support for the Castro regime after the Bay of Pigs invasion, the Soviet Union began to build sites capable of launching nuclear missiles at the United States. American U-2 surveillance fights discovered these sites. This led to the Cuban Missile Crisis, which lasted from October 16 to October 28, 1962. The United States blockaded Cuba. This raised international tensions and the specter of nuclear war. In the end the United States and Soviet Union reached an agreement—the Soviet Union pulled its missiles out of Cuba and the United States secretly dismantled missile sites in Turkey. The United States pledged to respect the sovereignty of Cuba. President John F. Kennedy was assassinated by Lee Harvey Oswald on November 22, 1963. In early August 1964, an American destroyer was engaged by North Vietnamese torpedo boats in the Gulf of Tonkin. In reporting the incident to Congress, President Lyndon B. Johnson did not reveal that the ship had been on an electronic intelligence mission to monitor North Vietnamese communications. Congress passed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution on August 7, 1964, authorizing the president to “take all necessary steps” to protect American forces and American allies in Southeast Asia. This resolution provided legal support for President Johnson’s intervention in the Vietnam War.

Day 153

Senator Barry Goldwater of Arizona won the Republican nomination for president in 1964. Goldwater was a conservative who rejected the political legacy of the New Deal. He called for a return to limited government and promised a more robust military and diplomatic response to Communist aggression. In the general election Goldwater was heavily defeated by President Lyndon B. Johnson, who worked to characterize Goldwater as a political extremist. Johnson’s landslide helped him push forward his Great Society expansion of government. Though Goldwater lost in 1964, his campaign is seen as laying the foundation for a conservative revival under one of his supporters, Ronald Reagan.

Day 154

In the 1950s the Eisenhower Administration began to put the federal government behind the Civil Rights Movement with its support of the Little Rock Nine and the Civil Rights Acts of 1957 and 1960. The African American struggle for civil rights continued in the 1960s with sit-ins and Freedom Rides on interstate buses. Dr. Martin Luther King, Jr., made a powerful appeal to the American people with his “I Have a Dream” speech during the March on Washington on August 28, 1963. President Lyndon B. Johnson helped pass the important Civil Rights Act of 1964, which outlawed discrimination by employers and in public locations. 1965 saw the passage of the Voting Rights Act, which removed impediments to African American voting. Following these great victories, a cultural movement toward “Black Power” began with some African Americans, inspired in part by the black nationalism of Malcolm X. Riots against unequal conditions in Northern and Western cities became part of the political turmoil of the later 1960s. The 1960s were a time of dramatic change for African Americans.

Day 155

The “Old Left” was associated with loyalty to the Communist Party and an approach to politics that emphasized ideological discipline and a hierarchical organization. The young founders of the “New Left” rejected this approach. They stressed “participatory democracy” and individual control of one’s life. A key document of the New Left was the 1962 Port Huron Statement issued by the Students for a Democratic Society (SDS). This criticized American materialism and called for nonviolent action to spur reform. The SDS became a leading force on many campuses in opposing the Vietnam War. The SDS broke up in the late 1960s, with some of its members joining the radical Weather Underground organization.

Day 156

The proper chronological order is Medicare, Tet Offensive, and Woodstock Music Festival. Medicare was passed in 1965 as part of President Lyndon B. Johnson’s Great Society. Medicare is a federal program that provides medical insurance to Americans aged 65 and older. The Tet Offensive was a massive series of Communist attacks that were launched all across South Vietnam beginning on January 30, 1968. After some initial successes, the Communists were repulsed with heavy losses. Despite this military success, many in the United States began to doubt the Johnson administration’s predictions of imminent victory. President Johnson and many of his advisors also lost confidence in their prospects in Vietnam. The Woodstock Music Festival took place from August 15 to August 18, 1969, in a rural area of upstate New York. Over 400,000 young people gathered to hear 32 acts perform. Among the famous musicians who appeared were Janis Joplin and Jimi Hendrix. The immense gathering at the Woodstock Music Festival became a highpoint for the 1960s counterculture.

Day 157

In this passage, President Lyndon B. Johnson is defending American involvement in Vietnam. At this point, the United States was supporting South Vietnam against an insurgency being waged by Viet Cong guerillas and soldiers from North Vietnam. In March, Johnson had sent a contingent of Marines into Vietnam to protect the American airbase at Danang. Later in the year, Johnson sent more troops and widened their mission into combat operations against the Communists. Based on the experience of World War II, Johnson believed that you could not appease aggressors. He assumed that if you give ground, they will merely attack somewhere else. Hence he believed that the United States had to resist the Communists in South Vietnam to prevent a “domino theory” spread of Communism into other parts of Southeast Asia.

Day 158

The Vietnam Veterans Memorial was designed by a young artist named Maya Lin and was dedicated on November 13, 1982. The design, two long black walls etched with the names of the 58,000 Americans who lost their lives in the Vietnam War, was controversial because some critics thought it was too modest and somber and did not celebrate the achievements of the memorialized servicemen and women. As a compromise, a statuary group created by Frederick Hart depicting three soldiers was placed to the side of the memorial in 1984. In 1993, a statuary group created by Glenna Goodacre depicting three nurses and a wounded soldier was placed near the memorial to honor female veterans of the Vietnam War. Though controversial at first, the Vietnam Veterans memorial has been embraced by most Americans. Family members trace the names of their loved ones at the memorial and leave commemorative mementos and flowers. The contemplative atmosphere of the Vietnam Veterans Memorial, with its mix of monuments, reflects the complex feelings Americans have about the Vietnam War.

Day 159

Andy Warhol was an important countercultural figure in the 1960s. He was a pioneer of “Pop Art,” which employed imagery drawn from popular culture or advertising in artistic works. Warhol created reproductions of Brillo detergent boxes and painted Campbell’s Soup cans and Coca-Cola bottles. He produced silkscreen images of iconic figures such as Marilyn Monroe and Elvis Presley. Warhol branched into filmmaking, and produced a long string of experimental films. At his studio, known as “The Factory,” he hosted parties that attracted many celebrities, as well as a following of countercultural figures whom he featured in his movies. Warhol also sponsored the development of the cult rock band the Velvet Underground.

Day 160

The 1973 Roe v. Wade Supreme Court decision argued that women had a right to an abortion before fetal viability. This set off a long-standing political battle over abortion. President Richard M. Nixon resigned on August 9, 1974. He was in danger of being impeached for obstruction of justice in the Watergate scandal, and his political situation had become untenable. South Vietnam fell to a military offensive by North Vietnam. The South Vietnamese government surrendered on April 30, 1975. President Jimmy Carter brokered a peace deal between Israeli Prime Minister Menachem Begin and Egyptian President Anwar El Sadat at the Camp David presidential retreat. The Camp David Accords were signed on September 17, 1978, and provided a framework for a formal peace treaty between Israel and Egypt. Following his overthrow in 1979, the former Shah of Iran, Mohammad Reza Pahlavi was allowed into the United States for medical treatment. In response, a mob of Iranian students supported by the revolutionary Iranian government of the Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini stormed the American embassy and took the staff hostage on November 4, 1979. A few female and African American hostages were released. The Iranians continued to hold 52 others. An American rescue mission in April 1980 was a failure. The hostage crisis was seen as sign of American weakness. The hostages were not released until January 20, 1981, the date of the inauguration of President Ronald Reagan.

Day 161

President Richard M. Nixon was innovative in his foreign policy initiatives. He launched a new phase of the Vietnam War with his policy of “Vietnamization,” which consisted of turning more of the fighting over to the South Vietnamese while gradually pulling out American troops. Nixon supported the South Vietnamese with training, supplies, and a massive bombing campaign. This eventually led to the signing of the Paris Peace Accords on January 27, 1973, and the end of American fighting in the war. Nixon and his national security advisor, and later secretary of state, Henry Kissinger were practitioners of “realpolitik” or balance of power international relations. They sought to check the threat posed by their leading cold war rivals by improving relations with both, putting pressure on the Soviet Union and China, who disliked each other, to look to the United States for support. They initiated a policy of “détente” with the Soviet Union. At the same time, Nixon and Kissinger reversed decades of American foreign policy by opening diplomatic relations with Communist China. Nixon made an unprecedented trip to China in February 1972.

Day 162

The Richard Nixon Presidential Library and Museum has the challenge of commemorating a president who was forced to resign because of his actions in the Watergate Scandal. The curators of the library meet this challenge by being scrupulously fair about the scandal in their exhibits and forthcoming with documentation about Watergate. President Nixon himself helped mitigate memories of his disgrace and resignation through a long post-presidential career of writing about international relations, restoring his reputation as an expert on foreign policy.

Day 163

President Jimmy Carter was referring to the energy crisis of the 1970s. The world faced shortages of oil as a result of the Arab Oil Embargo in the aftermath of the 1973 Yom Kippur War and the 1979 Iranian Revolution. In 1973 American drivers for a time had to submit to gas rationing. In 1974, Congress passed a law setting the national speed limit at 55 miles per hour. Americans began to purchase fuel-efficient foreign cars, leading to severe problems for the American automobile industry. President Carter tried to institute an ambitious program of energy conservation that met with only limited success.

Day 164

The proper chronological order is Strategic Defense Initiative, Tax Reform Act, and INF Treaty. Under President Ronald Reagan Cold War tensions intensified as he accelerated an arms buildup begun under President Jimmy Carter. Reagan also ramped up rhetorical attacks on the Soviet dictatorship. Reagan believed that the Cold War was not a permanent feature of world affairs and that the United States could prevail in this struggle. The heightening of the Cold War in Reagan’s first term led to fears of a nuclear war. To help allay these fears, Reagan launched the Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI), popularly known as “Star Wars,” a program to provide the United States with defense systems that could shoot down enemy nuclear missiles in space. The Tax Reform Act of 1986 was a continuation of the tax cutting begun in Reagan’s first term. This legislation reduced the top tax rate from 50 percent to 28 percent. By the later 1980s the Soviet leadership realized that the Soviet Union’s economy was crumbling and that they could no longer compete in a Cold War with the United States. This led to diplomatic actions to thaw relations between the superpowers. The 1987 Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty (INF Treaty), signed by President Reagan and Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev, eliminated a whole class of shorter-range nuclear missiles.

Day 165

President Ronald Reagan believed that prosperity came from Americans pursuing their economic goals as freely as possible from government intervention. To encourage this, Reagan attempted to cut the size of government and turn some services back to the states with his plan for a New Federalism. He also cut tax rates and promoted the policy of deregulation in the energy and transportation industries. Though Reagan was not able to achieve all his goals in reducing government, the general prosperity of the 1980s made his ideas the foundation for American conservatives for the next two decades.

Day 166

The late 1960s and early 1970s saw the production of many films that emphasized serious and socially conscious themes such as, Midnight Cowboy (1969) and Chinatown (1974). Steven Spielberg’s Jaws (1975) ushered in a new era of escapist cinematic blockbusters that often emphasized action and special effects. Star Wars (1977) was the most successful of these, inspiring sequels and a merchandizing line that continues to this day. The popularity of these films reflected the political mood in the country, which was drawing back from the political activism of the previous decade and was growing more conservative.

Day 167

The Berlin Wall was broken down in 1989 as the Soviet Union weakened and the people of Eastern Europe began to overthrow their Soviet-imposed Communist governments. Operation Desert Shield was implemented by President George Herbert Walker Bush to protect Saudi Arabia after Iraq invaded and conquered Kuwait in August 1990. The next year, in Operation Desert Storm, the United States and a large coalition of allies drove the Iraqi forces out of Kuwait. Soviet hardliners attempted to overthrow President Mikhail Gorbachev in August 1991. Boris Yeltsin led the resistance to the coup, which collapsed. Yeltsin ended the Soviet Union on December 24, replacing it with the Russian Federation. Bill Clinton was elected president in 1992, taking political advantage of a recession that ended earlier in the year and a desire on the part of the American people to enjoy a “peace dividend” with the conclusion of the Cold War. President Bush negotiated the North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA) in 1992. This agreement ended most tariffs between the United States, Canada, and Mexico. President Clinton successfully pushed for the passage of a congressional bill enabling the implementation of NAFTA in 1993.

Day 168

The end of the Cold War was a watershed event. The American people wanted to enjoy their “peace dividend” and move in new directions. In the election of 1992, President George Herbert Walker Bush was replaced by the much younger William Jefferson (Bill) Clinton. While scholars debated Francis Fukuyama’s thesis on the “end of history,” most Americans enjoyed the prosperity of the 1990s. Comparatively little attention was paid to ominous signs in other parts of the world, especially the Middle East. Arguably the escape from “history” ended with the Al Qaeda attack on the United States on September 11, 2001, which opened up a new era of warfare.

Day 169

George H. W. Bush served bravely during World War II as a naval pilot. At one point, he was the youngest pilot in the service. Bush came of political age during the Cold War, which helped shape his career as he served at ambassador to the United Nations, envoy to China, Director of Central Intelligence, and vice president. As president, Bush played an important diplomatic role in peacefully winding down the Cold War. Once the Cold War was won, and other international crises like the Gulf War were seemingly resolved, Bush appeared distant and out of touch to Americans interested in focusing on the economy and domestic affairs. Bush’s defeat by Bill Clinton was regarded by many as a symbolic moment, with power passing from a World War II veteran to the first baby-boom president.

Day 170

The proper chronological order is Contract with America, Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act, Kosovo War. The 1994 Contract with America was written by Republican congressmen Newt Gingrich and Dick Armey. It was signed by almost all Republican congressional candidates and laid out a detailed platform of objectives, including welfare reform and tax cuts. The Contract with America proved to be an effective and popular program. In the 1994 elections, the Republicans recaptured control of the House of Representatives for the first time since the 1952 election. Gingrich became the Speaker of the House, and Armey, the House Republican Leader. The Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act, better known as the Welfare Reform Act of 1996, put time limits on welfare assistance and added work requirements for those receiving assistance. This bill reflected the increasingly conservative mood in the nation and a sense that earlier welfare policies had not worked well. President Bill Clinton signed the bill as part of his political move to the middle during the election of 1996. President Clinton had ended the 1992–1995 Bosnian War in the former Yugoslavia with a brief bombing campaign near the end. The peace was negotiated at Dayton, Ohio. In 1998, the Federal Republic of Yugoslavia began a war with Albanian rebels in the territory of Kosovo. After reports of government atrocities, the United States led NATO in a bombing campaign against the Federal Republic of Yugoslavia from March to June 1999. The bombing forced Yugoslav forces out of Kosovo; they were replaced by international peacekeepers. The Kosovo War confirmed a popular American belief at the time that wars could be won with few or no casualties through technology and airpower.

Day 171

Because of continuing allegations of corruption about a land deal involving the Whitewater Development Corporation during President Bill Clinton’s time as governor of Arkansas, Ken Starr was appointed as an independent counsel to investigate his involvement. Over time, Starr’s investigation was broadened to include other matters, including the firing of the White House travel staff and allegations that Clinton had sexually harassed an Arkansas employee named Paula Jones. During the investigation of the Paula Jones case, Starr learned that Clinton had carried on an affair with a young White House intern named Monica Lewinsky. Pressed on this, Clinton lied about the matter in public and was evasive in sworn testimony. Starr decided that Clinton had committed perjury and obstructed justice by trying to influence Ms. Lewinsky’s testimony. The Republican-dominated House of Representatives voted to impeach Clinton. After a trial in the Senate, the Senate voted on February 12, 1999; 67 votes, or two thirds, would have been necessary to remove the president from office. On the perjury charge the Senate voted 45 for conviction and 55 against. On obstruction of justice, the vote was 50 to 50. Clinton survived the impeachment crisis, but dealing with the Lewinsky scandal ate up much of his second term. Clinton was later cited for contempt by a federal judge over his testimony in the Paula Jones case and ordered to pay a $90,000 fine. Before leaving office, Clinton accepted a further penalty of being disbarred from legal practice in Arkansas. He paid Paula Jones $850,000 in an out-of-court settlement. These scandals hurt Vice President Al Gore in the 2000 election.

Day 172

Democrats running as traditional liberals supporting an expansion of government programs to meet social problems lost the elections of 1984 and 1988. This led some Democrats to argue that the party needed to move closer to the political center. In 1985 the Democratic Leadership Council (DLC) was founded to promote this approach. Governor Bill Clinton of Arkansas was a member of the DLC and served for a time as its chairman. In 1992, Clinton ran for president as a “New Democrat.” He portrayed himself as more economically conservative than liberal Democrats, being open to middle-class tax cuts and welfare reform. In office, Clinton supported NAFTA. In 1996, Clinton declared that “the era of big government is over,” and worked with Republicans to pass tax cuts and welfare reform.

Day 173

Overall, the decade of the 1990s was prosperous, with the last years of the decade especially so. Americans benefitted from lower taxes, lower energy prices, and the emergence of new industries like the dotcom companies pioneering the developing Internet. Following the recession of 1992, the unemployment rate fell to 4 percent by the end of the decade. The absence of perceived foreign threats helped contribute to an optimistic economic atmosphere. The collapse of the overinflated dotcom boom in 2000 was a harbinger of harder economic times.

Day 174

The United States attacked Afghanistan on October 7, 2001. The United States invaded Afghanistan to overthrow the Taliban government that was sheltering Osama bin Laden and other Al Qaeda figures implicated in the 9/11 attacks on the United States. President George W. Bush created an Office of Homeland Security shortly after the 9/11 attacks. The Department of Homeland Security was formally established by the passage of the Homeland Security Act of 2002. The mission of the new department was to provide greater security against terrorism and other threats. In August 2005, Hurricane Katrina caused extensive damage and the loss of 1,254 lives along the Gulf Coast of the United States. The City of New Orleans was especially hard hit because of the failure of levees. The Bush administration was criticized because of its difficulties in getting relief to the area. The Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP) of 2008 was an effort to bolster banks and other financial institutions following the subprime mortgage crisis. The federal government helped these institutions by purchasing “troubled assets.” The goal of President Bush in signing this legislation was to head off a greater financial crisis that might lead to a depression. He secured the support of both 2008 presidential candidates, Senators John McCain and Barack Obama. The Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act, better known as the Affordable Care Act (ACA) or Obamacare, was signed into law by President Barack Obama. Passed with only Democratic votes, it was an effort to provide health care for more Americans while improving care quality and lowering costs. The law was controversial from its inception and spurred political opposition throughout President Obama’s presidency.

Day 175

The September 11, 2001, terrorist attack by Al Qaeda operatives flying hijacked airliners killed 2,996 people. The attacks destroyed the Twin Towers of the World Trade Center in New York City and damaged the Pentagon. Resistance by the passengers of United Flight 93 led the terrorists flying that plane to crash it near Shanksville, Pennsylvania. The terrorists probably intended to fly that plane into a government building in Washington, D.C. The 9/11 Memorial and the 9/11 Museum are dedicated to bearing “solemn witness” to the terrorist attacks. They commemorate the people who died that day and celebrate the achievements of the first responders who saved many lives. The memorial bears the names of all the victims of 9/11 and has two waterfalls and reflecting pools in the places where the Twin Towers stood. Nearby, as a sign of hope and reconstruction, stands One World Trade Center (Freedom Tower), the replacement for the World Trade Center.

Day 176

In this speech before a Joint Session of Congress, President Bush was arguing for his program of tax cuts. Bush believed that these cuts would stimulate the economy following a recession caused by the collapse of the dotcom boom. Bush secured tax cuts in 2001 and 2003. These cut the highest tax rate to 35 percent. The Bush tax cuts were intended to expire in 2010. That year President Barack Obama and Congress agreed to extend the Bush rates for two years. In 2012, President Obama and Republicans in Congress compromised on the American Taxpayer Relief Act. The Bush tax cuts were made permanent for individuals earning less than $400,000 and couples earning less than $450,000; for individuals and couples earning more than that, the highest tax rate returned to the pre-Bush rate of 39.6 percent. For most Americans, Bush’s tax cuts proved an enduring legacy.

Day 177

Following 9/11, the U.S. government and its allies grew concerned about the Iraqi dictator Saddam Hussein. They held him to be in noncompliance with UN Security Council Resolutions passed since the Gulf War. President George W. Bush, British Prime Minister Tony Blair, and other leaders believed that Saddam Hussein was stockpiling weapons of mass destruction. Saddam Hussein himself contributed to this impression by not cooperating with UN inspectors. On October 16, 2002, large majorities in Congress approved a Joint Resolution giving President Bush authority to use force against Iraq. Operation Iraqi Freedom was launched on March 20, 2003. Saddam Hussein was rapidly overthrown by an invasion of American, British, and allied forces. A bloody insurgency against coalition forces and a new Iraqi government ensued. After years of frustration in combating the insurgency, in 2007 President Bush ordered a surge of 20,000 troops into Iraq. In cooperation with Iraqi forces, the “surge” was militarily successful, suppressing the insurgents and bringing a degree of stability to Iraq. In 2011, President Barack Obama withdrew the last American forces from Iraq, declaring that they were leaving behind a “sovereign, stable, and self-reliant Iraq.” Soon afterward the Iraqi government fell into sectarian conflict. A successor group to the old Al Qaeda insurgency, the Islamic State of Iraq and Syria (ISIS) emerged as a powerful force in Iraq and in neighboring Syria, which was divided by a civil war. Promoting a fundamentalist form of Sunni Islam, ISIS forces captured large amounts of territory in Syria and Iraq in 2014, including the large Iraqi city of Mosul.

Day 178

In May 9, 2012, President Barack Obama publically announced support for gay marriage. Former Massachusetts governor Mitt Romney, his Republican opponent in the 2012 presidential election, opposed gay marriage and supported a proposed constitutional amendment to limit marriage to one man and one woman. Gay marriage was a divisive social issue until the Supreme Court held that same-sex couples had a right to marry in the 2015 Obergefell v. Hodges decision.

Day 179

Immigration and immigration reform were contentious political issues during the Bush and Obama Administrations. Immigration reform legislation failed to pass out of Congress. President Barack Obama was reluctant to deport law-abiding illegal immigrants. He supported the Development, Relief, and Education for Alien Minors Act (Dream Act), which would have provided resident status to illegal immigrants who came to the United States before the age of 16 and who graduated from an American high school and passed a criminal check. This law did not pass Congress. President Obama responded by announcing on June 15, 2012, that his administration would cease deporting people who met the Dream Act criteria.

Day 180

The Supreme Court made a number of important rulings during the Obama years. The 2010 Citizens United v. FEC decision prohibited government limits on political spending by nonprofit corporations, arguing that such limits violated the right to freedom of speech. The 2014 National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius decision upheld most provisions of the Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act (ACA). This legislation, popularly known as “Obamacare,” was President Barack Obama’s most important legislative achievement, and a subject of intense partisan debate between Democrats and Republicans. The 2015 Obergefell v. Hodges decision legalized gay marriage across the United States.
