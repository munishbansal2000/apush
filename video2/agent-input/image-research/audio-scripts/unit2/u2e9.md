Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: one 22-year-old's shot in the rain. It went global. Now London is sending the bill. This time: no new material. Eight episodes, ten questions, 1607 to 1763, in about thirteen and a half minutes. Jay's holding the list. The dare: say every answer out loud before I do. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now.

Jay: Question one. Four empires, one continent. Match the model, one breath each.

[10-second pause]

Maya: Spain: crown-run, souls and silver. Missions to convert, mines to pay, the crown skimming its fifth off the silver, and the casta labels ranking your grandparents.

Jay: Souls paid for by silver.

Maya: France: a fur empire. Beaver pelts for felt hats, Champlain's Quebec in 1608, and the colony stayed thin because a fur economy needed no farmers. The coureurs de bois paddled past the legal trading posts, and the crown barred the Huguenots, so the settlers never came.

Jay: Thin by design, almost.

Maya: Holland: a company colony. 1629: ship fifty settlers across and the company hands you a patroon estate, sixteen miles of riverfront on both banks. No wait — sixteen one side, eight both sides. Rensselaerswijck worked; most of the other lords' ventures never got off the ground. But New Amsterdam did: a jumble of languages on the docks, a Dutch governor who couldn't shut the doors, and an English fleet taking it all in 1664.

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] And England?

Maya: Company money, settler families, farms. The headright paid fifty acres to whoever paid your passage, which meant the planter. And the Native answer, all four at once. Spain folded Native peoples into its labor machine. France allied with the Wendat against the Iroquois. Holland mostly just traded. And England pushed them off the land.

Jay: Question two. Servants to slaves: walk the chain.

[10-second pause]

Maya: Start with the deal: four to seven years of labor for a boat ticket. Finish and collect freedom dues: a little corn, some clothes, maybe land. Then the system worked too well: the servants lived, and wanted land that was already taken.

Jay: And Berkeley crushed the rebellion, right? He won.

Maya: He won the battle and lost the job. Bacon raised four to five hundred men in 1676, attacked even the Pamunkeys, English allies, burned Jamestown in September, and died of dysentery the next month. Berkeley hanged twenty-three of the leaders, and London recalled him anyway. The planters' lesson: never let the poor unite across race again.

Jay: So they rebuilt the workforce on race.

Maya: Piece by piece, and the supply helped: the Dutch monopoly broke in 1682, so enslaved Africans got cheaper just as servants got scarcer. 1640: three runaways caught, two white, one Black. The white men got four more years; John Punch got life. 1662: a child's status followed the mother, so slavery became hereditary. Barbados wrote the template in 1661, Carolina's planters carried it north, and Virginia's 1705 code gathered decades of law into one book: slavery for life, people held as real estate.

Jay: Don't write that 1705 started it.

Maya: The code gathered; it didn't invent. And the historians' fight rides with it: did the racism make the slavery, or the slavery make the racism? Both sides have evidence. The codes are the fact; the motive is the argument.

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] Question three. New England: why'd they come, and who got kicked off the hill?

[10-second pause]

Jay: [sheepish] The Pilgrims and the Puritans, same people, right? Funny hats, Thanksgiving?

Maya: Different group, different decade. Plymouth's Pilgrims, 1620: Separatists, breaking with the Church of England for good. Massachusetts Bay, 1630: Puritans, still trying to purify it from within. More than twenty thousand crossed by 1640, and they came as families. They crossed for their own religion only. And the hill kicked people off it. Roger Williams, banished in 1635 for saying the colony had no right to Native land it hadn't bought. Anne Hutchinson, tried in 1637 for preaching against the ministers, excommunicated and gone by 1638.

Jay: And the towns ran on covenant.

Maya: Church and town fused into one contract with God, the meetinghouse at the center. Then the war: King Philip's War, 1675 to '76. Metacom, Massasoit's son. The spark was an English court hanging three of his men. Seventeen settlements destroyed, fifty more damaged. And say the per-capita part, or the deadliest label is wrong. The book everyone read afterward: Rowlandson's captivity narrative, 1682. Some call it America's first bestseller. Native power in New England never really came back.

Jay: Question four. The middle colonies: the diversity lab.

[10-second pause]

Maya: Penn's Holy Experiment, 1681: a colony paid for a debt, advertised in German, because empty land pays nothing. The Pennsylvania Dutch meant Deutsch, German. Palatine Germans, roughly a third of the colony by the Revolution, and something like half of them arrived as redemptioners: negotiating their servitude on the dock, sick and broke, instead of back in Europe.

Jay: And the Scots-Irish on the frontier.

Maya: Ulster Presbyterians, roughly a fourth of the colony, pushed west onto the frontier. Wheat and corn, the breadbasket, flour sailing south to feed the sugar islands. Franklin's 1751 essay fretted the Germans would Germanize Pennsylvania instead of Pennsylvania Anglicizing them, which tells you how German it had gotten.

Jay: So Quakers, no slaves. That was the brand.

Maya: [firm] The brand, not the books. Penn himself held enslaved people. In 1688 four German Quakers signed the first protest against slavery in the English colonies, and the Yearly Meeting filed it away. Then 1737: Penn's sons turned a day-and-a-half deed into a footrace and walked off with a Rhode Island-sized piece of Lenape land. Pluralist, but not necessarily tolerant.

Jay: Question five. Mercantilism: the theory, the rules, the dodge.

[10-second pause]

Maya: The theory: the world's wealth is a fixed pile, and power is how much of it sits in your vaults. The colonies ship raw materials cheap, buy finished goods dear, and never build the factory. Some historians call that a decent bargain: protected markets, the Navy's guarded sea lanes. Others call it a cage.

Jay: The rules had names and dates.

Maya: 1651, aimed at the Dutch: trade on English ships. 1660: the enumerated list, tobacco leading it, to England only. 1663: European goods pass through an English port first. The list kept growing: rice, then naval stores, and Parliament fenced off the factories: Wool in 1699, Hat in 1732, Iron in 1750.

Jay: And every ship sailed the triangle, all three legs.

Maya: The triangle was the pattern. Few ships sailed all three legs. Plenty of ships ran two legs and never closed the loop. New England rum sailed straight to the Caribbean. The arrows simplified the voyages; the chain didn't lie: distilleries needed Caribbean sugar, sugar plantations needed enslaved labor.

Jay: The dodge worked because nobody enforced the rules.

Maya: Salutary neglect. Walpole's supposed line was "let sleeping dogs lie." Supposed, because nobody's fully sure he said those words. But the neglect was real: understaffed customs, governors looking away, 1721 to 1742. Burke finally gave it the name in 1775, looking back at commerce grown something like twelvefold since 1700. And the poster boy: Hancock's Liberty, seized in 1768 on smuggling charges that were dropped for lack of evidence. Charged; never convicted.

Jay: My grandmother still points at the dotted line on my birthday cards and says "put your John Hancock here."

Maya: She means the signature. The fortune came first, and some of it came through the back door.

Jay: Question six. The Awakening: heart, head, and the split.

[10-second pause]

Jay: [sheepish] Whitefield started the whole thing, right? The fields, the crowds?

Maya: [firm] The trap answer. The fire was smoldering in the 1730s: cold churches, the halfway compromise of 1662. Whitefield poured the gasoline: 1739, mid-twenties, open fields, crowds in the thousands, newspapers claiming twenty-five thousand. Franklin walked backward down Market Street doing the math and computed thirty thousand could hear him.

Jay: And Edwards was the loud one.

Maya: Backwards. Whitefield's the act: the weeping, the crowds. Edwards read his sermon nearly flat, the famous account says barely looking up, while the room came apart. Enfield, July 1741, the spider over the fire: his actual words. His own congregation voted him out in 1750 anyway.

Jay: While Franklin was indoors reasoning.

Maya: The counterweight. Poor Richard's Almanack from 1732, the Junto debating club from 1727, and deism: God wound the clock and stepped back. The kite story's shakier than the textbooks say, but the lightning work was real. Then the split: New Lights for the revival, Old Lights for the old guard, though I always want to flip those, and the organizing taught ordinary colonists to defy authority and survive it.

Jay: Which is why some historians say it rehearsed the Revolution.

Maya: Some say rehearsal; others say the fires burned out decades before 1776. Argue both sides.

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] Question seven. How'd the assemblies get powerful while London looked away?

[10-second pause]

Jay: The purse.

Maya: The purse. They voted the taxes, the budget, and in a lot of colonies the governor's own salary. Massachusetts proved it in the 1720s: Shute demanded a permanent salary, the House voted his pay year by year instead. Burnet tried next; in 1728 they handed him seventeen hundred pounds and still refused the fixed salary.

Jay: And the Zenger verdict fixed press law for good.

Maya: [firm] Trap answer. The law didn't change. Cosby jailed printer Zenger in 1734, after the paper printed his sacking of a judge over a salary dispute. Zenger sat in jail the better part of a year; his wife Anna kept the paper printing. Hamilton argued truth should be the defense; the judge barred it. The jury acquitted anyway. The law on the books stayed ugly; the idea changed.

Jay: And Anglicization means they were becoming American.

Maya: [firm] The reverse. They were growing more British every year. English tea, English newspapers, English wigs, and the more British they felt, the harder they insisted on Englishmen's rights: assemblies, trials, presses. Their Britishness was the weapon.

Jay: Question eight. The war: one shot, one plan, one gamble.

[10-second pause]

Maya: [thoughtful tone] May 1754, Jumonville Glen, before dawn, in the rain. Washington, twenty-two, about forty men around a French camp. Somebody fires; the French commander dies. France called it murder, and the man was an ensign — a junior officer. Washington signed the surrender paper at Fort Necessity in French he couldn't read, and the French word in it was l'assassinat. Assassination. They carried it home like a confession.

Jay: The shot heard 'round the world.

Maya: [thoughtful tone] Different shot. Emerson's poem. Lexington and Concord, 1775. This one started a world war and nobody wrote a poem about it.

Jay: Then the Albany Plan united the colonies.

Maya: [thoughtful tone] The colonies said no first. They weren't disorganized; they were guarding their own power. Then London said no too. Then Braddock: July 1755, nearly fifteen hundred regulars marching on Fort Duquesne, and a smaller French and Native force shot them to pieces from the tree line at the Monongahela. European lines in American woods. Washington rode out with four bullets through his coat: his actual words, from his July 1755 letter home.

Jay: And Pitt's gamble won it.

Maya: [thoughtful tone] 1757. Spend in North America, pin France in Europe: "conquer America in Germany," the line attributed to him. Quebec, September 1759: under an hour, both generals dead. The Treaty of Paris, February 1763: Canada and everything east of the Mississippi to Britain. France kept the sugar islands, Guadeloupe and Martinique. A continent traded for sugar.

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] Question nine. 1763: why did winning break everything?

[10-second pause]

Maya: [thoughtful tone] The bill. An enormous debt. The books won't pin the number, and neither will I. Pontiac's rising the moment the French left: the British had ended the gift diplomacy, and his warriors besieged Detroit while forts fell across the lakes. Hundreds of soldiers, thousands of colonists, more than a year to break it.

Jay: So London punished the colonists with the Proclamation.

Maya: [thoughtful tone] Follow the money, not the revenge. October 1763: no settlement west of the Appalachians, because London wanted the frontier wars off its books. The colonists walked over the line anyway, including Washington, whose bounty land for his Virginia Regiment froze under it. And the century of looking the other way? Over.

Jay: Last one. Question ten: the whole unit, one sentence.

[10-second pause]

Jay: Growth made the colonies too valuable, too diverse, and too used to running themselves to be ruled on London's old terms. When London tried anyway, the arrangement broke.

Maya: [confident tone] The period thesis. Keep it. Eight episodes, ten questions. Land them in one breath. Four empires, four hungers. Spain folded Native peoples into its labor system. France enlisted them as allies. The Dutch traded with them. England pushed them off the land. Servants to slaves: the rebellion taught the planters to divide by race, and the 1705 codes: did they start it, or gather it? Gathered. The codes gathered decades of law. Covenant towns that banished their dissenters, then broke in King Philip's War. Penn's experiment: many peoples, unequal footing. Mercantilism's fixed pile, the Acts, the dodge, the neglect. The Awakening's heart and Franklin's head. They fought with the purse and the press, and their Britishness was the argument. One shot in the rain started it. Pitt's gamble won it. The bill broke the old arrangement.

Maya: Two predictions I'd bet on. One: evaluate the extent to which the British colonies had developed a distinct identity and self-government by 1754. Your turn.

[10-second pause]

Maya: Extensive self-government, thin distinct identity. The assemblies held the purse, a colonial jury acquitted Zenger, and decades of salutary neglect trained the colonies to rule themselves, yet Anglicization had them drinking English tea and demanding Englishmen's rights. The complexity point: the machinery was American, the loyalty was British, and the war starting in 1754 would test which one held.

Maya: Two: evaluate the extent to which the shift from indentured servitude to enslaved labor transformed the Chesapeake between 1660 and 1700. Your turn.

[10-second pause]

Maya: The labor force transformed; the engine didn't. Term contracts became lifetime, hereditary, race-coded slavery, written down from John Punch in 1640 to Virginia's 1705 code. But tobacco still demanded land and workers, and the planters' hunger never changed. The transformation was legal and racial. The economy barely changed.

Jay: Worth a fortune and running themselves.

Maya: [thoughtful tone] London's old terms couldn't hold them.

Jay: [intrigued] Next time: one region, up close — how the Chesapeake turned tobacco into a society.
