Marcus: In 1877: a wounded country coming off a civil war. A century later, the richest, most powerful nation in human history — and deeply divided. How you get from one to the other is the second story, and like the first, it runs on a single thread.

Marcus: The thread is power. Industrial power, military power, federal power. America spent a hundred years accumulating more of it than any nation in history, and every chapter since has asked the same question: what is all this power for? Watch for the pattern, because it never stops: each era's solution becomes the next era's problem.

Marcus: It starts with the machine age. In 1869, a golden spike at Promontory, Utah joined the first transcontinental railroad, and the continent became one market. Steel followed: by 1890, America made more of it than Britain, and then came oil, electricity, and the corporation itself. Carnegie and Rockefeller built fortunes the country needed new words for. The United States became the world's industrial colossus.

Marcus: But a colossus casts a shadow. The wealth was built by workers on twelve-hour days, children in mines and mills, farmers crushed between railroad rates and falling crop prices. At Homestead in 1892, the company brought Pinkerton detectives and the state brought the militia. Three hundred Pinkertons arrived by river barge. The Gilded Age glittered on top. Underneath, Mark Twain's joke held: gilded, not golden.

Marcus: The shadow fell on some Americans harder than others. The new immigrants, Southern and Eastern Europeans crowding into tenements, met nativism and outright Chinese exclusion in 1882. Black Southerners, abandoned at Reconstruction's end, met Jim Crow: poll taxes, literacy tests, and in 1896, the Supreme Court's blessing in Plessy v. Ferguson. Native peoples, confined to reservations, met the army's last campaigns, ending at Wounded Knee in 1890. Growth had a price, and the same people kept paying it.

Marcus: Then the giant started looking outward. In 1893, Frederick Jackson Turner announced the frontier was closed. And America went looking for new ones. The Spanish-American War of 1898 lasted four months. It left America holding the Philippines, Guam, and Puerto Rico: an empire, whether it used the word or not. At home, the Progressives tried to fix what the Gilded Age broke: trust-busting, food safety, the direct election of senators, and finally, in 1920, the vote for women.

Marcus: The world wars made America a world power, and each one grew the government. The First World War pulled America into the war in 1917, after German submarines and the Zimmermann Telegram ended neutrality. Wilson promised a peace built on self-determination, but the Senate refused the League of Nations, and America retreated. The 1920s roared — and in October of 1929, the market collapsed.

Marcus: The Great Depression broke the old bargain. With a quarter of the workforce out of work and banks failing by the thousand, Hoover's voluntarism wasn't enough. And in 1932, the country elected Franklin Roosevelt. The New Deal was the biggest peacetime expansion of federal power in American history. Jobs programs, Social Security, the promise that old age wouldn't mean poverty. The Depression really ended with the Second World War's mobilization, most historians say. The New Deal changed what Americans expected of Washington for good.

Marcus: The Second World War finished the transformation. Pearl Harbor, December seventh, 1941, ended isolationism in one morning. Sixteen million Americans served. The home front rationed and built. It also relocated, not always justly: Japanese American internment remains the starkest wartime violation of civil liberties. In August of 1945, two atomic bombs ended the war with Japan, and America stood alone at the top of the world: half the planet's manufacturing, the only atomic arsenal, and one question: what now?

Marcus: The answer was containment. The Soviet Union, America's wartime ally, became its rival. George Kennan's long telegram gave Washington its doctrine: stop communism from spreading, everywhere, by every means short of open war. The Truman Doctrine, the Marshall Plan, and the North Atlantic Treaty Organization in 1949: each one a link in a chain. It would shape every foreign decision for forty years. Historians still argue about whether containment worked, or whether it trapped America in wars it couldn't win. Hold that argument. You'll need it.

Marcus: At home, containment's twin was prosperity. The G.I. Bill sent veterans to college and new houses. The baby boom filled them. Eisenhower's highways paved the suburbs, and television taught the country the same songs. It was the richest middle class in history, and for the most part a white one, because redlining and restrictive covenants kept Black families out of the new suburbs. Conformity had comforts, and rebels: rock and roll, the beats, and a movement that refused to wait.

Marcus: The Civil Rights Movement is the second story's moral center. Brown v. Board in 1954. The Montgomery bus boycott in 1955. Students at lunch counters, marchers on a bridge in Selma, and in 1963, Martin Luther King Jr. telling two hundred thousand at the Lincoln Memorial about his dream. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 finally collected on Reconstruction's hundred-year-old promise. King's line about the arc of the moral universe bending toward justice wasn't a victory speech. It was a promissory note, and America was finally paying it.

Marcus: But every solution was becoming the next problem. Containment led to Vietnam: advisers became battalions, the Gulf of Tonkin became a blank check, and by 1968 the Tet Offensive was on television every night, proving the government hadn't told the truth. Then came 1968 itself: King assassinated in April, Robert Kennedy in June, Chicago in August, and the consensus cracked wide open.

Marcus: The crack-up kept going. Watergate: a 1972 break-in at Democratic headquarters, a cover-up that reached the Oval Office, and in August of 1974, Richard Nixon resigning on television rather than face removal. Trust in government nearly bled out. Then the economy broke too: oil embargoes, gas lines, inflation and stagnation at once: stagflation, a word economists had called impossible. By the end of the seventies, the country was asking whether the giant was finished.

Marcus: The answer came as a reversal. In 1980, Ronald Reagan won by turning the New Deal's logic inside out: government is not the solution to our problem, government is the problem. Tax cuts, deregulation, and a military buildup, backed by evangelicals and free-marketers who reshaped both parties. The economy recovered. The Cold War ended: the Berlin Wall fell in November of 1989, the Soviet Union dissolved two years later. Historians argue whether his policies caused the recovery, the Cold War's end, or the deficits. But the era's real legacy was the argument itself, and the country has been fighting about the size of government ever since.

Marcus: The new century opened with the argument still going, and added new shocks. September eleventh, 2001: hijacked planes, nearly three thousand dead. And a war on terror that stretched from Afghanistan to Iraq, justified by weapons that were never found. At home, the Patriot Act expanded surveillance. The old tension, safety versus liberty, came back in new clothes. In 2008, the financial system nearly collapsed, and the government stepped in to catch it again: the New Deal's echo, seventy-five years on.

Marcus: And the argument has a new temperature now. The country that fought about the New Deal and the Great Society now fights through screens, sorted into camps that barely share a set of facts. Barack Obama's election in 2008, the first Black president, felt to many like payment on that old promissory note, and to others like proof the country they knew was gone. Both reactions were about the same thing: who gets to say what America is.

Marcus: So here is the second story, whole. A wounded country built the greatest industrial machine in history, and the machine crushed whoever stood in its way. It stepped onto the world stage, fought two world wars, and bought power at a permanent price. It contained communism for forty years and built a middle class the world envied, then watched the consensus shatter in Vietnam and Watergate. It answered with a conservative reversal, won the Cold War, and walked into a century of terror, crisis, and unbroken polarization.

Marcus: The first story asked who gets to be free. The second asks what all this power is for. And here is the thing the exam can't ask you, because it isn't history yet: the story doesn't end. Every generation in this course inherited a solution gone stale, and answered with one their children would fight about. That chain runs straight through you. You're not at the end of the American story.

Marcus: You're the next chapter's first line.
