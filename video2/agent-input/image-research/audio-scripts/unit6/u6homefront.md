# APUSH Unit 6, Lesson: The WWII Home Front
# Episode: APUSH Unit 6, Lesson: The WWII Home Front. ~11 min experienced (1795 words speech + 60s pauses).

# Pronunciation: Korematsu (koh-reh-MAH-tsoo); bracero (brah-SEH-roh); Nisei (nee-SAY); Issei (iss-SAY); Randolph (RAN-dulf).


Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: the war abroad, Midway, Normandy, island-hopping across the Pacific. Today the war comes home, because the home front was its own battlefield: a fight over who did the work — and who the government locked up. Four boxes on your sheet. One: mobilizing the economy, the War Production Board and the Office of Price Administration. Two: women in the wartime workforce, the Rosie the Riveter story. Three: civil liberties under pressure, Executive Order 9066 and Korematsu. Four: labor and migration, the bracero program and the wartime Great Migration. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. About eleven minutes.

Marcus: [measured] Start with the machine, because everything else on the home front runs on it. In 1942 Roosevelt created the War Production Board to oversee the conversion from civilian industry to war production. He had promised, back in 1940, to make America the arsenal of democracy. The Board was the promise turned into orders.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And the Depression just ended? Like that?

Marcus: premium2027's phrase is "almost overnight": persistent unemployment ended. With millions of men and women in uniform, the problem flipped from too few jobs to too few hands.

Maya: So rationing is everybody tightening their belts together? Shared sacrifice?

Marcus: Poster version. Underneath it was an allocation system: starting in 1942 the Office of Price Administration rationed key goods: gasoline and tires first, then sugar, meat, coffee, lard, butter. Families used ration books and stamps. Its real job was economic: rationing scarce goods so the military got what it needed.

Maya: [warm tone] My grandma kept an old ration book in her kitchen drawer. Little paper squares for sugar and coffee. I always thought they were play money.

Marcus: [speaking slowly] That drawer was the OPA, up close. And the money side: the government paid for the war with war bonds and higher taxes, and the national debt grew six times over between 1940 and 1949. Massive spending, massive debt. The war years show how spending like that can jump-start a stalled economy.

Marcus: Even the kids got drafted into the effort, in a way. Children organized Tin Can Clubs to collect scrap metal, melted down for weapons and ammunition production.

Maya: Okay, so the whole country is on a war footing now.

Marcus: Test-day habit: on a stimulus showing ration posters, the tempting answer is the warm one, shared sacrifice. The exam is really testing whether you see what's underneath the posters. Pick that.

Maya: One down: mobilizing the economy. Now the hands.

Marcus: [measured] Factories ran around the clock with most of the men gone, so the government ran a full campaign to recruit women, with posters through the Office of War Information showing women in industrial jobs.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Wait, Rosie was one real woman, right? An actual riveter they put on the posters?

Marcus: [speaking slowly] Rosie was the fictional face of it, a character invented for the posters. And the campaign had to fight the culture: in the thirties, women were told to leave the job market for male breadwinners. By 1945 a third of the entire workforce was female.

Maya: A third. So all those women kept the factory jobs after the war?

Marcus: [serious tone] Careful, that's the trap. After the war, women were expected to go back to more traditional roles. The swing was real and enormous, but for most women it wasn't permanent.

Maya: So the real story is the swing: pushed out in the thirties, pulled in for the war, pushed back out after. The hinge was the emergency.

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] Sequencing trick for test day: keep the three eras in order, thirties, war years, postwar. Rosie belongs to the middle one. Any answer that stretches her into the fifties is lying.

Marcus: And not all of them were in factories. More than 150,000 women joined the Women's Army Corps and the Navy's WAVES, plus nurses in large numbers. The military opened up too.

Maya: Your turn. December 1941: Pearl Harbor, and the West Coast is terrified of spies and saboteurs. Roosevelt has the army and an executive order pen. Reason it through: who does the panic point at, and what does the order do to them?
[10-second pause]

Marcus: [serious tone] In 1942 came Executive Order 9066. It authorized removing more than 100,000 Japanese Americans from the West Coast to camps in more than a dozen western states.

Maya: [confident tone] Wait, I actually know this one: it only covered the non-citizens, the Issei who had emigrated from Japan. You couldn't do that to citizens.

Marcus: [firm] You could, and it did. The order covered Issei and Nisei alike. Nisei were native-born American citizens. Most internees lost their property in the rush. And in 1944 the Supreme Court upheld the relocation in Korematsu v. United States, calling it acceptable on national security grounds.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Did anyone inside the government say no?

Marcus: [speaking slowly] The verdict that counts came later: in 1988 the government apologized and paid survivors twenty thousand dollars each. The law said it plainly: race prejudice, war hysteria, and a failure of political leadership — the government's own verdict, decades later.

Marcus: And here is the cruelest irony of the box. Japanese Americans served anyway. The 442nd Regiment, recruited mostly from Japanese Americans in Hawaii, became the most highly decorated American unit of the war.

Maya: Meanwhile the same war was opening a different fight over race, at home and in uniform.

Marcus: Black newspapers promoted the Double V campaign — the defeat of Nazism and Japanese imperialism abroad and racial discrimination at home. The NAACP pushed it too. Over a million Black Americans served in the military, in segregated units like the Tuskegee Airmen. The bet was that fighting for democracy overseas would buy it at home.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Hold on — zoot suits? That sounds like a fashion story. What's it doing in a civil rights box?

Marcus: [speaking slowly] Los Angeles, June 1943. White servicemen and teenagers attacked Mexican American and Black kids wearing zoot suits, wide flashy outfits that were the style then. Over 150 people injured, over 500 arrested. Wartime migration brought opportunity and resentment in the same truck.

Marcus: [firm] Korematsu questions test the verb. The Court upheld the relocation. Any answer choice saying the Court struck the order down is a lie the distractors love. Check the verb first.

Maya: Your turn. Millions of men in uniform, factories desperate for hands — and plenty of plants won't hire Black workers. You're running the labor effort in 1942. Where do the workers come from, inside the country and outside it?
[10-second pause]

Marcus: [speaking slowly] From outside first: the bracero program, 1942, temporary contract workers brought in from Mexico. More than 200,000 came. The Mexican government made Washington promise the workers wouldn't be drafted.

Maya: Temporary contract workers, so not immigrants, not citizens. Guests with paperwork.

Marcus: Classic trap: it was a wartime labor program, not an immigration policy. Inside the country, the Great Migration that started in World War I kept going, Black families moving to northern factories and now to the West Coast too. And in 1941, A. Philip Randolph of the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters threatened a march on Washington over discrimination in war plants. Roosevelt answered with Executive Order 8802, banning discrimination in war industries, and Randolph called the march off.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So no strikes at all during the war? Total labor peace?

Marcus: Almost. To keep production running, the unions pledged not to strike for the duration, and the pledge held, except in the coal mines, which struck anyway. One exception that hides in a list of absolutes.

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] Two executive orders, two directions: 8802 in 1941 opened war jobs to Black workers; 9066 in 1942 removed Japanese Americans from the West Coast. A question pairing them is testing whether you can hold both directions at once.

Maya: Four boxes, let's land them. One: mobilizing the economy. The War Production Board turned factories to war production in 1942, and the OPA rationed goods through ration books and stamps.

Maya: [confident tone] Checking that one.

Maya: Two: women in the wartime workforce. Rosie the Riveter was a poster campaign, fictional face, real result: a third of the workforce was female by 1945. Then most were pushed back out after the war. And the WAC and the WAVES put over 150,000 women in uniform.

Maya: Two down.

Maya: [sheepish] Three: civil liberties under pressure. The good order was 9066, and 8802 was the relocation one. Internment upheld in Korematsu in 1944, apologized for in 1988.

Marcus: [firm] Swapped. 8802 opened war jobs to Black workers in 1941; 9066 removed Japanese Americans from the West Coast in 1942. The rest of your box is right.

Maya: Three down, with help. The 442nd, the Double V, and the zoot-suit riots live in this box too.

Maya: Four: labor and migration. The bracero program brought temporary contract workers from Mexico in 1942; the Great Migration kept rolling to the North and the West Coast; unions pledged no strikes, except the coal mines.

Maya: And that's four.

Maya: [incredulous] Wait, the Court upheld internment and then the same government paid out twenty thousand dollars a person? How does that not break the Court's credibility?

Marcus: It dented it badly. And the Court itself backed away: in 2018 the Chief Justice said Korematsu "has no place in law under the Constitution." The 1988 law had already called it what it was. Courts get wartime wrong sometimes, and the apology is the country admitting it.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Ten seconds each. Say your answer before I give mine. First, a source — two verdicts, actually. The Court in 1944, paraphrased: the relocation was acceptable on national security grounds. The 1988 law, quoted: "race prejudice, war hysteria, and a failure of political leadership." What do the two verdicts tell you about the decision over time?
[10-second pause]

Maya: The reputation flipped. In 1944 the Court deferred to the military during wartime, that's why it upheld the order. In 1988 the government judged the same facts without the panic and found prejudice instead of necessity. The facts stayed the same. What changed was the distance.

Maya: Second. An SAQ asks how the Double V campaign connected the war abroad to change at home. What goes in the answer?
[10-second pause]

Maya: The link is the second V. Black Americans fought fascism overseas, over a million served in segregated units, and used that service to demand victory over racism at home. The campaign shows wartime participation feeding the civil rights push that followed.

Maya: Third. An MCQ asks why the government created the Office of Price Administration. The tempting answer is patriotic sacrifice. Why is it wrong?
[10-second pause]

Maya: Because the OPA's real job was economic: rationing scarce goods so the military got what it needed. Sacrifice was the poster. The mechanism was allocation.

Maya: One more, fast. Executive Order 8802 versus 9066, one sentence each.
[10-second pause]

Maya: 8802 in 1941 banned discrimination in war industries. 9066 in 1942 removed Japanese Americans from the West Coast. One order opened doors; the other locked people up.

Maya: The home front out-produced everyone on earth, then spent decades answering for who paid the price.

Maya: [intrigued] Next time: the decision over the atomic bomb.

Maya: [confident tone] Check your boxes.
