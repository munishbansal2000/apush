Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: the troops left, and the South undid Reconstruction without repealing a single amendment: Jim Crow, the New South, and the fight over how to fight back. This time: no new material. Eleven lessons, eleven questions, 1844 to 1877, in about ten minutes. The house divides, the war, the rebuilding. I'm firing the questions. You answer out loud before Jay does. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] One. August 1846: a congressman tacks a rider onto a war-funding bill, no slavery in the land taken from Mexico. The bill never becomes law. Why does the exam care?

[10-second pause]

Jay: Because it would have freed the enslaved people out west?

Maya: Classic error, and the exam sets it. The Proviso freed nobody. The Mexican cession had no plantation slavery yet. It was about the future: could slavery GROW? It passed the House again and again, two or three or four times depending on the book, and died in the Senate every time, and forced every politician into a sectional camp. Underneath it, Senate math: about fifteen each, with California about to tip the balance. Both sides read the West as survival.

Maya: And the slogan the old review got wrong: "Fifty-four forty or fight" was shouted AT Polk by Northern congressmen. Never his campaign slogan. Polk settled Oregon at the 49th parallel instead.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Two. California demands in as a free state, and Clay bundles five measures into one bill. Name the five.

[10-second pause]

Jay: California in free. New Mexico and Utah decide later. Texas takes ten million for its boundary claims. The slave trade banned in D.C. And the Fugitive Slave Act.

Maya: Five for five. The traps: the Missouri line never covered the Mexican Cession, Louisiana Purchase land only, and south of the line, slavery was permitted, never guaranteed. The old summary that drew the line to the Pacific was just wrong. Second: the D.C. part banned the slave TRADE; slavery itself stayed legal there.

Jay: Hold on. Clay's name is on the thing, and he did none of the work?

Maya: Third: Clay's name is on it, but Douglas did the parliamentary work. Clay's omnibus died; Douglas unbundled it into five separate bills and passed them. The Fugitive Slave Act was the poison pill: commissioners paid ten dollars to send someone south, five to free them, no jury, no testimony. And the deal bought about four years.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Three. Kansas-Nebraska said let the settlers vote on slavery. What did Kansas prove?

[10-second pause]

Jay: That the vote didn't settle anything. Missourians flooded across the border and stole the election.

Maya: Fifteen hundred legal voters, more than six thousand votes cast. Border Ruffians crossing over, voting, and going home. Two governments: Topeka free, Lecompton pro-slavery, and Pierce recognized Lecompton. Lawrence gets sacked; Brown answers at Pottawatomie, five men killed with swords — the old version said broadswords, and that word is out. The death toll is disputed, so never pin a number.

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] And the Republicans — they were the abolitionist party, right?

Maya: Wrong, and the exam punishes it. Born 1854 on one plank: keep slavery out of the territories. Abolition wasn't the platform. Same year, the caning: Sumner mocked Butler on the Senate floor, Brooks beat him bloody at his desk, and the country split into two opposite verdicts. The Know-Nothings flared and died on the same split.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Four. Dred Scott. Taney's three holdings, one breath each.

[10-second pause]

Jay: No Black citizenship, so no right to sue. Congress can't ban slavery in the territories. The Missouri Compromise is unconstitutional.

Maya: [firm] Clean. The North heard Slave Power proof, the South heard vindication, and the middle died. Two traps: Scott won a round in 1850 and lost every round after. Never write that he won his freedom in court. And never write that the South demanded a federal slave code — the old version said it, but it can't be verified, so it doesn't get taught.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Five. 1858, Illinois. Lincoln springs a trap at Freeport. What was the question, and how did Douglas dodge it?

[10-second pause]

Jay: Lincoln asked how popular sovereignty survives Dred Scott, and Douglas said the settlers could still vote slavery out, and that won him the presidency?

Maya: The question's right; the prize is the trap. If the Court says nobody can ban slavery in a territory, how does your "let the settlers vote" still work? Douglas's dodge — the historians' label is the Freeport Doctrine — settlers just never pass the laws slavery needs, so it withers. It saved him in Illinois and poisoned him with the South. And the office was the Senate, 1858. The legislature sent him back, 54 to 46. Douglas kept the seat, and the debates put Lincoln on the national map.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Six. A seceding state declares: "Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery…" What's the point of this source?

[10-second pause]

Jay: Mississippi, saying it straight. Secession was about slavery.

Maya: Mississippi's own declaration. When the exam asks about causes, the seceders put slavery first. Don't answer "states' rights" and stop. I wrote "states' rights" on a quiz in eighth grade, and my teacher circled the whole thing in red.

Jay: Deserved.

Maya: The math that moved them: Lincoln won about forty percent of the popular vote and 180 electoral votes. Douglas finished second in votes and dead last in electoral votes: twelve. Forty percent won on the map, and the map decided it. But the headcount shaped the secession narrative. The South read the trend: eighteen free states to fifteen slave, and every new western state coming in free. Containment looked like a cage.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Seven. July 1863: Gettysburg and Vicksburg, same week. One line on what each one did.

[10-second pause]

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] Gettysburg stopped Lee. Vicksburg took the Mississippi. So the war was basically over?

Maya: Two more years of it. Gettysburg ended Lee's invasions of the North; Vicksburg split the Confederacy in half. And the complication the exam rewards: Antietam, a year earlier, the bloodiest single day, about twenty-three thousand casualties, tactically a draw. Never call it a Union win flat. It was the opening Lincoln could claim, and he used it for the preliminary Emancipation Proclamation. Then Grant's attrition and Sherman's hard war finished it: Atlanta falls, Lincoln wins reelection, Appomattox. The whole war cost something like seven hundred thousand lives, and historians are still fighting over that number.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Eight. The Proclamation freed people where Lincoln had no power and nobody where he did. So what did it actually change?

[10-second pause]

Jay: It made the war about slavery. Killed any chance Europe would recognize the Confederacy. And it put Black soldiers in uniform.

Maya: The full scoreboard. Preliminary September 1862, five days after Antietam; final January first, 1863. The border states were exempt, the classic scope trap. And the number the old version got wrong: about a hundred and eighty thousand Black men served. Two hundred thousand is the wrong number. The 54th Massachusetts led the assault on Fort Wagner. The legal finish came with the 13th Amendment.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Nine. Three plans for bringing the South back: Lincoln's ten percent, Wade-Davis, Johnson's. Which plan won?

[10-second pause]

Jay: Wade-Davis wanted fifty-one percent of voters to swear loyalty, and Lincoln pocket-vetoed it. Then Johnson —

Maya: Stop at the number. Wade-Davis wanted a bare majority. Never pin fifty-one. Lincoln pocket-vetoed it. Johnson pardoned almost everyone, kept the leniency, and charged the 13th Amendment as the price of readmission. The South answered with the Black Codes; Congress refused to seat the Southern delegations and took over — five military districts, the 14th in 1868, the 15th in 1870. Congress's plan won, but only where federal power stood.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Ten. Was Reconstruction a success or a failure?

[10-second pause]

Jay: Both? The amendments survived, but everything else got taken back.

Maya: Both. With evidence, or it's a coin flip. Success: the 13th, 14th, and 15th never left the Constitution, and the civil rights movement argued from them a century later. And the officeholder number the old version inflated: about six hundred thirty Black state legislators, sixteen in the House, one senator. Not two thousand. Failure: sharecropping's debt trap, the Court narrowing the 14th in Slaughterhouse and Cruikshank, and the Compromise of 1877 trading the troops for the presidency. The amendments stayed in the Constitution. The power to make them real was gone by 1877.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Eleven. The 15th Amendment stayed on the books. How did the South kill the Black vote without touching it?

[10-second pause]

Jay: The machinery. Poll taxes, literacy tests, grandfather clauses, whites-only primaries: race-neutral on paper, enforced against Black voters only.

Maya: The whole machine. Then Plessy in 1896 blessed separate but equal, with Harlan's color-blind dissent, and it held nearly sixty years until Brown. The New South industrialized on top: mills, textiles, real cities. But cotton stayed the base underneath, and the financing came from the north. And the fight over how to fight: Washington said economics first, Wells and Du Bois said confront now. Brutal decades for Black freedom — the books don't use the word "nadir," so neither do we.

Maya: [confident tone] Eleven questions, one sentence underneath. Check your boxes. The slavery question broke every compromise, broke the nation. And the peace failed to secure what the war had won.

Jay: Compromises broken, nation broken — and a peace that gave back what the war won.

Maya: Test it. The Wilmot Proviso never became law, and it still broke the parties apart. Kansas voted on slavery and got a shooting war instead. Gettysburg and Vicksburg broke the Confederacy in the field, and 1877 traded the peace for a presidency. The sentence holds.

Maya: Two bets I'd take. First: evaluate the extent to which Reconstruction was a success or a failure. Your turn.

[10-second pause]

Maya: The thesis: constitutional success, political and economic failure. One: the amendments. The 13th, 14th, and 15th never left, and the civil rights movement argued from them a century later. Two: the failures. Sharecropping kept freed people landless, the Court narrowed the 14th, and 1877 traded the project for a presidency. Both sides, evidence on each. Name the success, name the failure, and put the evidence behind each one. That's what the points reward.

Maya: Second: was slavery the central cause of the Civil War? Your turn.

[10-second pause]

Maya: Yes. Prove it with the seceders' own words. One: every crisis from the Mexican Cession to Kansas to secession was a fight over slavery's expansion. Two: the South said so itself, in Mississippi's declaration and the Cornerstone Speech. Three: the both-sided line the exam rewards. Tariffs and states' rights rode along, but nothing else explains why the nation split in 1860 and not before. Slavery was the load-bearing cause.

Maya: [confident tone] Eleven lessons, one cram, nothing dropped. Say the thesis once more.

Jay: [confident tone] Broke the compromises, broke the country. And the peace gave back what the war won.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Sleep. Water. Go get the five. And say the thesis in your sleep.
