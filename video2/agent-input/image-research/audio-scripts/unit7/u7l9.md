# Pronunciation: Ngo Dinh Diem (noh din zee-EM), Ho Chi Minh (hoh chee MIN), Viet Cong (vee-et KONG), Tet, Saigon (sy-GON), Dien Bien Phu (dyen byen FOO), Cronkite (KRON-kite), Pleiku (play-KOO)

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: the war that ate the dream. Vietnam — the quagmire that broke the generation it drafted. Four boxes: the Domino Theory, the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, the Tet Offensive, and Vietnamization. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. About eleven minutes, pauses and all.

Marcus: Start with why Washington cared about a small country most Americans couldn't find on a map. After World War Two, America runs a policy called containment: box communism in wherever it appears. France is still holding Vietnam as a colony, and the Vietnamese are fighting for independence under a communist leader named Ho Chi Minh.

Maya: So a colony fighting for freedom, and America backs the colonizer?

Marcus: That's the knot. America bankrolled France's war there — paid for over eighty percent of it, because stopping communism mattered more than supporting independence. Then in nineteen fifty-four the French lose everything at Dien Bien Phu and walk away.

Maya: So containment just packs its bags and moves to Asia.

Marcus: Exactly. The Geneva Accords split Vietnam at the seventeenth parallel: communist North under Ho, Western-backed South. Reunification elections were promised within two years. They never happened. Washington was sure Ho would win, so America backed a Southern leader, Ngo Dinh Diem, who refused to hold them.

Maya: [incredulous] The democracy side canceled the election?

Marcus: [firm] It did. And Diem turned out brutal: jailing opponents, persecuting Buddhists. By nineteen sixty-three the Kennedy administration quietly approved a generals' coup, and Diem was killed. Three weeks later Kennedy himself was assassinated, and Lyndon Johnson inherited the whole mess.

Maya: [sheepish] And the idea holding all this together is the domino thing. That was about communism invading from the outside, right? One country falls and the neighbors get pushed?

Marcus: [firm] The outside-only picture is the theory's hidden assumption, and the exam will test whether you spot it. Eisenhower laid it out in nineteen fifty-four: if Vietnam goes communist, the neighbors topple one by one. The theory only ever imagines communism arriving from the outside. It never admits the Vietnamese might simply want every foreigner gone.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Quick counterfactual: Kennedy lives. Does he send the half-million anyway?

Marcus: Nobody's fully sure. Historians still fight about that one. What we know is Johnson leaned in.

Maya: Okay, so how does Johnson go from inheriting a mess to owning a war?

Marcus: It started in August, nineteen sixty-four. Johnson announces North Vietnamese gunboats attacked American destroyers in the Gulf of Tonkin. The first clash was real but minor. The second attack, the one he took to Congress, almost certainly never happened.

Maya: [incredulous] He went to Congress with an attack that didn't happen?

Marcus: [serious tone] And Congress handed him the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution: a near-blank check to wage war across Southeast Asia, with no declaration of war ever voted. That's the box. The resolution mattered more than the incident.

Maya: [sheepish] So that was Congress basically declaring war, then?

Marcus: [firm] No declaration of war was ever voted, and that's the trap. The resolution handed the president a blank check to wage war across Southeast Asia without one. Watch the power shift. The incident was the fog.

Maya: Your turn. The evidence is shaky, Congress is cheering, and there's an election in three months. Escalate, or walk away?

[10-second pause]

Marcus: Johnson escalated. In early nineteen sixty-five the bombing of the North begins, after an attack on the American base at Pleiku. The troop count goes vertical: a hundred thousand within months, nearly five hundred thousand by the end of nineteen sixty-eight. To fill those boots, Johnson leaned on the draft: in nineteen sixty-four the Selective Service starts pulling more young men, and within a year the monthly draft calls have doubled.

Maya: Two boxes down and two to go, so America's all in. Why couldn't the strongest military on earth win?

Marcus: It was the wrong kind of war. The Viet Cong were southern guerrillas: no uniforms and no front lines, supplied down the Ho Chi Minh Trail. They'd strike and melt into the villages. It was an unconventional war against a mostly invisible enemy, with no traditional measures of victory. And you can't bomb an enemy you can't see without burning the village around him.

Maya: [playful] Full disclosure: everything I know about this war is movie night: Apocalypse Now, Forrest Gump, the works, and every one of them is about guys who come home broken.

Marcus: Then you already know the shape of the ending. The date was January thirtieth, nineteen sixty-eight: the Tet Offensive. During a holiday truce, communist forces attacked all across South Vietnam at once. Fighters even breached the walls of the American embassy in Saigon.

Maya: [sheepish] So that's the battle that broke the American army —

Marcus: [firm] That wrong answer is a classic, and it's worth a full box. Militarily, Tet was a crushing American and South Vietnamese win: over forty thousand communist dead, against about sixteen hundred Americans. But the generals had spent years promising victory was just around the corner, and now the enemy was inside the embassy compound. It was the win that looked like a loss.

Maya: The cameras were there.

Marcus: [serious tone] It was the living-room war. Television was in nearly every American living room by nineteen sixty, and Vietnam was the first American war it ever carried. A month after Tet, Walter Cronkite, the most trusted newsman in the country, looked into the camera and said the war was [emphasis]mired in stalemate. A month after that, Johnson announced he wouldn't seek another term.

Marcus: The gap between the briefings and the broadcasts got a name: the credibility gap. In nineteen seventy-one the Pentagon Papers leaked: a secret Defense Department study, leaked by an analyst named Daniel Ellsberg, proving the government had misled the public about the war for years. And My Lai: in nineteen sixty-eight, American soldiers killed hundreds of Vietnamese civilians, and the Army buried it for over a year. [sigh]

Maya: How does a country come back from that?

Marcus: It came back slowly, and never all the way — not when the briefings promised progress and the evening news showed the opposite.

Maya: So the war's lost at home before it's lost over there. What did it do to the generation that had to fight it?

Marcus: [serious tone] Start with who went. Eighty percent of the troops were working class and poor. A college deferment could keep you out; a factory job couldn't. The draft fell hardest on the kids with the fewest options.

Maya: And then came the birthdays. A lottery number decides your future.

Marcus: By the end of the decade the draft had become a lottery: birthdays drawn, and a low number meant Vietnam. The standard histories tell it that way. Muhammad Ali refused induction in nineteen sixty-seven and lost his heavyweight title for it, and the record is clear there too. More than thirty thousand young men went to Canada instead. And King had said it back in sixty-seven: "The promises of the Great Society have been shot down on the battlefields of Vietnam."

Maya: [incredulous] You were old enough to be drafted at eighteen, but you couldn't vote till twenty-one?

Marcus: [confident tone] That was the argument, and it won. In nineteen seventy-one the Twenty-sixth Amendment set the voting age at eighteen. The war rewrote who counts as a voter.

Maya: And what happened on the campuses?

Marcus: [serious tone] When Nixon widened the war into Cambodia in nineteen seventy, students marched. At Kent State, Guardsmen opened fire. Four students dead at Kent State. Protests and broken trust: this was the generation the last episode promised the war would break.

Maya: So that was the exit. Nixon wins in sixty-eight promising a secret plan to end it. What was the plan?

Marcus: Vietnamization was the plan: train up the South Vietnamese army, pull the Americans out, keep the bombs falling. It cut the troop count fast: by nineteen seventy-two only about twenty-four thousand American personnel were left in Vietnam. As an exit it worked. As a strategy for keeping South Vietnam alive, it failed within two years.

Maya: Paris, seventy-three — that's the peace?

Marcus: [firm] The Paris Peace Accords were signed in January nineteen seventy-three. The last troops came home. The American bill was almost sixty thousand dead. And two years later Saigon fell to the North, and Vietnam reunited under communism. It was the one outcome the whole war had been fought to prevent.

Maya: And Congress finally snaps.

Marcus: [confident tone] Congress passes the War Powers Resolution in nineteen seventy-three, over Nixon's veto. Any troop deployment gets reported within forty-eight hours, and Congress can force a withdrawal after sixty days. The blank-check era was over.

Maya: The credibility gap never really closed, did it.

Marcus: It just found new scandals. A burglary at the Watergate complex was already unraveling: same machinery, different lie. That one's a later lesson's story. And around those marches a whole counterculture was rising.

Maya: Four boxes, let's land them. Box one covers the Domino Theory. If Vietnam went communist the neighbors would topple, so America bankrolled France's war and then took it over. I'm checking that one. Box two covers the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution. It started with a shaky incident and a blank check, and ended with nearly half a million troops there on the draft's back. That one's checked too. Box three covers the Tet Offensive. January of, uh, sixty-eight?

Marcus: Sixty-eight. January thirtieth.

Maya: Right, I'm checking that one. The military win that looked like a defeat opened the credibility gap. Box four covers Vietnamization: Nixon's exit formula. Under it I'm filing the generation the war broke: the working-class draft, the protests, Kent State, Ali. Paris in seventy-three, then Saigon falls in seventy-five. And Congress takes the war power back. I'm checking that one too.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it.

Maya: One: stimulus style. A textbook prints a 1968 photo of the American embassy in Saigon, its wall breached, captioned "Tet." What's the AP point of that image? What is it actually evidence of?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The Americans held the embassy. The firefight was their win. The AP point is perception. After years of promises that victory was near, the enemy struck everywhere at once, on camera. The gap between the promise and the picture is what turned the country against the war. The image is evidence of a political earthquake.

Maya: Two. Why did the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution matter more than the Gulf of Tonkin incident?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The incident was murky. The second attack probably never happened. The resolution was crystal clear: Congress handed the president a blank check for war without ever declaring one. Trivia loses; the exam wants the power shift.

Maya: Three. A student writes: "Vietnamization proves America achieved its goals in Vietnam." Where does that sentence go wrong?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Vietnamization was America's exit formula. It got the troops home in seventy-three. It didn't keep South Vietnam alive: two years later Saigon fell and Vietnam reunited under communism. The sentence mistakes an exit for a victory. The war ended with the one outcome it was fought to prevent.

Maya: One more, fast. Which Cold War idea said Vietnam's fall would topple the rest of Southeast Asia?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The Domino Theory. Box one is landed.

Marcus: [professional broadcast tone] The quagmire swallowed the promise —

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] — and the country learned to stop believing the briefings.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Check your boxes. Next time we get the Green Rebellion. The marchers from this episode build a counterculture — and a river catches fire.
