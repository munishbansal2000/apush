Maya: No horses in America. No tomatoes in Italy. No potatoes in Ireland. And in some towns, within a single generation, nine out of ten people dead. Same ships.

[hold 0.8s]

Marcus: Last time: the three Gs and the pope's line that split the ocean in two. This time the ships move, and they carry cargo nobody bought a ticket for.

Maya: Four boxes today: the Exchange inventory, the disease front, who won and who paid, and the labor crisis. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. By the end, you'll check all four.

Marcus: Westbound first, Europe to the Americas: wheat, sugarcane, and the animals. Horses, cattle, pigs, sheep, chickens.

Maya: Hold on. Horses are new here? I thought the horse was, like, the most American animal there is.

Marcus: Horses evolved here, then died out at the end of the Ice Age, more than ten thousand years ago. Every mustang goes back to European imports.

Maya: So cowboy movies have been lying to me my whole life. Okay. What sailed east?

Marcus: Maize, potatoes, tomatoes, cacao, tobacco. And one bird: the turkey.

Maya: Wait, the turkey went east? I was about to say every animal went west. {trap}

Marcus: Almost every one. The big farm animals went west; the Americas had few domesticated animals to send back. The turkey is the exception, and an answer choice might test it.

Maya: Turkey goes east. Noted. And the potato?

Marcus: The potato might be the most important passenger. It packs calories, grows in poor soil, and helped feed a population boom across Europe. It also set a trap: by the 1840s, much of Ireland lived on one potato variety, and blight brought famine.

Maya: So the potato is a hero and a time bomb.

Marcus: And then there's the tomato.

Maya: Don't. Do not tell me marinara sauce is an import.

Marcus: Nobody in Rome tasted a tomato before 1492. Many Europeans first grew it as a curiosity, wary of it as a cousin of the poisonous nightshade.

Maya: My family's Sunday dinner is basically marinara. You've ruined it.

Marcus: You're welcome. Tobacco crossed too, and Europe picked up the habit fast.

Maya: Okay, here's my take. Europe got the groceries, and the Americas got some farm animals. The Americas lost the trade. {trap}

Marcus: Not so fast. The Americas got calories too: wheat, cattle, and pigs fed millions. And the horse remade whole societies. On the Great Plains, peoples like the Comanche rebuilt their lives around it, hunting bison at speed across huge distances.

Maya: So the classic picture of Plains life, the horse and the buffalo hunt, that's...

Marcus: Post-Exchange. A few centuries old at most. Tradition with a start date.

Marcus: And the animals rearranged the land. Pigs went feral and overran the Southeast, rooting through forests and Native cornfields. Cattle spread into the herds that made ranching possible.

Marcus: Maize didn't stop in Europe, either. Portuguese traders carried it to Africa, where it became a staple, and on to Asia, feeding population growth on both continents.

Marcus: In 1972 a historian named Alfred Crosby put a name to the whole two-way transfer of plants, animals, people, and disease: the Columbian Exchange.

Maya: So the Exchange was almost five hundred years old before it even got a name.

Marcus: And Crosby's point: the biggest cargo crossed without a ticket.

Maya: Let me try box one in one line. Livestock went west, crops went both ways, and the turkey broke the rule.

Maya: Checking that one.

Maya: Your turn. Crops and animals crossed in both directions. Germs mostly crossed one way. Which side empties out, and what does that make possible?

[10-second pause]

Marcus: The side with no immunity empties out. And emptied land is easier to conquer.

Marcus: Smallpox, measles, influenza, sailing west with the colonists. People in the Americas had been cut off from Afro-Eurasian diseases for thousands of years: no prior exposure, no immunity. In the hardest-hit places, eight or nine out of ten died.

Maya: Eight or nine out of ten. That's not a war. That's an erasure.

Maya: Was it just that the Spanish brought so many more people?

Marcus: It wasn't numbers. Many of the worst Old World diseases came from living alongside herd animals for millennia. The germs jumped to people and became childhood illnesses. The Americas had far fewer herd animals, so those diseases never took hold. Historians called the result a virgin-soil epidemic.

Maya: Virgin soil. That phrase bugs me a little. It makes the dying sound like weather.

Marcus: A lot of historians push back for exactly that reason. The dying had help: war, enslavement, forced labor, hunger. Disease led, but it didn't work alone.

Maya: So if the exam asks why the Spanish conquered so fast, I'd say smallpox was basically their secret weapon. {trap}

Marcus: Careful. That's the box-two trap. The Spanish didn't aim the epidemics or understand them, and disease often ran ahead of the soldiers. Lead with disease, then keep going: steel, horses, and Native allies like the Tlaxcalans.

Maya: I keep hearing numbers. I'd rather hear from someone who was there.

Marcus: The Florentine Codex, compiled by a Spanish friar, Bernardino de Sahagún, with Nahua scholars decades after the conquest. Their account of smallpox in Tenochtitlan, in our own words: the sick lay in their homes, unable to move, unable even to turn over, and there was no one left to care for them.

Maya: Decades after. So it's memory, written down under a Spanish friar.

Marcus: Exactly what the exam wants you to notice. It's the Nahua side of the story, but not a raw diary.

Marcus: And the dying went almost entirely one way. The one disease that might have sailed east is syphilis, and historians still argue whether it crossed from the Americas or was already in Europe.

Marcus: Now the ledger. Europe gained calories: maize and potatoes meant more people, more workers, more soldiers. And wealth: silver, sugar, and land.

Maya: So Europe gained calories. What did the Americas get?

Marcus: The Americas paid in people. Estimates run roughly fifty to ninety percent of the Native population lost between 1500 and 1650. Villages emptied, and conquest became possible on a scale steel alone never managed.

Maya: So, final answer: Europe won the Exchange. {trap}

Marcus: That's the box-three trap. Half an answer. Evaluating the Exchange means both halves: what Europe gained, what the Americas lost, and what they gained, like the horse, wheat, and cattle.

Marcus: Then the labor. The Spanish first forced Native people to work through grants called encomiendas. As the dying spread, that labor collapsed. Sugar was the hungriest crop of all, and Europeans already had a model: the sugar islands of Madeira and São Tomé, worked by enslaved Africans.

Maya: Your turn. Native labor is collapsing. Sugar needs workers. The islands already have the model. What happens next?

[10-second pause]

Marcus: The model crosses the ocean. Enslaved Africans, shipped to the Americas: thousands at first, then millions.

Marcus: {soft tone} The Middle Passage. African states and merchants often sold captives to European traders, who packed them into ships bound for the Americas. Most went to the sugar colonies, Portuguese Brazil above all, and the Caribbean. Many did not survive the crossing.

Maya: So the Exchange doesn't end with crops. It ends in chains.

Maya: And I always thought slavery in the Americas started at Jamestown, in 1619. {trap}

Marcus: That's the box-four trap. Enslaved Africans were in Spanish Hispaniola within about a decade of Columbus, and the trade started with sugar, not cotton. Don't start the story at Jamestown.

Maya: Four boxes. One, already checked: the inventory. Livestock west, crops both ways, the turkey east. Crosby named it in 1972.

Marcus: An exchange nobody negotiated.

Maya: Two: the disease front. Smallpox, measles, influenza sailed west. Eight or nine out of ten in the hardest-hit towns. And the one disease that might have sailed east?

Marcus: Syphilis. Maybe. Historians still argue.

Maya: Box two, checked. Three: who won and who paid. Europe gained calories and wealth. The Americas paid in people, and gained the horse, wheat, and cattle. Both halves.

Maya: Box three, checked. Four: the labor crisis. Native labor collapsed, sugar needed workers, and the island model crossed the Atlantic in chains.

Marcus: Germs, then sugar, then chains.

Maya: Box four, checked.

Maya: Before you go, one fast one. Name the one domesticated animal that went east.

[5-second pause]

Maya: The turkey. The big farm animals all went west.

Marcus: Want the exam-style questions? They're in the Unit 1 practice video, once you've watched the whole unit.

Maya: Next time: England stops raiding and starts planting. Jamestown, 1607, the colony that almost didn't make it.

Marcus: The food went both ways —

[hold 1.0s]

Maya: and the dying only went one.
