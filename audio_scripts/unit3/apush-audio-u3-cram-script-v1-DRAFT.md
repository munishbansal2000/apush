Maya: Unit Three cram session. Eleven episodes, ten questions, no mercy. Jay, you ready?

Jay: As ready as I'm going to be. Hit me.

Maya: Then here's the thesis that holds the whole unit together. The Revolution won independence on the battlefield, but the republic was argued into existence, over representation, slavery, and who the promises were for. Every question today is one of those arguments. This is the unit the whole course pivots on. Get these ten cold and the DBQ writes itself. That's the deal. Question one: why did Britain abandon salutary neglect after seventeen sixty-three?

Jay: Because they were broke. The war doubled the national debt, and the empire was twice as big to run.

Maya: And? What did London want from the colonies?

Jay: Money. Troops kept in America, ten thousand of them. Grenville's math: the colonists paid almost nothing for the war, so tax them now.

Maya: And from London's desk, that looked reasonable. The colonists had paid almost nothing toward the war. Asking them to chip in wasn't crazy. It just ran into a century of self-government. Question two: virtual versus actual representation. One sentence each.

Jay: Virtual: Parliament speaks for all British subjects, even ones who never voted. Actual: only a body you've elected can tax you.

Maya: The constitutional core of the whole crisis, right there. James Otis gets credit for the famous line, taxation without representation is tyranny, though John Adams wrote it down decades later. The idea was real even if the quote is fuzzy. Question three: Sugar, Stamp, Townshend, Tea. What did each tax, and what did the colonists do?

Jay: Sugar, seventeen sixty-four, molasses and the first revenue tax, colonists grumbled. Stamp, seventeen sixty-five, paper, dice, everything, massive boycotts and the Stamp Act Congress. Townshend, seventeen sixty-seven, imported goods like tea and glass, boycotts again, women spinning homespun. Tea, seventeen seventy-three, cheap tea that still carried the tax, dumped in the harbor.

Maya: Four for four. And the Declaratory Act, the fine print: Parliament repeals the Stamp Act and claims power in all cases whatsoever. The win that wasn't. Notice the pattern: tax, resistance, repeal, repeat. Each cycle more radical. Question four: why did the Coercive Acts backfire?

Jay: They were meant to punish Boston and isolate the radicals. Closing the port, killing Massachusetts self-government.

Maya: And instead?

Jay: The other colonies freaked out. If London could do that to Boston, it could do it to anyone. Twelve colonies sent delegates to Philadelphia. The First Continental Congress, and the boycott machine behind it.

Maya: Which built the Association, a colonies-wide boycott with teeth, and local committees to enforce it. Punishing one colony united twelve, and protest started becoming government. Classic overreach. Question five: Paine versus the Loyalists. The core disagreement, one line each.

Jay: Paine: monarchy is absurd, republics are common sense, break away now. Loyalists: order and protection beat a gamble, and a fifth of colonists agreed.

Maya: Common Sense sold like nothing before it. A hundred thousand copies in months. Plain language, dynamite argument. And the Loyalists weren't fools. A fifth stayed loyal. They were just wrong about which gamble was safer. Meanwhile enslaved people heard the word liberty and ran to the British. Dunmore's proclamation, seventeen seventy-five: fight for us, go free. The promises were already leaking past their authors. Question six: the two hinges of the war. Saratoga and Yorktown. Why did each matter?

Jay: Saratoga, seventeen seventy-seven: Burgoyne surrenders a whole British army, and France finally believes Franklin. The alliance, money, guns, the fleet.

Maya: And Yorktown?

Jay: Seventeen eighty-one. French fleet seals the bay, Washington marches south, Cornwallis surrenders. The war's effectively over. The legend says the British band played The World Turned Upside Down.

Maya: Supposedly. Probably apocryphal, but it tells you how the moment felt. Don't forget the survival years between the hinges: Trenton, Christmas seventeen seventy-six, and Valley Forge, the winter that forged an army without fighting a battle. Could America have won without France?

Jay: No. Not the money, not the guns, not the fleet.

Maya: Say it with confidence, it's the right answer. Question seven: why did the Articles of Confederation fail? And I don't want weak. I want the structural flaw.

Jay: Congress couldn't tax, so it couldn't pay for anything. No executive, no courts, and amendments needed all thirteen states. Designed by fear of centralized power.

Maya: And the proof it was broken?

Jay: Shays' Rebellion. Farmers drowning in debt, not taxes, march on the Springfield arsenal, and Congress can't raise an army. Boston merchants fund the militia privately.

Maya: Debt, not taxes. You remembered. And the one triumph: the Northwest Ordinance. Banned slavery north of the Ohio, funded schools, set the statehood rule. Outlived the government that wrote it. Question eight: the Convention's three bargains. Representation, slavery, executive. One line each.

Jay: Representation: the Connecticut Compromise, House by population, Senate equal, two per state. Slavery: the Three-Fifths Compromise counted enslaved people for House seats, the trade protected for twenty years, and the word slavery never appears. Executive: the Electoral College picks the president, electors equal to House plus Senate seats.

Maya: And the accident nobody planned: federalism. Power split between the nation and the states. Born as a compromise, worshipped as a philosophy ever since. Who wasn't in the room?

Jay: Women, enslaved people, Native Americans. Rhode Island didn't even send delegates.

Maya: Question nine: Federalists versus Anti-Federalists. What was the fight really about?

Jay: Whether a big, energetic national government would protect liberty or swallow it. Federalists said extend the republic, ambition checks ambition. Anti-Federalists said distant power grows, and demanded a bill of rights.

Maya: Eighty-five essays, Hamilton, Madison, and Jay writing as Publius, at speed. Federalist Ten: factions die in a big republic. Fifty-One: ambition counteracts ambition. And how close was ratification?

Jay: Way too close. Massachusetts by nineteen votes, Virginia by ten, New York by three. The Bill of Rights was the ransom. The losers wrote the winners' homework.

Maya: Last one. Question ten, two parts. Hamilton's program versus Jefferson's vision, and was eighteen hundred really a revolution?

Jay: Hamilton: assumption, the Bank, tariffs, excise taxes, loose construction, a commercial empire with good credit. Jefferson: strict construction, agrarian republic, independent farmers, no bank, no debt machine. And eighteen hundred... the tie, seventy-three to seventy-three, thirty-six ballots, Jefferson wins, Adams just leaves.

Maya: And the tie broke the system so badly they passed the Twelfth Amendment, separating the presidential and vice-presidential votes. Never again. Was it a revolution?

Jay: The transfer was. Peaceful, between rival parties, first time in modern history. But slavery expanded and most people couldn't vote. Revolution with an asterisk.

Maya: Keep that phrase. Revolution with an asterisk. Now, predict the DBQ. If the College Board asks you one big question about this unit, it's this: evaluate the extent to which the American Revolution fundamentally changed American society. Three paragraphs. The change: republican government, the franchise question opened, slavery questioned. The limits: slavery expanded, women voteless, Native dispossession. The verdict: political revolution, social evolution. Your complexity point is the uneven ideals, the promises and who they left out. Argue the change, then argue the limits. Write that essay.

Jay: Ten for ten. I think.

Maya: Eleven episodes, from the Stamp Act to the spelling book. One argument: who counts, and who rules. You earned it. Unit Three: independence won on the battlefield, the republic argued into existence. Go argue well.
