# Pronunciation: Shays (SHAYZ); Miami (my-AM-uh); Harmar (HAR-mer); St. Clair (saynt KLAIR); Greenville (GREEN-vil); Annapolis (uh-NAP-uh-lis); Knox (nocks).

Maya: Last time: Yorktown, 1781. The war is won, the British are going home. Now the founders face a harder problem than beating a king: governing without becoming one. Four boxes today: the structure they built, why it failed, the one thing it did brilliantly, and why it lasted eight years anyway. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eleven and a half minutes, and you'll check all four off.

Jay: Eight years? A whole government lasted eight years?

Maya: Start to finish, 1781 to 1789. And here's my thesis for the episode: the Articles failed at almost everything, except the one thing that mattered most. Pop quiz first. The founders just won a war against a king. They sit down to build a new government. What's the one thing they're terrified of?

Jay: Losing? Like, going broke?

Maya: Power. They're terrified of power itself. One strong government, one man on top. They just fought a king for eight years, so they build a government designed to make sure it can never happen again. Box one: the structure. Jay, under the Articles, how many votes did each state get in Congress?

Jay: One? One vote per state?

Maya: One vote per state, no matter the size. Virginia, giant, gets one. Delaware, tiny, gets one. Now what couldn't Congress do? Give me the list.

Jay: Tax people?

Maya: Couldn't tax. Congress could ask the states for money, and the states could say no, and usually did. What else?

Jay: No president, right? Like, nobody in charge?

Maya: No executive at all. No national courts either. And here's the killer: to change any of it, to amend the Articles, every single state had to agree. All thirteen. One no stopped everything.

Jay: That can't have worked even once.

Maya: It didn't. 1781: Congress proposes a five percent tax on imports to pay the war debts. Twelve states say yes. Rhode Island says no. Done. One state, and Congress stays broke.

Jay: So they built it broken on purpose?

Maya: They didn't fail to build a strong government. They refused to. The fear was the design. And it took four years just to ratify the thing, because Maryland wouldn't sign until the big states gave up their claims to western land. The states were already fighting over the map before the government even started.

Maya: Your turn. You're sitting in Congress, and Rhode Island just killed your only tax. You're broke, soldiers are unpaid, and you need money somewhere. What's left to sell?

[8-second pause]

Jay: Uh... the furniture? No — wait. The western land. The stuff Maryland fought over.

Maya: Land. The one thing Congress actually controlled was the western territory. Remember that answer, because it's the whole third box.

Maya: The exam point students miss: the founders weren't stupid for making it weak. The weakness was the design. They'd just fought a king, and a king was what they were building against.

Jay: Okay, but couldn't they see it wouldn't work?

Maya: Some did. But nobody wanted to be the one who said the Revolution's government was a failure. Checking the first box. The structure: fear-built, one vote a state, no tax power, no executive, no courts, and a unanimity trap. Now box two: the failures, and they came fast.

Maya: Congress can't tax, so it can't pay its debts or its soldiers. It can't regulate trade, so states start taxing each other's goods at the border. Britain never really left, either. They kept their frontier forts on American soil, right where the treaties said they'd go, and they're trading guns to Native nations resisting American settlers. Congress can't touch any of it.

Jay: So it's like thirteen countries pretending to be one?

Maya: Pretty much. And then the crisis that broke it. 1786, Massachusetts. Farmers are drowning. Now the part most people get wrong, so listen close: it wasn't just taxes, and it wasn't just debt. It was both, plus a currency crisis. Farmers owed private debts they couldn't pay, the state piled on taxes, and everything had to be paid in hard currency, coin, which nobody in western Massachusetts had. Miss a payment, the courts took your farm.

Jay: Wait, so they fought a war over taxes and then their own state taxed them harder?

Maya: In hard money they didn't have. The irony wasn't lost on them either. A former Continental Army captain named Daniel Shays leads thousands of farmers. They shut the courts down so no more foreclosures, then march on the federal arsenal at Springfield.

Maya: Your turn. You're a Massachusetts farmer, 1786. You owe debts, the taxes are due in silver, and the sheriff is coming for the farm. The courts won't help you. What do you do?

[8-second pause]

Maya: What Shays did. You stop the courts yourselves. Shut them down, farm by farm, until the state can't take anything.

Jay: And Congress just watched?

Maya: Congress couldn't do anything. No money, no tax power, no army to raise. So a group of wealthy Boston merchants passed the hat and funded a private militia themselves. Private citizens hired the army that crushed the rebellion. Think about that.

Jay: Embarrassing. For the government, I mean.

Maya: It terrified the elite. Henry Knox, Washington's old artillery chief, writes to Washington in a panic. Close paraphrase: the rebels see the weakness of the government, and they feel their own strength. Translation: the poor know the government can't stop them. That letter is the moment a lot of founders realize the Articles might not survive.

Maya: Exam tip, and it's a real student error: don't write that Shays' Rebellion was only about taxes. It was crushing debt plus heavy taxes plus hard currency, all at once. If a short-answer question asks for causes, give all three or you're leaving points out.

Jay: So Shays proved the government couldn't govern.

Maya: Checking the second box. The failures: no money, no trade control, no army, and a rebellion that private merchants had to put down. Now box three. The one triumph. The Articles did exactly one thing brilliantly, and it might be the most important law you've never thought about. The Northwest Ordinance. 1787.

Jay: The Northwest. Like, Ohio?

Maya: The territory north of the Ohio River. Future Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, Michigan, Wisconsin, Minnesota. Remember the question I asked you a few minutes ago? Congress couldn't tax, but it could sell land. So it wrote a rulebook. How do you turn wilderness into states? First: survey it into townships and sell it, orderly, no chaos. Second: set aside land in every township for public schools. Public education, written into the frontier's law.

Jay: So my school exists because Congress needed to sell Ohio? I'm putting that on a poster. Third, and it's huge: no slavery north of the Ohio River. Banned.

Jay: Whoa. So they just ended slavery? In 1787, before the Constitution even existed?

Maya: North of the Ohio River only — and that's the classic error, so watch it. Don't write that the Ordinance ended slavery. South of the river, it kept expanding.

Maya: And fourth: when a territory hit sixty thousand people, it could write a constitution and join the Union as a full state, equal to the original thirteen. Not a colony. A state. That rule is why America grew by adding equals instead of building a colonial empire out west. One textbook calls the Ordinances the most consequential legislation of the whole Confederation period. The government died. The Ordinance lived.

Jay: Okay, but did the orderly part actually happen? People don't usually wait for surveyors.

Maya: They didn't. Second half of this box: the map versus the mess. The Ordinance promised order. The 1790s delivered war. Settlers poured over the Ohio anyway, squatting on land the Ordinance said to sell properly. And the Native nations already living there weren't leaving. A Miami chief named Little Turtle built a confederacy that destroyed two American armies, one after another. Harmar's in 1790. Then St. Clair's in 1791, at the Wabash River. Over six hundred Americans killed — still the worst defeat the United States Army ever suffered at Native hands.

Jay: So the law said one thing and the frontier did the opposite.

Maya: Until General Anthony Wayne, Mad Anthony Wayne, beat the confederacy at Fallen Timbers in 1794. Then the Treaty of Greenville in 1795, where the tribes gave up most of Ohio. So Ohio didn't open by survey. It opened by war, then paperwork. On westward movement in the 1790s, give them both: the Ordinance's orderly promise, and the violent reality.

Jay: The map and the mess.

Maya: You got it. Checking the third box. The triumph and its limits: the Ordinance's schools, slavery ban, and statehood rule, against the reality of Harmar, St. Clair, Fallen Timbers, and Greenville. Now the last box, and it's the question I opened with. If the Articles were this broken, why did they last eight years? Why not collapse in year two?

Jay: Because nobody had a better idea yet? And it kind of worked?

Maya: Both, honestly. It worked just well enough to get through the war's aftermath, and replacing it meant admitting the Revolution's first government was a failure, which nobody wanted to say out loud. It took a real scare to change the math. Shays and his farmers marching on an arsenal made the alternative scarier than the embarrassment. And the scare kept building. 1786: a convention at Annapolis to talk about trade problems. Only five states bother to show up. Alexander Hamilton uses the flop to call for a bigger meeting in Philadelphia the next year, to fix the Articles.

Jay: Which became the Constitutional Convention.

Maya: Which was supposed to fix the Articles and wrote a whole new government instead. And here's the argument historians still have about that. Was the Convention a rescue, saving the republic from collapse? Or was it a counter-revolution, elites grabbing power back from the states and the people? The historian Charles Beard argued the framers were protecting their money and their class. Others say they were saving the country. You don't have to pick. But on the exam, you have to know the fight exists.

Maya: Quick exam note: Beard's argument has a name you'll see. If a question asks about interpretations of the Constitution's framing, "the framers were protecting their property" is the Beard thesis. Free points if you recognize it.

Jay: So: built weak on purpose, couldn't tax or govern, Shays proved it, the Ordinance was the one win, and it took a scare to replace it.

Maya: Checking the fourth box. Four boxes, let's land them: a fear-built structure with no tax power and a unanimity trap, failures from debt to Shays' Rebellion, the Northwest Ordinance's promise against the violent reality of the Ohio country, and eight years of barely working until the scare got bigger than the embarrassment.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One: under the Articles, what did it take to amend the document?

[5-second pause]

Maya: Claim: the bar was impossibly high. Evidence: all thirteen states had to agree, and Rhode Island alone killed the 1781 import tax. Reasoning: unanimity meant any single state could veto reform, which is why the Articles could never fix themselves.

Maya: Two, stimulus-style. A historian quotes Henry Knox writing to Washington that the rebels "see the weakness of government, and feel their own force." What's the point of this source?

[8-second pause]

Maya: Claim: it shows Shays' Rebellion terrified the elite into action. Evidence: Knox, Washington's own artillery chief, is writing in panic, and private Boston merchants had to fund the militia themselves. Reasoning: the rebellion proved the Confederation couldn't defend itself, which is what pushed leaders toward the Philadelphia Convention.

Maya: Three: name two things the Northwest Ordinance did, and one limit.

[5-second pause]

Maya: Claim: it was the Articles' one triumph, with a hard edge. Evidence: it banned slavery north of the Ohio and set the sixty-thousand-person statehood rule, but the ban stopped at the river. Reasoning: the Ordinance shaped how America grew, while leaving slavery untouched everywhere else.

Maya: One more, fast. Rescue or counter-revolution: what's Beard's argument?

[5-second pause]

Maya: That the framers were protecting their money and their class. Whether you buy it or not, know the thesis.

Jay: I think I got all four. The fast one too.

Maya: One thing to carry forward: the Articles solved the founders' fear — no king — and built a government too weak to do anything else. And that's Episode 6.
