# Pronunciation: duh-KAYN (Duquesne), ir-uh-KWOY (Iroquois), PON-tee-ak (Pontiac), oh-TAH-wah (Ottawa), app-uh-LAY-chun (Appalachian), muh-non-guh-HEE-luh (Monongahela)

Maya: Last time: the Unit 2 cram: thirteen colonies built under British neglect, and empires colliding across the continent. This time the collision becomes a war. One river valley, and Britain wins everything except the bill. Three boxes on your sheet: the Albany Plan of Union, the Ohio Valley fight, the 1763 settlement. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes, and you'll check all three off.

Marcus: The setup first. By the 1750s the British colonies were bursting. Roughly ten colonists for every French settler in North America, and they wanted land. West of the Appalachians sat the Ohio River Valley: rich farmland to the British, and to the French, the beating heart of their fur-trade web. Same valley, two hungers.

Maya: And the people already living there get the deciding vote, whether the empires like it or not.

Marcus: They get courted hard. Both empires offered trade, guns, promises. The French had the easier pitch: they honored the local gift-giving traditions and never built big settlements. A trader needs a friend. A farmer needs the field.

Maya: Hold on. So France didn't even want the land? Then why fight for a forest?

Marcus: Because the forest was the business. The fur routes ran through the Ohio Valley, and British farms would choke them off. France was defending a trade web. Britain was defending future cornfields. Different wars, same map.

Maya: So who fires the first shot?

Marcus: George Washington. A young lieutenant colonel in the Virginia militia, sent west in 1753 to order the French out of the Ohio Valley. The French commander at Fort Duquesne, their fort at the forks of the Ohio, ignored the warning completely.

Maya: And Washington came back with friends.

Marcus: He came back in 1754 with his militia and some Native allies, and attacked. It went badly. The French hammered him at Fort Necessity and sent him packing. That skirmish opens the French and Indian War, which then spills worldwide as the Seven Years War.

Maya: A militia kid starts a world war. Tough line on the resume.

Marcus: Meanwhile the colonies try something new. 1754: the Albany Congress. Its real job was to win over the Iroquois Confederacy, and the Iroquois played it beautifully, staying neutral to keep trading with both sides before finally backing Britain.

Maya: And the French had their own allies, right? Not just Britain shopping for friends.

Marcus: The French had tied themselves to the Algonquin and Huron peoples, who fought alongside them through the whole war. Both sides fielded colonial armies marching with Native allies. Nobody fought this one alone.

Maya: And Franklin shows up with a union plan.

Marcus: Franklin shows up with the Albany Plan of Union: one council, delegates from every colony, with the power to tax and raise militias for the common defense, and to run relations with Native nations. The first serious attempt at uniting the colonies under one body.

Maya: And he drew the snake for it. Join, or Die, the cut-up snake. I have seen that cartoon on at least three t-shirts.

Marcus: The cartoon outlived the plan by about two centuries. The colonies rejected it: they said it stole their right to govern themselves. Britain rejected it too: the crown said it stole royal authority. No union. Not yet.

Marcus: Then the war starts horribly for Britain. In 1755 a British army three times the size of the French force got shredded at Monongahela. That was Braddock's defeat. Redcoats stood in lines and made perfect targets for French and Native fighters who refused to stand in lines.

Maya: The world's best army, losing to guys behind trees.

Marcus: Until London changed the game. After 1758, William Pitt poured money and ships into North America, treasury spending the crown barely had. The tide turned. Quebec falls in 1759. Montreal follows in 1760, and the French position in Canada collapses.

Maya: So 1763. What does the map say?

Marcus: The Treaty of Paris. France loses nearly everything on the mainland. Britain takes Canada and the Ohio Valley, plus Florida. Spain hands Florida to Britain in exchange for getting Cuba and the Philippines back, and France hands everything west of the Mississippi, Louisiana, to Spain.

Maya: So France is just gone.

Marcus: Gone from the mainland. And the victory nearly bankrupts the winner: Britain's national debt had doubled, and running the colonies now cost five times what it used to: a population boom plus endless frontier conflict.

Maya: And then Pontiac says no.

Marcus: An Ottawa leader named Pontiac built an alliance of Ottawa, Potawatomi, and Huron fighters and struck British settlements. London wanted no new war, so Parliament drew a line: the Proclamation of 1763. No colonial settlement west of the Appalachian Mountains. Full stop.

Maya: My dad once drove two extra hours to dodge a twelve-dollar toll. I understand, bone-deep, the fury of being told you can't go somewhere you already paid for.

Marcus: The colonists had fought this war for that land. In their eyes it was the prize. In London's eyes it was a fire Britain couldn't afford to keep putting out.

Maya: And the bill part comes next.

Marcus: The taxes. London's logic was cold and clean: the colonists reaped the rewards of the victory, so they'd help pay for the war that bought it. A whole string of new tax and trade laws was already on its way.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: the Albany Plan of Union. Franklin, 1754, one council with the power to tax and raise militias, and both sides shot it down.

Marcus: Checked.

Maya: Two: the Ohio Valley fight. Washington at Fort Necessity in 1754, Braddock's disaster at Monongahela in 1755, then Quebec falling in 1759.

Marcus: On the sheet.

Maya: Three: the 1763 settlement. Treaty of Paris: Britain gets Canada and Florida, Spain gets Louisiana... wait, no. Spain got Florida, right?

Marcus: Spain gave up Florida and got Louisiana. You had it flipped.

Maya: Gave up Florida, got Louisiana. Then the Proclamation line at the Appalachians, and Pontiac's alliance answering with a war of its own.

Marcus: That's the set.

Maya: Two questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One: a textbook prints a map of the Proclamation of 1763, the line running along the Appalachians with settlement arrows stopping dead at it. The prompt asks: what's the point of including this map?

[5-second pause]

Maya: The point is Britain's priority: control over expansion. The war ended with colonists flooding west and a Native alliance pushing back, and London couldn't afford another frontier war, so the map shows the crown choosing order over land, and the colonists reading it as betrayal.

Maya: Two: the Albany Plan died in 1754. Why did both the colonies and the crown hate it?

[5-second pause]

Maya: Because both feared losing power. The colonies said the plan stole their self-government; the crown said it stole royal authority. Same fear, opposite sides.

Maya: Check your three boxes. Episode one in the books. Next time: the tax bill comes due. The Stamp Act, and why "no taxation without representation" was a legal argument, not a slogan.

Marcus: Britain won the continent —

Maya: and handed the colonists the bill.
