# Pronunciation: PON-tee-ak (Pontiac), OT-uh-wuh (Ottawa), nee-OH-lin (Neolin), GREN-vil (Grenville), ap-uh-LAY-chun (Appalachian), AM-urst (Amherst), gayj (Gage), kon-uh-STOH-guh (Conestoga)

Maya: Last time: two centuries of English colonies: New England, the middle colonies, the South. This time those colonies stop arguing with each other, and start arguing with London. 1763. Britain's won the biggest war of the century, and the prime minister opens the books and turns pale. Three boxes on your sheet: the end of salutary neglect, the Proclamation Line of 1763, and Pontiac's Rebellion. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eleven and a half minutes, and you'll land all three.

Marcus: Then start with the bill. The war left London drowning in debt. The books' word is "enormous," so we'll borrow it. And the empire cost more to run than ever: Canada to garrison, Florida to administer, a frontier past every map London owned. The thesis for your notes: Britain won the war, and wrecked the arrangement that had made the empire work.

Maya: And now London's looking for someone else to bill.

Marcus: Won the world — and emptied the treasury. Now, who's holding the calculator?

Maya: Let me guess. Some poor soul whose name I'll have to spell on the test.

Marcus: George Grenville. Prime minister from 1763. And his math was brutally simple. The colonies had gained the most from the war, and they'd paid the least toward it. Their tax burden sat light next to an English farmer's, even on the same goods. So Grenville asked the obvious question: why should London drown in debt for an empire the colonists got on the cheap?

Maya: I'm going to argue with you. The colonists sent their sons. They'd provided so many soldiers, they said, that they'd fulfilled their obligation. Then London mails them a bill with no vote attached. That's not math. That's a shakedown.

Marcus: The colonial case, word for word. We bled, so the ledger's balanced. London's answer never moved: your taxes were a fraction of ours, and the empire that protected you is sinking. Both sides could add. They just kept different ledgers.

Maya: Different ledgers. I'm stealing that.

Marcus: Keep it, but cite me in the footnotes. And those ledgers are the whole war in miniature. London counted the money. The colonies counted what the war had cost them. So what does Grenville do with his math? He ends the era of looking the other way. For a hundred years, London barely enforced its own trade laws: absentee customs officers, smuggled molasses, everybody rich enough not to ask questions. Decades later, a politician named Edmund Burke would coin the name for it, looking back in 1775: salutary neglect. Beneficial neglect.

Maya: Wait, 1775 Burke? The speech guy?

Marcus: The very one. The neglect was real long before it had a name. And in 1763, it ended. Old laws got real teeth. And on Grenville's desk, already drafted: the Sugar Act, the first big postwar tax, and a crackdown on the smuggling everyone had gotten rich on. The taxes get their own episode.

Maya: One for the exam, and it's a thinking one. When a prompt asks why London turned to taxes, start with the war debt. Smuggling makes a colorful sentence. Debt is the engine.

Marcus: And the mistake to avoid for this box: pinning a number on that debt. The books say "enormous." Use their word, not a figure you can't source.

Maya: Box one, done. But the frontier's already on fire.

Maya: Your turn. London's staring at a burned frontier and an empty treasury. Cheaper move: draw a line and freeze everyone east, or pour more soldiers west? Which one buys time?

[9-second pause]

Marcus: The line. Always the line, if you're the one paying. London drew it. The Proclamation of 1763: a line along the crest of the Appalachians, and no colonial settlement past it. The official reason, buried in the dry language: London didn't want another frontier war, or the cost of fighting one. It wanted the fur trade profits to keep flowing. A second frontier war would have cost more than an empty treasury could bear. Triage, not revenge. Cheap beats fair when the treasury's empty.

Maya: Hold on. So this wasn't Britain punishing the colonies? "You cost us a fortune, so no Ohio for you?" Because that was my whole read.

Marcus: The colonial read, and it's wrong in a useful way. Nobody in London was trying to punish anyone. They were terrified of paying for a second war. The line was a bandage on a bleeding frontier.

Maya: Triage. Okay. But somebody must have been furious.

Marcus: The land speculators. Men who'd bought western claims, betting prices would rise, watched their fortunes get fenced off. Including a young George Washington, already speculating in western holdings and already resenting London's limits on westward movement.

Maya: Washington the landlord. My brain keeps filing him under general and president, and he keeps showing up as a businessman.

Marcus: He was all of them. And the settlers were furious too. They'd fought for that land. But here's the part London never planned on: the line didn't hold. Settlers were already west of it, and more kept coming. The books put it bluntly: the proclamation came too late. It stung, as an insult. It failed as a fence.

Maya: We drove the Blue Ridge Parkway two summers ago. My family counted tunnels, and the mountains just never ended. So when you say "a line along the crest," I see that. A real fence. On paper.

Marcus: On paper, exactly. And it set a pattern: drawing a line on a map and calling one side Indian country. America itself would copy that trick later.

Maya: Tip for the test. If a stimulus hands you the Proclamation's promise to leave Native lands undisturbed, ask what the source is for. London dodging another expensive war. Purpose before the grievance — that's how these sources crack open.

Marcus: Common slip: the Mississippi was the prize, the Appalachians were the fence. Don't swap them.

Maya: Your turn again. London leaves roughly ten thousand soldiers in America after the French are gone. Three people hear that news: a minister in London, a settler in Pennsylvania, a British officer. Who sleeps best?

[9-second pause]

Marcus: The officer: he's employed. The minister: the frontier's covered, cheaply. And the settler? He hears boots in peacetime and does new math. Officially, to enforce the line and keep a burning frontier quiet. Ask a colonist in Boston and you'd get a third answer: us. An army that expensive, sitting in peacetime, starts to look like it's there to police the colonists.

Maya: The Proclamation Line of 1763, in one line: London draws a fence it can't patrol, to dodge a war it can't afford.

Marcus: Now the fire the line was drawn against. Pontiac's Rebellion.

Maya: Pontiac — like the car? Please tell me the car wasn't named after a war chief.

Marcus: The car company always said it was. Anyway —

Maya: Wait, really? My uncle drove a Firebird.

Marcus: Pontiac was a chief of the Ottawa, around the Great Lakes. And to understand why he fought, you have to understand what the French did differently. The French negotiated. They sat through ceremonies. They gave gifts, and to the Native nations, gift-giving meant dominance and protection. It was diplomacy. Then the British took the French forts, and a general named Amherst decided the gift ceremonies were demeaning and cut them off. No more gifts. No more diplomacy. Just soldiers and settlers.

Maya: Taking the forts cost them the room.

Marcus: Exactly. And the message that caught fire was older than Pontiac. In 1760 and 1761, a Delaware holy man named Neolin had preached a vision, an apocalyptic one. Curb contact with the fur traders. Put down the guns and the alcohol. Stop the infighting, or disappear. When Pontiac organized the resistance in 1763, that message was still burning.

Maya: So this wasn't random. It was organized.

Marcus: Deeply. The alliance struck fort after fort around the Great Lakes, Detroit first, then six more, and settlements from upstate New York down the Appalachian frontier. Several forts fell. The books count more than four hundred British soldiers and around two thousand colonists killed or captured. At Fort Pitt, by the usual account, the British handed out blankets deliberately infected with smallpox.

Maya: Biological warfare. In 1763.

Marcus: One of the books calls it germ warfare, flatly. London replaced Amherst that August and sent Thomas Gage. Gage broke the rebellion. The bloodshed dragged into 1764, with skirmishes sputtering on for years.

Maya: And the colonists had their own ugly answer, didn't they? The Paxton Boys.

Marcus: The mirror image. A mob of Scots-Irish frontiersmen in Pennsylvania decided the guilty and the innocent looked alike. In 1763 they murdered peaceful Conestoga Indians, Christians, people who'd had nothing to do with the fighting. Twenty dead. Then about two hundred fifty of them marched on Philadelphia itself, demanding the colony stop protecting Indians. London got the message: the frontier was a tinderbox, and every policy poured something on it. The Proclamation was the first pour.

Maya: Sorting rule, test-ready: the rebels in 1763 were Ottawa-led Native nations. If your draft says the colonists rebelled, you've written the wrong war.

Marcus: And watch for this mistake: Neolin was the prophet, Pontiac was the war chief. Swap the jobs and the paragraph falls apart.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: the end of salutary neglect. The war left London with an enormous debt. No number; the books won't give me one. Grenville did the math, and London started enforcing everything it used to ignore.

Marcus: Checked.

Maya: Two: the Proclamation Line of 1763. No settlement past the — the Mississippi? No —

Marcus: The Appalachians.

Maya: The Appalachians. The Mississippi was the prize, the mountains were the fence. London was dodging a war. Pure triage. And it failed as a fence anyway.

Marcus: On the sheet.

Maya: Three: Pontiac's Rebellion. Pontiac, the Delaware prophet — no, wait: Pontiac the Ottawa chief, 1763. Neolin was the prophet. Forts fell, and London panicked its way into a whole new imperial policy.

Marcus: All three.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One: a stimulus prints the Proclamation's promise that Native nations would be left undisturbed in their reserved lands, and asks what situation produced it. Make your case.

[15-second pause]

Maya: The Proclamation was London's cheap answer to Pontiac's Rebellion: it came in response to the outbreak, drawing a settlement line through the Appalachians. With the treasury empty, freezing settlement west cost less than fighting another frontier war. Cheap beats fair.

Maya: Two: most significant effect of the Seven Years' War — the debt, the territory, or the broken trust?

[15-second pause]

Maya: The broken trust: the debt was a policy problem that later governments managed, and the territory stayed British. But Britain started treating the colonies like a problem to be managed, and the colonies started seeing Britain as a threat to their freedom. That never fully healed, and it's the crack every later crisis widens.

Maya: Three: an SAQ asks what changed after 1763 that pushed Pontiac's alliance to war. Make your case.

[15-second pause]

Maya: The British replaced French diplomacy with soldiers: Amherst ended the gift ceremonies the French had used, and British troops occupied the French forts. The forts stopped being a trading partner's posts and became symbols of a new order, so they were the first targets.

Maya: Check your three boxes. Episode eighteen in the books. Next time: the taxes arrive. First sugar, then stamps, and the fight over who gets to tax whom.

Marcus: Britain won the war —

Maya: — and couldn't afford the peace.
