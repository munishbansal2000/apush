Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: the First Continental Congress answered the Intolerable Acts, and the shooting started anyway, Lexington and Concord in April, Bunker Hill in June. Now Congress has to say the word it has avoided for a year: independence. Four boxes: Paine's Common Sense, the vote that almost didn't happen, the Declaration as a legal argument, and who the promises were actually for. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Thirteen minutes. Arguing for independence: Marcus. Against it: our Loyalist guest, a New York merchant.

Maya: One note first: the quoted lines you'll hear are the real words: Paine's, Jefferson's, Abigail Adams's, Lee's resolution, Adams's "pomp and parade," one New Yorker's verdict on the crowds, and the Declaration's own charges. The debate around them is our dramatization.

Maya: Box one: the pamphlet that moved the masses. Marcus, January 1776.

Marcus: Thomas Paine, only emigrated from England in 1774, publishes Common Sense. Within three months, over a hundred thousand copies are printed. In a country of about two million, that's one copy for every twenty people. The presses can't keep up.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] A hundred thousand copies in three months, half a million by the war's end, and Paine reached nearly everyone who could read. But sales aren't arguments. What did he actually say?

Marcus: He started with monarchy itself. Paine wrote that "monarchy and hereditary succession have laid the world in blood and ashes."

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Okay, that's the philosophy. What made a farmer in Pennsylvania care?

Marcus: Distance. A continent wasn't meant to be governed by an island. Then the kicker: America had it in its power to begin the world over again. Farmers read that and thought he was talking about them.

Loyalist: [passionate] He was talking about them right up until the committees started deciding who counted as a good American. I watched the Sons of Liberty burn a man's warehouse for refusing the boycott. I watched neighbors denounce neighbors, while Paine's pamphlet poured fuel on the fire. One New Yorker wrote that "the unthinking multitude are mad for it."

Maya: A fair warning. But the trick of the thing—

Marcus: The philosophy moved people because Paine didn't write like a philosopher. He wrote plain, angry English a farmer could read aloud at the tavern. That was the whole trick.

Maya: [confident tone] Exam tip: when a stimulus quotes Paine on monarchy, "blood and ashes" or a continent governed by an island, the question is testing Common Sense, 1776. Students mix the pamphlet up with the Declaration. Don't.

Maya: So Paine was the first one to say independence out loud.

Marcus: [firm] Common mistake. Others had said the word. Paine was the one who made the masses say it back.

Maya: Box two: the vote. June 1776, and Congress is stuck.

Marcus: June seventh. Richard Henry Lee of Virginia moves "that these United Colonies are, and of right ought to be, free and independent States…" And Congress stalls: some delegations don't have instructions from home, and everyone knows voting yes is treason if this fails.

Maya: [sheepish] So by June the whole Congress was on board, right?

Marcus: Not close. Some delegations didn't even have permission to vote yes. July first, they debate it all day. July second, they vote: twelve colonies yes, none no, and New York abstains, because its delegates still don't have permission. Twelve to nothing, one abstention. Done. The United States is born on July second.

Loyalist: [intense] And the arithmetic, since no one in this room will say it: the finest navy on earth against thirteen colonies with no treasury and no allies. You are voting for the rope.

Maya: [incredulous] Wait, July second? But we do fireworks on the fourth.

Marcus: John Adams wrote that July second would be celebrated with "pomp and parade"... forever. He was two days off. July fourth is when Congress approved the Declaration explaining the vote. The announcement, not the decision.

Maya: Adams predicted the wrong party date. Very human of him.

Maya: Your turn. You're a Pennsylvania delegate in early June 1776, and your instructions still say reconcile. Lee's resolution is on the table, and voting yes means the rope if you lose. What changes your vote?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Most of you just said "proof we could win." Nobody in that room could give them that. They voted yes anyway, carried by the argument.

Maya: [confident tone] Scoring move: any question pitting July second against July fourth is a timing trap. The vote was the second; the fourth was the press release. Adams picked the second.

Maya: And they all signed on July fourth, right? That's the famous date.

Marcus: [firm] Common mix-up. Most of the famous signatures went on weeks later, when the formal engrossed copy was ready.

Maya: Two down, two to go. Box three: the Declaration as a legal argument.

Marcus: The committee of five hands the draft to Jefferson. Thirty-three, the best pen in the room. What he writes reads like a legal brief, and it opens with the principle: all men are created equal, with certain unalienable rights, life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness, and governments get their just powers from the consent of the governed. John Locke, through a colonial reader: when government becomes destructive of those ends, the people can alter or abolish it.

Maya: I am physically unable to hear "we hold these truths" without the Hamilton cast album. Moving on.

Marcus: Then comes the evidence: a long list of charges against the king. I count twenty-seven. Three of them, in Jefferson's words: "He has refused his Assent to Laws, the most wholesome and necessary for the public good." "He has kept among us, in times of peace, Standing Armies without the Consent of our legislatures." "For imposing Taxes on us without our Consent." And then the verdict: these colonies are, and of right ought to be, free and independent states. Principle, evidence, verdict. A case.

Loyalist: [passionate] A case built for an audience, and the audience wasn't in Philadelphia. Every charge had an answer. Jefferson wasn't interested in answers. He was writing an advertisement for rebellion, and the buyers were foreign. Don't mistake propaganda for philosophy.

Marcus: Arguments can be answered. A legal brief filed by a new nation can't be un-filed. Which is why the structure holds.

Maya: [confident tone] When a stimulus hands you a "He has..." charge, the question is testing the brief's structure: the charges are the evidence section. Name the section — the grievance is just the illustration.

Maya: So Jefferson dreamed up the whole natural-rights argument from scratch.

Marcus: [firm] Not quite. Locke wrote the theory; Jefferson wrote the application. Give Locke his credit.

Maya: Box four: who was it actually for. And this is where the document bleeds.

Marcus: [serious tone] Even the draft knew. Jefferson wrote a passage blaming the slave trade on the king. Congress cut it out to placate South Carolina and Georgia. The final document says "all men are created equal" and keeps slavery. That's the wound at the center of it.

Loyalist: [intense] And here's the part your Patriots skip: the British were the ones offering freedom first. November 1775. Lord Dunmore, the royal governor of Virginia, proclaimed that enslaved people held by rebels would go free if they fought for the Crown. Hundreds ran, as many as two thousand by most counts, for his lines. They formed Ethiopian units. By the usual account, their uniforms read "Liberty to Slaves." Your side talked about liberty. My side's governor offered it.

Marcus: Offered it as a weapon, not a principle. Dunmore wanted soldiers, and he was a slaveholder himself. But the larger point stands, and it's devastating. The Revolution promised equality and kept slavery.

Maya: And kept it after the war. Most new state constitutions left slavery untouched; Vermont banned it outright, Pennsylvania passed gradual emancipation, and a handful of northern states followed in the 1780s; in the South it stayed. Enslaved people voted with their feet anyway, running where they could. They heard "all men are created equal" and took it seriously. Their owners didn't.

Maya: What about the women? Abigail Adams wrote her husband in March 1776: "remember the ladies... Do not put such unlimited power into the hands of the husbands."

Marcus: John read it, smiled, and did nothing.

Maya: And after the war, women got what historians call "Republican motherhood": raising virtuous sons counted as their contribution, and the vote stayed out of reach.

Marcus: The Native nations don't get a mention in the philosophy. They get a slur. The Declaration calls them "merciless Indian Savages." Most backed Britain or tried to stay neutral, and it was rational: the people taking their land weren't in London. They were the colonists.

Maya: Your turn. You're an enslaved Virginian in the winter of 1775. You've heard two offers: the Patriots' "all men are created equal," and Dunmore's proclamation. Which one can you actually collect on, and what's the catch in each?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Dunmore's you can collect, if you reach his lines and survive the war. The Patriots' is a promise with no delivery date. One is a military tactic, the other an undated promise.

Maya: [confident tone] The big trap: never write that the Declaration ended slavery. It didn't free a single person. The question testing the exclusions wants the gap between the promise and the practice. Name the gap.

Maya: So the Loyalists were just rich guys protecting their deal with London.

Marcus: [firm] Not exactly. They came from every rank — plenty were ordinary farmers and laborers. The rich-traitor caricature is the mistake.

Maya: Where I land: the Declaration worked as diplomacy better than as philosophy. It told the world, and especially France, that this was a real nation worth backing. But the promises outlived the authors: abolitionists, suffragists, civil rights marchers all picked up the same sentence and used it against the country that wrote it. The Patriots didn't live up to the Declaration. Nobody has. But they wrote down the standard they'd be judged by.

Loyalist: [passionate] Fine words. You gambled thirteen colonies on a philosophy and kept slavery, left women voteless, and called the Native nations savages in the founding document. If that's victory, the word has lost its meaning.

Marcus: And the alternative was accepting we'd stay subjects under an empire that had closed Boston's port and was landing troops. We took the gamble because obedience had its own price, and it was steeper.

Maya: Four boxes, let's land them. One: Common Sense, Paine, January 1776, a hundred thousand copies in three months. Monarchy was the disease, and America could begin the world over again.

Marcus: And the Loyalists heard a mob being handed a script.

Maya: [confident tone] Box one, checked. Two: the vote. Lee's resolution, June seventh, "free and independent States." July second: twelve yes... and New York, what, voted no?

Marcus: Abstained. No instructions from home.

Maya: [confident tone] Abstained. July fourth, the announcement. Adams bet on the second, fireworks on the fourth. Box two, checked. Three: the Declaration as a legal brief. Locke's principle, the charges, twenty-seven give or take, and the verdict.

Marcus: Twenty-seven's the usual count.

Maya: [confident tone] Box three, checked. Four: who it was for, and who it wasn't. The deleted slavery passage, Dunmore's offer, Abigail Adams's letter, and the slur that told the Native nations where they stood.

Marcus: The promise outran the practice.

Maya: [confident tone] By two centuries. Box four, checked.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. And on the long essay: any prompt about whether the Revolution's ideals matched its reality, this episode is your evidence paragraph.

Maya: One, and it comes with a source. Paine, 1776: "monarchy and hereditary succession have laid the world in blood and ashes." What argument is he making, and why did it land harder than anything Congress had said?

[10-second pause]

Maya: He's making the case against monarchy as a system. And it landed because he wrote plain, angry English a farmer could read aloud, nothing like Congress's polite petitions.

Maya: Two. A student writes: "America declared independence on July fourth, 1776, when the delegates signed the Declaration." How many things are wrong, and what actually happened?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Two things wrong. The vote was July second, twelve yes with New York abstaining, and the fourth was the announcement. And most of the famous signatures went on weeks later, when the formal engrossed copy was ready. The fourth explained the vote. The decision came on the second.

Maya: Three, with a stimulus. "He has kept among us, in times of peace, Standing Armies without the Consent of our legislatures." Which part of the Declaration is this, and what's its job in the argument?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The evidence section, the long list of charges. It proves the verdict: sitting between the principle and the resolution, Jefferson stacks the crimes so the breakup reads as a legal conclusion.

Maya: One more, fast. Whose offer of freedom came first, the Declaration's or Dunmore's?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Dunmore's. November 1775, eight months before the Declaration. The Crown's governor moved first; the Patriots' promise arrived in July with no delivery date.

Maya: [intrigued] The breakup letter worked — and the promises are still being collected on, with interest. Check your four boxes. Next time: the war itself. Lexington and Concord, Bunker Hill, and a Congress that has to turn a protest into an army.
