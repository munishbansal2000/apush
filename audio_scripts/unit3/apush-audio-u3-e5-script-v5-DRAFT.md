MAYA: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: the breakup letter, Congress declares independence. The catch: the war was already a year old. Lexington, Concord, and Bunker Hill all happened before the Declaration was printed. This episode: how a protest becomes an army that beats Britain's overwhelming military advantage. Four boxes. The war begins: Lexington, Concord, Bunker Hill. The Second Continental Congress. Saratoga and the French alliance. Yorktown and the Treaty of Paris. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Thirteen minutes, pauses counted.

MARCUS: April nineteenth, seventeen seventy-five. The British commander in Boston, Thomas Gage, sends troops to Concord to seize the colonists' weapons stockpile. At dawn on Lexington Green, minutemen are waiting. Somebody fires, nobody knows who, and within minutes eight Americans are dead. The British push on to Concord, then turn for home, and the march back is the real battle: militia firing from behind stone walls and trees all the way to Boston. By nightfall the British are surrounded in the city.

MAYA: And that's where "the shot heard round the world" comes from.

MARCUS: True, but check the date on it. Nobody at Lexington said that. It's Ralph Waldo Emerson, writing a poem about Concord sixty years later. For the exam, don't quote the phrase like it was shouted on the green. It's literature about the battle, not evidence from it.

MAYA: [curious, inquisitive tone] Okay, next fight: Bunker Hill, June. I'm going to guess the name's a giveaway. They fought on, you know, Bunker Hill.

MARCUS: Breed's Hill. The battle got named for the wrong hill and kept the name. June seventeenth, seventeen seventy-five. Americans dig in above Boston. The British charge uphill into their fire, twice, and get cut down. The third charge takes the hill, because the Americans run out of gunpowder. The British won the hill and lost roughly a thousand men doing it. London read that list and understood this wouldn't be quick.

MAYA: [curious, inquisitive tone] Quick timeline check: the war starts with the Declaration, right? July seventeen seventy-six?

MARCUS: [firm] Common mix-up. The fighting started in April seventeen seventy-five, more than a year before the Declaration. If a question asks what began the war, July seventeen seventy-six is a year too late.

MAYA: One down. The war begins: Lexington and Concord in April, Bunker Hill, on Breed's Hill, in June.

MARCUS: The Second Continental Congress convenes in Philadelphia in May seventeen seventy-five, and with a war burning outside Boston it stops being a protest meeting and starts being a government. It creates the Continental Army, prints money, and later sends diplomats to Europe. In June it picks a commander: George Washington.

MAYA: [curious, inquisitive tone] Because he was the best general they had?

MARCUS: The instinct is tactics, but Congress was doing politics. Washington was a solid officer from the French and Indian War, but the books don't pin the motive on tactics. The political read is they wanted a Virginian, a southern commander for what looked like a New England war, telling every colony this was everybody's fight. By the usual account he showed up to Congress in his uniform, which didn't hurt.

MAYA: [curious, inquisitive tone] So the army's brand-new, the commander's a political pick, and they're facing the British Empire. How bad is the mismatch?

MARCUS: About as bad as it gets. Britain had an overwhelming military advantage: a navy that controlled the seas, a highly regarded professional army, tens of thousands of hired German soldiers, the Hessians, and roughly a fifth of the colonists still loyal to the crown, against American militia with bad muskets. So the strategy wrote itself: don't lose the army. As long as the army existed, the Revolution existed.

MAYA: [curious, inquisitive tone] So the SAQ answer: Washington was the best general they had, military brilliance?

MARCUS: [firm] Common mistake. The question is really asking: why a Virginian to lead? The answer is politics. Congress wanted a southern commander for what looked like a New England war. Write "military brilliance" and you've answered a different question than the one they asked.

MAYA: [curious, inquisitive tone] Okay, so the mistake I'd make: Congress builds this army to win independence. Right?

MARCUS: [firm] Not quite. A full year before independence was declared, they're still British subjects demanding their rights — the army came first, and the breakup letter came later.

MARCUS: Seventeen seventy-six is a disaster. The British drive Washington out of New York and chase what's left of the army across New Jersey, and by December enlistments are about to expire. So on Christmas night, Washington ferries his men across an ice-choked river in a storm and hits the Hessian garrison at Trenton at dawn, December twenty-sixth. The Hessians are professionals, but nobody crosses a frozen river on Christmas night to start a battle, and that's the whole gamble.

MAYA: I walked back from the library once in freezing rain in sneakers and I still bring it up at dinner. These men crossed a river full of ice. In the dark.

MARCUS: And it worked. Close to a thousand prisoners, barely an American scratch — and a week later, Princeton keeps the momentum. Neither battle changed the map, but enlistments steadied and the cause lived through the winter. Days earlier, Paine's The American Crisis had been read to the troops: "These are the times that try men's souls."

MARCUS: Your turn. Washington's staring at a frozen river on Christmas night. Two questions: why could this work, and what happens if it doesn't? [9-second pause]

MAYA: It could work because nobody sets a defense for Christmas morning in a blizzard. Surprise is the entire weapon. And if it fails, he loses the army, and the army is the Revolution. So he loses everything.

MARCUS: Surprise was the entire weapon — and the army was the Revolution. Lose that gamble, lose everything.

MARCUS: Seventeen seventy-seven. General Burgoyne marches down from Canada to cut New England off. It falls apart. American forces under Horatio Gates close in, and at Saratoga, Benedict Arnold, yes, that Arnold, before the treason, leads the charges and takes a bullet in the leg. In October, Burgoyne surrenders an entire British army.

MARCUS: Your turn. You're France, watching from the sidelines for three years. Why does a British surrender at Saratoga change your mind, when Lexington and Trenton didn't? [9-second pause]

MAYA: Because now the Americans don't look like a lost cause. France isn't a charity. If it's spending money and ships, it needs a winner. And didn't France lose big to Britain in the last war? This smells like payback.

MARCUS: Payback's the engine. France got humiliated in the Seven Years' War and wants Britain cut down. The Americans just proved they're a bet worth making. So in seventeen seventy-eight, the Treaty of Alliance. France sends money and guns, then troops, and eventually the navy. Paris had already been cheating, a front company shipping muskets while France pretended to stay neutral. Franklin had been working the French court since late seventeen seventy-six, and Spain jumps in on France's side in seventy-nine. The war goes global, and that's what Britain can't afford.

MARCUS: One winter between the big moments, and it's the one everyone remembers. Valley Forge, seventeen seventy-seven to seventy-eight. No battles. Just cold, hunger, no shoes, men wrapping their feet in rags. Then Friedrich von Steuben, a Prussian officer who'd padded his résumé a bit, drills those barefoot men into professionals. The army that marched out in spring wasn't the army that marched in.

MARCUS: When a question calls Saratoga the turning point, answer with the mechanism: Saratoga convinced France the Americans could win, and French entry turned a colonial rebellion into a worldwide war. That part is what Britain couldn't afford. And a correction on the old textbooks: France joined to settle a score — revenge on Britain and a weaker rival.

MARCUS: By seventeen eighty, London tries a new plan: the southern strategy. The South supposedly holds more Loyalists. Take Charleston, rally them, roll north. Charleston falls, an entire American army surrenders. Then Camden falls as well — another disaster.

MAYA: So the strategy works.

MARCUS: It looks like it works. But the Loyalists never rise in the numbers London counted on, and Nathanael Greene finds the counter: lose battles, win the war. Cowpens, Guilford Courthouse: Greene bleeds Cornwallis's army and slips away before it can be destroyed. The British keep winning the field and losing the war.

MAYA: And Cornwallis ends up cornered in Virginia.

MARCUS: Dug in at Yorktown, waiting for the Royal Navy to resupply him. Instead, in September seventeen eighty-one, a French fleet under de Grasse beats the British off the Chesapeake and seals the bay. Washington marches the allied army south from New York, American and French, Rochambeau and Lafayette, and London never sees it coming. Then came three weeks of siege trenches. Cornwallis tries to slip across the river at night, and a storm scatters his boats. October nineteenth, seventeen eighty-one: he surrenders, pleads illness, sends a subordinate with his sword.

MAYA: [curious, inquisitive tone] Hold on. Lafayette, the "immigrants, we get the job done" guy from Hamilton? He was actually there?

MARCUS: Actually there, commanding troops at twenty-four. The musical got that part right.

MAYA: [curious, inquisitive tone] And the band, did they really play "The World Turned Upside Down" while the British marched out?

MARCUS: [thoughtful tone] The story goes they did, but the story shows up much later, so historians hedge. Nobody's fully sure.

MARCUS: The treaty takes two more years. In seventeen eighty-three, Paris sets the terms: Britain recognizes American independence. The western border runs to the Mississippi. Americans keep fishing rights off Newfoundland. Florida goes back to Spain. Generous terms, deliberately. The books say Lord Shelburne was looking ahead to highly profitable two-way trade between Britain and the fast-growing United States — generosity as good business.

MAYA: [curious, inquisitive tone] Map question on Paris: the U.S. gets everything east of the Mississippi. Just shade the whole east coast, right?

MARCUS: [firm] Common mix-up. Almost everything east of the Mississippi — except Florida, which went back to Spain. Shade Florida separately.

MAYA: [curious, inquisitive tone] So Yorktown and Paris are basically one event, right? The war ends and the treaty gets signed?

MARCUS: [firm] Not quite. Two years apart: Yorktown, October seventeen eighty-one; the Treaty of Paris, seventeen eighty-three. Merge them into one event and the essay's wrong before it starts.

MAYA: The war is won — now let's make sure it sticks.

MAYA: Four boxes, let's land them. Box one: the war begins. Lexington and Concord, April seventeen seventy-five: the march to Concord, then the long bloody walk back to Boston. Bunker Hill, June, fought on Breed's Hill, not Bunker Hill. The British take it and bleed for it.

MARCUS: [serious tone] Roughly a thousand casualties, for one hill.

MAYA: Box two: the Second Continental Congress, May seventeen seventy-five. It becomes a war government, builds the Continental Army, and in June hands it to Washington. Picked because he was a Virginian?

MARCUS: The appointment was politics. The general grew into it.

MAYA: Box three: the long middle. Trenton, Christmas seventeen seventy-six, the crossing that saved the army. Saratoga, seventeen seventy... eight? No. Seventy-seven. The French alliance is seventy-eight, so Saratoga has to be seventy-seven.

MARCUS: Seventy-seven. Clean it up in your notes.

MAYA: [confident tone] Valley Forge in between, where von Steuben drills the army into shape. Box four: Yorktown, October seventeen eighty-one. The French fleet seals the bay, Cornwallis surrenders. Treaty of Paris, seventeen eighty-three. Independence, and the Mississippi becomes the border. Check your boxes.

MAYA: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it.

MAYA: One. "These are the times that try men's souls." December seventeen seventy-six, read aloud to Washington's troops. What does this source tell you about the state of the cause that winter? [15-second pause]

MAYA: The army was dissolving. Enlistments expiring, desertion everywhere. Paine is writing to men about to walk away — soldiers with desertion on their minds. The battlefield that winter was morale itself. Washington needed them to stay more than he needed a new plan.

MAYA: Two. Saratoga, October seventeen seventy-seven. France watched three years of war from the sidelines. Why does this surrender change the calculation? [17-second pause]

MAYA: Saratoga proved the Americans could beat a British army in the field. France wasn't betting on a lost cause anymore. And France had its own reasons: payback for the Seven Years' War, and a weaker Britain. Paris bet on the odds; ideals were the sales pitch.

MAYA: Three. At Yorktown, what did France bring that Washington could never have supplied himself? [20-second pause]

MAYA: A navy. De Grasse's fleet sealed the Chesapeake and trapped Cornwallis against the sea. Without that fleet, Cornwallis sails away, and the war drags on for years.

MAYA: One more, fast. Washington's appointment or the Declaration of Independence: which came first? [5-second pause] The appointment. June seventeen seventy-five, a full year before the Declaration.

MARCUS: [professional broadcast tone] So the war's won, and the bills come due. And underneath it all, the question nobody's answered: what freedom did this war actually win for the enslaved people who fought in it?

MAYA: [intrigued] Next time: the Articles of Confederation. The first government the founders built, and why it fell apart.

Both: [professional broadcast tone] Win the war — then survive the peace.
