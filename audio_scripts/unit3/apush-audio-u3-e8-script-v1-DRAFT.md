Maya: Last time: the Constitution got ratified. The Bill of Rights sealed the deal in 1791. But a constitution is a blueprint, not a building. Somebody had to hire the crew, pay the bills, and prove the thing could survive a punch. Three boxes on your sheet: Hamilton's financial plan, the Whiskey Rebellion, and the first party system. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes, and every one of them lands.

Marcus: First up, George Washington. The Constitution says the president executes the laws and stops there. So Washington invented the job. He built a cabinet of department heads: Jefferson at State, Hamilton at Treasury, Knox at War, Edmund Randolph as Attorney General.

Maya: The original cabinet. Four guys and a brand-new country.

Marcus: And the original exit. Nothing in the Constitution said how many terms a president gets, so after eight years Washington just left. Voluntarily. That two-term precedent held until Franklin Roosevelt broke it in 1940.

Maya: Walking away from power on purpose. That's the flex.

Marcus: Then the farewell address. Two warnings: don't get tangled in foreign alliances, and don't let political parties tear the country apart.

Maya: My family group chat has a no-politics rule that lasts about eleven minutes. Washington would understand.

Marcus: He'd recognize the next part too. Because the cabinet he built split in two over money: Hamilton's financial plan.

Maya: The plan had funding the debt, the bank, and taxes? Something like that.

Marcus: Close enough to work with. Hamilton said the federal government should pay off the war debts, its own and the states', because a nation that pays its bills can borrow again. Virginia had already paid its debts and hated the idea. Then the big one: a national Bank of the United States, chartered in 1791, to handle the government's money and steady its credit.

Maya: And Jefferson said the Constitution doesn't mention a bank anywhere.

Marcus: Word for word the argument. Hamilton answered with the necessary and proper clause, the elastic clause, saying collecting taxes implies somewhere to put them. The bank won. The tariff and the whiskey excise came in the same package: revenue to pay it all off.

Maya: Box one is Hamilton's financial plan: debts, the Bank, taxes. And Washington's cabinet was the crew running it, so I'm parking his precedents under the same box.

Marcus: Parked. Now watch the tax part explode. The whiskey excise lit the fuse. Western Pennsylvania farmers turned grain into whiskey because grain rotted on the road to market. The tax hit their actual livelihood. By 1794 they'd had enough: marches on tax collectors, a few tarred and feathered.

Maya: Wait, Washington actually led the troops himself? Like, the president rode out?

Marcus: He federalized the militias of several states and rode at the head of the column. The rebellion folded before the army even arrived. And here's the point to tattoo on the box: under the Articles, Shays's Rebellion got nothing from Congress. Under the Constitution, the government showed up with thousands of militiamen.

Maya: So the Whiskey Rebellion: Washington crushed it, and the Democratic-Republicans cheered because order?

Marcus: The opposite. Jefferson's people called it tyranny. An army marching on citizens over a tax. Same event, two parties reading it two ways.

Maya: And that split is the birth of the party system. Federalists, Hamilton's people, wanted a stronger federal government. Democratic-Republicans, Jefferson's, wanted power kept with the states. The Constitution never mentions parties. Washington warned against them. They formed anyway.

Marcus: Over four fights. Money was one. The French Revolution was the second: Democratic-Republicans wanted to back France, our old ally. Federalists looked at the guillotines and said absolutely not. Washington's Neutrality Proclamation of 1793 kept America out, and Jefferson quit the cabinet over it.

Maya: Then Citizen Genêt showed up and made it worse.

Marcus: France's ambassador toured the country recruiting Americans to fight Britain, right past the president's proclamation. Meanwhile Britain was still arming Native groups in the Ohio country and seizing American ships, so Washington sent John Jay to London. Jay's Treaty won some trade concessions and a British pullout from the western forts, but left the big grievances alive — the Democratic-Republicans hated it.

Maya: And Pinckney's Treaty was the one people liked?

Marcus: With Spain: it set the southern border and opened New Orleans and the Mississippi to American trade. Western farmers could finally get their goods to market. That one was popular.

Maya: So parties fight over money, France, and treaties. What broke it open?

Marcus: The Alien and Sedition Acts of 1798. President John Adams's Federalists made it a crime to criticize the government and gave the president power to deport immigrants deemed dangerous. The target was French sympathizers and Democratic-Republican newspapers.

Maya: That's the opposite of the Bill of Rights they just added.

Marcus: Jefferson and Madison thought so too. Their answer was the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions: the states declared the acts unconstitutional, arguing the Constitution was a compact among states and a state could judge when the federal government overstepped. That compact theory, the seed of nullification, would echo all the way to the Civil War.

Maya: Nullification: that's the word South Carolina picks up later, right? With the tariffs?

Marcus: Thirty years later, same playbook. And then the system passed its hardest test. The election of 1800: Jefferson beat Adams, and power changed hands between rival parties without anyone getting shot. For a republic that young, that was the miracle.

Maya: Peaceful transfer of power. We take it for granted; they invented it.

Maya: The republic survived its shakedown. Now the question gets personal: who are these Americans, anyway?

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: Hamilton's financial plan. Assumption of the state debts, the Bank of the United States in 1791, the tariff, the whiskey excise, Jefferson crying foul on all of it. And Washington's cabinet and the two-term precedent ride under this box, since his crew ran the plan.

Marcus: On the sheet.

Maya: Two: the Whiskey Rebellion. 1794, Pennsylvania farmers, Washington rides out with the militia. The new government enforces its laws, which the Articles never could.

Marcus: Checked.

Maya: Three: the first party system. Federalists versus Democratic-Republicans, split over the money plan, the French Revolution. Wait, which side wanted to help France? The Democratic-Republicans?

Marcus: Right. Jefferson's people backed France; Hamilton's feared the chaos.

Maya: The Neutrality Proclamation, Jay's Treaty, Pinckney's Treaty, the Alien and Sedition Acts, the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions with that nullification seed, Washington's farewell warning about parties, and 1800: power changes hands peacefully.
