# Pronunciation: Talleyrand (TAL-ee-rand).

Maya: Seventeen ninety-eight. French warships are seizing American merchantmen, and the country is rallying behind President Adams. In Philadelphia, the Federalist majority in Congress answers with a law that makes it a crime to criticize them. The war is real. The target is at home. Tonight: the first time America criminalized dissent. Arguing the Federalist case: Marcus. Arguing from the other side of a jail cell, in the voice of Anthony Haswell, the Vermont newspaper editor prosecuted under the Sedition Act: Haswell.

Marcus: Context first. France is seizing American ships. Our envoys go to Paris, and Talleyrand's agents, history remembers them as X, Y, and Z, demand a quarter-million-dollar bribe plus a ten-million-dollar loan just to open negotiations. Our envoy's answer: no, no, not a sixpence. The country rallies. Congress builds a navy. We fight an undeclared naval war, the Quasi-War, for two years. And in wartime, unity isn't optional. French agents are operating in America, and their American sympathizers, the Democratic-Republican press, are printing poison every week. The Alien and Sedition Acts were wartime security. Every nation in history has drawn this line. Adams signed them into law. The Republicans called them tyranny, and started organizing.

Haswell: I drew no line. I printed one. I published an advertisement for a lottery to raise the fine levied on Congressman Matthew Lyon, and I called the Federalist marshal a hard-hearted savage. For that, the marshal came to my house in the middle of the night, put a sick man on a horse, and rode me fifty miles over muddy roads to a jail in Rutland. My trial lasted a day. The judge told the jury that truth would only save me if I proved every word of every charge. I was fined two hundred dollars and given two months in the Bennington jail. My crime was printing words. In a republic with a Bill of Rights not ten years old.

Maya: Two cases on the table: national security versus a free press. Clash one: the victims. Because this law had victims, with names. Marcus, you say wartime security. Who did it actually catch?

Marcus: It caught people working against the government in wartime. Matthew Lyon, a congressman, who accused President Adams of, quote, a thirst for ridiculous pomp, foolish adulation, and selfish avarice. Editors printing lies about the administration while French frigates seized our ships. The law punished false, scandalous, and malicious writings against the government. False. There was a truth defense. This wasn't a king jailing poets. It was a republic defending itself with a statute that expired on its own.

Haswell: Read the statute's crime aloud and tell me it's narrow. Quote: any false, scandalous, and malicious writing against the government of the United States, or either house of Congress, or the President. End quote. Against the government. Not against the nation in wartime. Against the government, which in seventeen ninety-eight meant the Federalist Party. Twenty-five Americans arrested. Benjamin Franklin Bache, Ben Franklin's own grandson, arrested for his newspaper and dead of yellow fever before he could be tried. Congressman Lyon, fined a thousand dollars, four months in jail, and re-elected from his cell, because his constituents understood exactly what was happening. And me. Two hundred dollars, two months, for reprinting another man's paragraph and selling lottery tickets. If that's a narrow wartime statute, I'd hate to see a broad one.

Maya: Marcus, the truth defense point. Was it real?

Marcus: It was in the text. Truth as a defense was actually an advance over English common law, where truth made libel worse. The Federalists wrote protections in.

Haswell: Protections the judges refused to honor. At my trial, Justice Paterson instructed the jury that truth would exonerate me only if I met every contention of the indictment, an impossible standard, and added that it wasn't even necessary that I wrote the words. If it was issued in my paper, that was enough. The truth defense was a promise the courtroom wouldn't keep.

Maya: Name the four acts. People mix them up, and the details matter.

Marcus: One: Naturalization. Citizenship stretched from five years to fourteen, the longest wait in American history, before or since, aimed at the immigrant voters who backed Jefferson. Two: the Alien Friends Act. The president could deport any alien he deemed dangerous. Three: the Alien Enemies Act. Citizens of a hostile nation could be deported in wartime. Four: the Sedition Act, the speech crime.

Haswell: The pattern wasn't subtle. Make citizenship harder for the people likely to vote against you. Deport the ones you can't convince. Jail the editors who complain. An election strategy wearing a war's uniform.

Maya: Twenty-five arrests. A dead editor. A congressman re-elected from his cell. The narrow wartime statute's record.

Maya: Clash two: the counterattack. Because the Republicans didn't just take it. Haswell, your side answered with the Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions. What did they say?

Haswell: Jefferson wrote Kentucky's, in secret, because even writing it was dangerous. His argument: the Constitution is a compact among the states, and when the federal government grabs powers the compact never gave, a state can nullify the law. Nullification. Madison's Virginia Resolutions were cooler in tone: the states could interpose, step between the federal government and the citizen. Different words, same claim. The states created this government. The states can judge when it breaks the deal.

Marcus: And that claim nearly broke the country. Nullification is secession's first draft. If each state can veto federal law, there's no federal law, there's no union, there's the Articles of Confederation again with better stationery. Jefferson wrote nullification in a panic, anonymously, and the other states refused to endorse it. Not one state signed on to Kentucky's doctrine. The counterattack failed on its own terms.

Haswell: It failed as doctrine and won as politics. The real verdict wasn't a resolution. It was an election. Eighteen hundred. The people threw the Federalists out, Jefferson in, and the Sedition Act expired on March third, eighteen oh one, the day before the inauguration. President Jefferson pardoned every victim still suffering under it, Lyon among them. The country didn't nullify the law. It buried the lawmakers.

Maya: So the resolutions failed as law and won as politics. The election did what nullification couldn't.

Maya: One question hanging over all of this. Why didn't the Supreme Court stop it? The First Amendment was right there.

Marcus: Because judicial review didn't exist yet. The Court striking down a federal law, that machinery gets invented in eighteen oh three, in Marbury. In seventeen ninety-eight, nobody was sure the Court could do that at all. The check the framers imagined was elections. And elections worked. The system corrected itself without judges.

Haswell: Elections corrected it after the damage was done. Tell that to Bache, dead before trial. The First Amendment says Congress shall make no law. Not, no law unless frightened. Not, no law until the next election. No law. The Court's silence wasn't the system working. It was the system looking away.

Maya: Closing statements. One paragraph each. Marcus, you first.

Marcus: The Federalists faced a real threat: a naval war, foreign agents, a press corps cheering for France. They overreached, and the voters punished them for it. But the republic survived the overreach, the war ended with the Convention of eighteen hundred, and the precedent set was that even wartime repression gets reversed. Security panics. Liberty returns. And the deeper precedent: the voters reversed it, not the courts. America's free-speech tradition was born at the ballot box, not the bench. That's the American cycle.

Haswell: I was taken from my house at midnight for printing words, tried in a day, and jailed for two months, and two thousand of my neighbors marched me out of that jail to Yankee Doodle when my time was done. They understood what the judges didn't: criticism isn't treason, and a republic that jails its printers has forgotten why it exists. The Act expired. The principle didn't. It's still on trial every time the country gets scared, and every generation gets to decide the case again.

Maya: Here's my verdict. The Sedition Act was the first American free-speech crisis, and it set the template. A frightened government criminalizes dissent. The dissenters go to jail. The country argues about it for two centuries. The historians' question: did the backlash create America's civil-liberties tradition, or just pause the repression? The Espionage Act of nineteen seventeen suggests pause. But the pardons, the expired statute, the election that threw the jailers out, that suggests something was learned. Every security-versus-liberty fight since, nineteen seventeen, nineteen forty-two, two thousand one, is this fight. Next time: the election of eighteen hundred. A tie, a constitutional crisis, and the most boring radical event in American history.

Marcus: The jailers lost the election. The editors got the last word.
