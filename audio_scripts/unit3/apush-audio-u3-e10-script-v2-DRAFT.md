# Pronunciation: Talleyrand (TAL-ee-rand), Haswell (HAZ-wel), Pinckney (PINK-nee), Gerry (GEH-ree).

Maya: Last time: the parties we just built turn on each other, and on France. Now, 1798: American diplomats sail to Paris, and the French foreign minister's men hand them a price list: a quarter of a million dollars in bribes just to start talking. America answers with warships, and Congress passes a law making it a crime to criticize the president. Four boxes tonight. One: the XYZ Affair and the Quasi-War. Two: the four Alien and Sedition Acts, and what each one did. Three: the Republican counterattack, the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions, and compact theory. Four: the election of 1800, and the verdict. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Twelve and a half minutes. Every one of them gets argued tonight.

Maya: One ground rule. You'll hear real quotes tonight: the Sedition Act's words, the First Amendment, a congressman's letter, the Kentucky Resolutions' nullification line, and the famous reply to the French. I'll flag each one. The debate is dramatized: Marcus and Haswell argue the real positions of 1798, but their back-and-forth is ours. Marcus's knowledge stops at 1800. The afterlife is my territory only.

Maya: Marcus, set the table. How do France and America get from allies to a bribe demand?

Marcus: Start with the ships. France is at war with Britain, and French privateers are seizing American merchantmen, more than three hundred by 1797. France is furious about Jay's Treaty, our deal with Britain, and it's taking it out on our cargo. Adams sends three envoys to Paris: Pinckney, Marshall, and Gerry.

Haswell: And Talleyrand, the French foreign minister, won't receive them. His agents demand a quarter-million-dollar bribe, plus a ten-million-dollar loan, before talks begin. The envoys refuse, with the reply history gives them, quote, "No! No! Not a sixpence!" End quote.

Marcus: Adams publishes the dispatches, the agents' names scrubbed to X, Y, and Z. The country rallies behind "millions for defense, but not one cent for tribute." Congress builds up the Navy, and from 1798 to 1800 America and France fight the Quasi-War, an undeclared naval war. No declaration, just warships.

Maya: Haswell, the threat was real. Why isn't the Federalist answer justified?

Haswell: The threat was real. The answer was aimed somewhere else. France seized our ships, and Congress made it a crime for an American editor to criticize an American president. If the danger is French privateers, why is the target a Vermont printing press?

Marcus: Because a government can't fight a war with its own press cheering the enemy. French agents are operating in America, their sympathizers run the Republican papers, and those papers print poison every week while our sailors are taken. Every nation in history has drawn this line in wartime.

Maya: Your turn. Adams holds the dispatches, proof of the bribe demand. Publish or sit on them: does publishing help him or hurt him? [9-second pause]

Maya: It helps him, enormously. A diplomatic insult becomes a national one, the Republicans who'd cheered France go quiet, and Adams gets his war fever. Published evidence converts embarrassment into outrage. If an SAQ asks for a cause of the Quasi-War, name the mechanism, not just the event.

Maya: The mix-up I keep seeing: X, Y, and Z were the French agents, not the American envoys. The Americans were Pinckney, Marshall, and Gerry. Don't flip them.

Maya: Box one is landed, the XYZ Affair and the Quasi-War.

Maya: The war fever is the kindling. The Acts are the fire. Marcus, name them.

Marcus: Four laws, summer of 1798. The Naturalization Act: citizenship goes from five years to fourteen, hitting immigrants, who mostly vote Republican. The Alien Friends Act: in peacetime, the president can deport any foreigner he calls dangerous. The Alien Enemies Act: in wartime, citizens of a hostile nation can be arrested or deported. And the Sedition Act: it makes it a crime to publish, quote, "any false, scandalous, and malicious writing" against the government, Congress, or the president. End quote.

Haswell: Notice who that crime protects. Not the nation in wartime. The government, which means the Federalist Party. Slow citizenship for the people likely to vote against you, deport the ones you can't convince, jail the editors who complain. An election strategy wearing a war's uniform.

Maya: But dozens of French spies were rounded up and shipped out, right?

Marcus: No, and that's the tell. As far as the record shows, Adams never deported a single person under the Alien Friends Act. The deportation power was a threat, not a program. The weapon they used was the Sedition Act, against editors and one congressman.

Haswell: I felt that weapon. I printed an ad for a lottery to pay Congressman Lyon's fine, and called the marshal a hard-hearted savage. The marshal came to my house at midnight, put a sick man on a horse, and rode me fifty miles to jail. The judge told the jury the truth would save me only if I proved every word of every charge. Two hundred dollars fine. About two months in a Bennington jail. My crime was printing words, ten years after the Bill of Rights.

Maya: My sophomore year, our yearbook roasted the cafeteria food and the principal killed it for a week. I was furious, and it was a lunch menu. I keep thinking about that trial, Haswell. His was a jail cell.

Marcus: The law did include a truth defense, an advance on the old English rule of seditious libel, where criticizing the government was a crime and truth made it worse.

Haswell: A defense the courtroom wouldn't honor. Here is the text that matters. The First Amendment, quote: "Congress shall make no law…" End quote. Not no law unless frightened. No law.

Maya: The victims had names. Congressman Lyon: a thousand-dollar fine, four months in jail, re-elected from his cell, for a letter accusing Adams of, quote, "an unbounded thirst for ridiculous pomp, foolish adulation, and selfish avarice." End quote. And Benjamin Franklin Bache, Ben Franklin's grandson: arrested for his newspaper, dead of yellow fever before trial.

Maya: Don't write that the Alien Acts were the speech crime. The speech crime was the Sedition Act. The Alien Acts were about immigrants. Students blur all four into one law, and the exam separates them.

Maya: Bank points by explaining who each act targeted and why. Naturalization: immigrant voters. The Alien Acts: French immigrants. The Sedition Act: Republican editors. Targeting is the analysis.

Maya: Box two is landed, the four Acts and what each one did. Checking that one.

Maya: The Republicans don't just take it. Haswell, your side answers with the Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions. What did they say?

Haswell: Jefferson wrote Kentucky's in secret; even writing it felt dangerous. The Constitution is a compact, a deal among the states. When the federal government grabs powers the deal never gave, a state can nullify the law, declare it void inside its borders. Madison's Virginia Resolutions were cooler: the states could interpose, step between the federal government and the citizen. The states made this government. The states can judge when it breaks the deal. Kentucky's own words, quote, "its acts are unauthoritative, void, and of no force." End quote.

Marcus: And that doctrine nearly breaks the country. If each state can veto federal law, there's no federal law. There's the Articles of Confederation again with better stationery. And Kentucky and Virginia stood alone. Not one other state adopted them.

Maya: Not one did. The resolutions failed as law the day they were written. But the idea survived. Compact theory, the idea that the Constitution is a deal among sovereign states, became the seed of many states'-rights fights to come. If the prompt asks about continuity, start here and walk forward: Kentucky's compact theory becomes South Carolina's nullification argument in the 1830s. The exam rewards the thread, not the single event.

Maya: Students lose points when they write secession but mean nullification. Nullification says a state can void a federal law. Secession says a state can leave. Different claims, different crises.

Maya: Honest question. If the French had just negotiated, no bribe, no XYZ, do the Acts still pass?

Marcus: Honestly? Nobody's fully sure. But without the war scare, I doubt the Federalists have the votes or the public behind them. The bribe demand is what made the country hold its breath. Take that away, and the whole thing looks like what it was: a party protecting itself.

Maya: Box three is landed, the Republican counter and the compact theory under it.

Marcus: The French were seizing our ships and demanding bribes in Paris. A government at war that cannot defend itself is not a government. We drew the line where every nation draws it, and we wrote the line to expire.

Haswell: They drew the line around their critics. I printed words and rode fifty miles to a jail. Tell Bache about the sunset. If the republic can't survive a newspaper, it isn't the newspapers that are the problem.

Maya: You're both stuck in 1800, so this part is mine. The verdict wasn't a resolution. It was an election. In 1800 the country threw the Federalists out and elected Jefferson. The Sedition Act had that sunset written in: it expired March 3rd, 1801, Adams's last day. Jefferson pardoned the convicted, Lyon among them. And no court struck the law down. Judicial review didn't exist yet; the Court wouldn't claim that power until 1803. The check was the ballot box.

Maya: The Alien Friends Act expired too, never renewed. The Alien Enemies Act is the odd one out. It's still on the books. The template was set here: a frightened government criminalizes dissent, the dissenters go to jail, and the voters get the last word.

Maya: The verb trap: the exam baits you with "repeal." Jefferson didn't repeal the Acts. He let them lapse. Lapse, not repeal.

Maya: A scoring move: when the question is how the Sedition Act was checked, the answer is the election of 1800, not the courts. In 1798 the Court couldn't strike down a federal law.

Maya: Box four is landed, the election of 1800 as the verdict. That's all four.

Maya: Four boxes, let's land them. One: the XYZ Affair. French agents demand a bribe, Adams publishes the dispatches, and the Quasi-War follows, 1798 to 1800. Two: the four Acts. Naturalization, Alien Friends, Alien Enemies, and the Sedition Act, the speech crime. Three: the Republican counter. Kentucky's nullification, Virginia's interposition, compact theory underneath. Four: the election of 1800. Jefferson wins, the Sedition Act expires on Adams's last day, and the pardons follow.

Maya: Wait, Kentucky was Madison's, the interpose one. No. Kentucky was Jefferson, nullification. Virginia was Madison, interposition. I had it backwards for a second.

Marcus: You did. Jefferson, Kentucky, nullify. Madison, Virginia, interpose. The hotter doctrine from the hotter head.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it.

Maya: First. A 1798 document says that when the federal government assumes powers never delegated, quote, "its acts are unauthoritative, void, and of no force." End quote. Which resolution, and what doctrine? [15-second pause]

Maya: The Kentucky Resolutions, Jefferson in secret. The doctrine is nullification — the quote only makes sense if the Constitution is a compact among the states, a deal the states can police. And the giveaway is the verb "void."

Maya: Second. Adams publishes the XYZ dispatches, the agents' names replaced by X, Y, and Z. Explain the effect on public opinion, and connect it to the Quasi-War. [18-second pause]

Maya: Publication turned a diplomatic insult into a national one — a country split over France rallied behind "millions for defense, but not one cent for tribute," and Congress funded the naval buildup. Outrage needs a visible trigger, and a published bribe demand was it.

Maya: Third. Sort by target: the Naturalization Act, the Alien Friends Act, the Sedition Act. Who does each hit? [20-second pause]

Maya: Naturalization hits immigrants who'd vote Republican: fourteen years instead of five. The Alien Friends Act hits foreign residents the president calls dangerous: deportation, in peacetime. The Sedition Act hits Republican editors and critics: jail for words. Voters, foreigners, and the press. Three moves, one strategy.

Maya: One more, fast. The Sedition Act died by repeal, court ruling, or expiration? [6-second pause] Expiration. It lapsed on Adams's last day, and Jefferson pardoned the convicted.

Maya: Criticism isn't treason. The voters said so in 1800, and every frightened generation since has had to decide the case again. [held breath — do not rush it]

Maya: Next time: the Revolution of 1800, the tie that threw the election into the House, and the first peaceful transfer of power between parties. Check your boxes.
