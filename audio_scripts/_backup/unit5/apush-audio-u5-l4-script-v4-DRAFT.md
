# Pronunciation: DRED SKOT (Dred Scott), TAY-nee (Taney), KUR-tiss (Curtis), SAN-ford (Sandford)

Maya: Last time: Kansas bled, a senator got caned at his desk, and the party system shattered. This time the Supreme Court tries to end the slavery debate forever, in a single ruling. Spoiler: it goes badly. Three boxes on your sheet: Dred Scott's case, the three holdings, and the backfire. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes, and you'll check all three off.

Marcus: 1857. An enslaved man named Dred Scott has been living on free soil for years. Illinois. Wisconsin Territory. Places where the Missouri Compromise said slavery couldn't exist. He sues for his freedom. The case reaches the Supreme Court. And the Chief Justice decides to use it to settle the slavery question once and for all. The case name itself carries a typo, by the way. It's Dred Scott against Sandford, with one s. A clerk misspelled the defendant's name, and it stuck for a hundred and seventy years.

Maya: Who was Dred Scott? As a person, not a case name.

Marcus: Born enslaved in Virginia around 1799. His owner, an army surgeon named John Emerson, got posted to Illinois and then to Wisconsin Territory, and took Scott along. Scott lived there for years, married Harriet, another enslaved person, and they had two daughters. When Emerson died, Scott tried to buy his family's freedom. The widow refused. So he sued.

Maya: On what grounds?

Marcus: That living on free soil had made him free. It was an established legal principle, and enslaved people had won freedom suits on those grounds before. Scott had every reason to think he'd win. The case bounced through Missouri courts for a decade before it reached Washington. He actually won at first, in 1850. Then the Missouri Supreme Court reversed it in 1852, writing that times now are not as they were when the earlier freedom suits were decided.

Maya: Ten years. I once spent three weeks arguing with a teacher over a grade. Ten years for a freedom suit puts that in perspective.

Marcus: And then Chief Justice Roger Taney uses the case to blow up the country. By settling it, I mean making it worse.

Maya: I figured.

Marcus: Taney is eighty years old, a former slaveholder from Maryland, Andrew Jackson's old Treasury Secretary. He'd freed his own enslaved people years earlier. He genuinely believes he's saving the Union by taking the slavery question away from politicians who keep making it worse.

Maya: Almost sympathetic. Almost.

Marcus: He doesn't just rule against Scott. He issues three holdings that detonate the entire political system.

Maya: So what did the Court actually hold?

Marcus: Holding one: Dred Scott isn't a citizen. Black people, enslaved or free, have no rights the white man is bound to respect. They can't sue in federal court. The case shouldn't even be heard.

Maya: "No rights which the white man was bound to respect." Everybody quotes that line.

Marcus: In full, it's worse. Taney writes that Black people were "a subordinate and inferior class of beings" with "no rights or privileges but such as those who held the power and the Government might choose to grant them." That sentence has haunted the Court ever since.

Maya: Holding two?

Marcus: Congress has no power to ban slavery in the territories. Slaveholders can't be deprived of their property, even on free soil. Holding three, the consequence: the Missouri Compromise was unconstitutional all along. Every territory in America is now open to slavery.

Maya: That was the plan, anyway. Did it work?

Marcus: Taney's strategy was to take slavery out of politics by judicial fiat. If the Court says Congress can't touch it, the Wilmot Proviso fights end. Popular sovereignty ends. The whole territorial debate is over, because the Constitution already answered it.

Maya: But it does the opposite.

Marcus: Because the North reads it as the ultimate Slave Power overreach. The Fugitive Slave Act already forced Northerners to help catch runaways. Now the Court says there's no square inch of American soil safe from slavery's expansion. Republicans call it proof the Court is captured. And Lincoln starts talking about a conspiracy to nationalize slavery.

Maya: What did Lincoln actually say?

Marcus: June 1857, Springfield. Lincoln answers Taney directly. He argues the Founders never meant the Constitution to protect slavery forever. They put it, in his words, on a course of ultimate extinction. And he warns that the next Dred Scott decision will say the states can't ban slavery either, that slavery will become national.

Maya: Was he right to worry?

Marcus: Taney's logic pointed that way, even if the Court never got there. And Lincoln turned the decision into a weapon. The House Divided speech, the debates with Douglas, all of it flows from Dred Scott.

Maya: And the South?

Marcus: Emboldened. They move from defending slavery where it exists to demanding a federal slave code for the territories. If the Court says Congress can't ban it, they argue, then Congress should actively protect it. That demand pushes every remaining moderate toward the Republicans.

Maya: So who pushed back on Taney?

Marcus: There was a dissent, and it matters. Justice Benjamin Curtis of Massachusetts. He shows that free Black men voted in several states at the Founding. They were citizens. Taney's history was invented.

Maya: Wait, so Taney just made up the history?

Marcus: Curtis dismantles it point by point. He argues that since the Court found Scott had no standing, it had no business ruling on anything else. Lincoln carried Curtis's dissent in his pocket during the debates with Douglas. It became the Republicans' legal bible.

Maya: Did Curtis pay a price for it?

Marcus: He resigned that September. Historians still argue how much of it was the case and how much was money and misery on the circuit, but the timing tells you what he thought.
Marcus: Curtis stayed in the history books anyway: every account of this case still quotes his dissent as the model of honest judging.

Maya: The Fourteenth Amendment's first sentence is basically a rebuttal — and this is one I actually know. "All persons born or naturalized in the United States are citizens." Dred Scott, reversed.

Marcus: Eleven years later, written into the Constitution. The amendment's citizenship clause exists because Taney said citizenship was impossible. You just connected the case to Reconstruction in one sentence.

Maya: So where does this leave us? Worst decision in Court history, or most consequential?

Marcus: Historians argue both, and they might be the same thing here. Some say Taney was a pro-slavery ideologue who hijacked the Court. Others say he genuinely tried to prevent a war and miscalculated catastrophically. Either way, the decision is taught as the moment the Court tried to solve a political problem with law and made it unsolvable. It killed every middle position. Popular sovereignty? The Court just said territorial votes don't matter. Compromise? About what? The Court said the answer was already in the Constitution.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: Dred Scott's case. Born enslaved around 1799, lives on free soil in Illinois and Wisconsin Territory, sues on the free-soil principle, fights for a decade to reach the Court.

Marcus: Checked.

Maya: Two: the three holdings. No Black citizenship, no congressional power over slavery in the territories, the Missouri Compromise unconstitutional. Seven to two, by the way.

Marcus: On the sheet.

Maya: Three: the backfire. Taney tries to take slavery out of politics. The North sees Slave Power conspiracy, Lincoln warns of nationalized slavery, the South demands a federal slave code, and Curtis's dissent becomes the Republican playbook. Every compromise position dies.

Marcus: And that's the set.

Maya: Two questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One: name the three holdings.

[5-second pause]

Maya: One, Black Americans are not citizens and can't sue in federal court. Two, Congress has no power to ban slavery in the territories. Three, the Missouri Compromise was unconstitutional, so every territory is open to slavery.

Maya: Two: a historian quotes Curtis's dissent, noting that free Black men voted in several states when the Constitution was written. The prompt asks: what's the point of including this source?

[5-second pause]

Maya: The point is that Taney's history was invented. Curtis proves Black citizenship existed at the Founding, which means the decision wasn't law discovering the truth. It was politics dressed as law.

Maya: One more thing. Did Scott ever get free?

Marcus: He did. After the ruling, Taylor Blow, his original owner's son, bought him and freed him. Dred Scott lived as a free man for about a year and a half before dying of tuberculosis in 1858. He never saw the war his case helped cause.

Maya: Check your three boxes. Episode four in the books. Next time: Lincoln and Douglas debate for a Senate seat, seven times, in front of thousands of people, and the loser wins bigger than the winner.

Marcus: The Court tried to end the argument —

Maya: and scheduled the war instead.
