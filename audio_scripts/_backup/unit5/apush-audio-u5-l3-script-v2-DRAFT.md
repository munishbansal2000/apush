# Pronunciation: toh-PEE-kuh (Topeka), leh-KOMP-tun (Lecompton), BOR-der RUF-ee-unz (Border Ruffians), pot-uh-WOT-uh-mee (Pottawatomie)

Marcus: May 21st, 1856. Dawn over Lawrence, Kansas. A column of pro-slavery men rides in from the east, some said eight hundred of them, led by a federal marshal carrying a court order. By noon the town's two newspapers are wrecked, their presses smashed and their type thrown into the Kansas River. The Free State Hotel, the pride of the town, takes cannon fire and then burns. When the column rides out, the strangest fact of the day: only one man is dead. A pro-slavery raider, killed by accident, hit by falling masonry from the hotel his own side was destroying.

Marcus: To understand how a town gets sacked over an election, rewind two years. 1854. Senator Stephen Douglas of Illinois wants a transcontinental railroad, and he wants it running through Chicago. A railroad through your state means money: land values explode, businesses flood in, the future arrives by rail. But Congress has to organize the territory west of Illinois first, and the South wants the railroad on a southern route, through Texas, through their country.

Marcus: So Douglas makes a deal. He introduces the Kansas-Nebraska Act: two new territories, Kansas and Nebraska, carved out of the unorganized plains. And the slavery question? Let the settlers decide. Popular sovereignty. Douglas owns a small fortune in Chicago real estate, so this isn't abstract for him. A railroad through Chicago makes him rich. A southern route makes him nothing. There's just one problem. All of it sits north of the thirty-six thirty line, where the Missouri Compromise banned slavery thirty years ago.

Marcus: Douglas repeals the line. Thirty years of compromise, gone in a paragraph. The North reads it as proof of what it's starting to call the Slave Power: the South reaching into free soil. And Douglas never answers the practical questions. If slaveholders are already there and the territory votes free, what happens to the enslaved people? What happens to the owners' property? He never says.

Marcus: What happens next is predictable to everyone except, apparently, the United States Senate. Thousands flood into Kansas. The fraud starts immediately: in the 1855 territorial elections, more than six thousand votes get cast in a territory with only about three thousand legal voters. The pro-slavery legislature that results is one the free-state settlers refuse to recognize. From Missouri come the Border Ruffians, pro-slavery men who cross the border on election day, vote, and go home. They brag about it in their own newspapers. One Missouri paper says it will send enough men to outvote the abolitionists ten to one. From New England come the free-state settlers, organized by the Emigrant Aid Company, carrying rifles and Bibles. The rifles arrive in crates marked as books. The settlers call them Beecher's Bibles, after the preacher whose congregation paid for them. The repeal radicalizes Northern Democrats too. The Anti-Nebraska movement springs up overnight, and out of it will come a new party.

Marcus: Most actual Kansans want a free state. The Ruffians vote it slave anyway. Nobody can even tell who is eligible to vote. Two governments form. The pro-slavery men set up at Lecompton, and their legislature passes what the free-state papers call the bogus laws: it becomes a capital crime to help a runaway, a felony to even question the right to hold slaves in Kansas. The free-state men set up at Topeka. Each calls the other illegitimate. Each is right.

Marcus: The fuse is already lit. Back in April, Sheriff Samuel Jones rides into Lawrence to arrest free-state leaders, and somebody shoots him. He survives, but a pro-slavery grand jury indicts the whole free-state leadership and declares the town's hotel and newspapers public nuisances. That's the court order the marshal carries in May. And in May of 1856, the pro-slavery government gets its order, its marshal, and its eight hundred men, and marches on Lawrence. The sack you just heard about. The presses destroyed. The hotel burned. One man dead by accident.

Marcus: The news hits Washington like a cannon shot, and the telegraph carries it everywhere. And a senator from Massachusetts decides to answer it with words. Charles Sumner takes the Senate floor on May 19th and speaks for two days. The speech is called "The Crime Against Kansas." It denounces the Slave Power, and then it gets personal. He doesn't spare Douglas either, calling him slavery's Sancho Panza. Two days of this, printed in every Northern paper. Sumner mocks Andrew Butler, the elderly senator from South Carolina, for taking what Sumner calls the harlot Slavery as his mistress.

Marcus: Butler's nephew hears about it, and he is not in a forgiving mood. Preston Brooks. Thirty-seven. A congressman from South Carolina, a veteran of the Mexican War. He doesn't challenge Sumner to a duel, which would have been the honorable Southern way. On May 22nd, he walks into the Senate chamber, waits until Sumner is seated at his desk, and beats him with a cane. The desk is bolted to the floor. Sumner tears it loose trying to escape. Brooks keeps striking until the cane breaks.

Marcus: The Senate watches. One senator lunges to help and gets a pistol in the face from one of Brooks's allies. Sumner lies bloody and unconscious. He won't return to his seat for three years. Massachusetts leaves the chair empty rather than replace him. The North makes Sumner a martyr for the free-soil cause. The South sends Brooks dozens of new canes. A country that can't agree whether beating a senator unconscious is wrong has stopped being one country.

Marcus: While Sumner bleeds in Washington, Kansas bleeds worse. John Brown has come to Kansas with his sons: a militant abolitionist who believes God has called him to end slavery by force. When the news of Lawrence reaches him, he says it's time to fight fire with fire. On the night of May 24th, Brown leads a small party, four of his sons among them, to three cabins along Pottawatomie Creek. They drag five pro-slavery settlers out into the dark and kill them with broadswords. Swords, so the gunshots won't wake the neighbors.

Marcus: It's murder, and Brown knows it. He believes slavery is already a war against Black people, and he's answering it. Kansas splits down the middle over him: hero to some, fanatic to others. Brown flees the territory with a price on his head, already becoming a legend the abolitionist papers call Captain Brown. And Kansas descends into guerrilla war. Raids and reprisals, burned cabins, ambushed patrols. The Northern papers give it a name that sticks: Bleeding Kansas. About fifty Americans die before it burns itself out in 1859. The lesson is written in blood. Popular sovereignty didn't let settlers decide peacefully. It moved the violence from Congress to the frontier.

Marcus: And the party system shatters with it. The Whigs, the party of Clay and Webster, are destroyed in the 1854 elections, punished for failing to stop the Slave Power. Out of the wreckage rise two new parties. The Republicans, born in 1854 in schoolhouses and under the oaks of Michigan and Wisconsin: a Northern party, pledged to stop slavery's expansion. Not to abolish it, just to contain it. And the Know-Nothings, the American Party really, built on hatred of Irish Catholic immigrants. Ask a member about the secret organization and he says "I know nothing," and the name sticks. They win governors and mayors in 1854. Then slavery swallows every other issue, and by 1856 they're gone, most of them absorbed into the Republicans. Brooks himself resigns after the caning, and South Carolina re-elects him unanimously. That tells you everything about where the country stands.

Marcus: The 1856 election proves it. The Democrat Buchanan wins the presidency with Southern votes. The Republican Fremont, a famous western explorer, sweeps the North and almost wins as a brand-new party. The Know-Nothings split the difference and die. Three sectional candidates, three sectional verdicts.

Marcus: By 1856 the old politics is dead. The national parties, with their Northern wings and Southern wings holding together, have become impossible. You can't have one party that's both pro-slavery and anti-slavery. The Republicans don't try. They're the North's party, and the South hears their platform, slavery is wrong and it must not expand, as a declaration of war.

Marcus: So here is the one thing to carry forward. For thirty years, America settled the slavery question with compromises: lines on maps, packages of bills, votes in chambers. The Missouri line, the Compromise of 1850, popular sovereignty itself: three answers, all dead. Kansas proved the machinery was broken. When the votes are fraudulent, when the senator is caned at his desk, when the cabins burn at Pottawatomie, politics has failed.

Marcus: And when politics fails —

Marcus: violence votes.
