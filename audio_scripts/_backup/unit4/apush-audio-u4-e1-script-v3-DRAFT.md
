Maya: Last time: 1800. The republic survived its first stress test, and Jefferson moved in promising small government. Three years later, he bought half a continent.

Maya: Grab the episode sheet. Three boxes: Saint-Domingue, the Louisiana Purchase, strict construction. Eight minutes, and you'll check all three off. Start here: the small-government president stretched the Constitution like taffy, and he'd tell you it worried him.

Marcus: The whole episode hangs on that contradiction: Jefferson believed the federal government could only do what the Constitution explicitly said. In short: strict construction. Buying foreign territory? The Constitution doesn't mention it anywhere.

Maya: So by his own rules, impossible.

Marcus: By his own rules, no. But the opportunity was too big, which tells you how principles bend under pressure.

Maya: But why was France selling? Napoleon doesn't strike me as the garage-sale type.

Marcus: He wasn't. He dreamed of a French empire, with Louisiana as the breadbasket feeding the Caribbean sugar islands. The whole plan depended on one island: Saint-Domingue.

Maya: Saint-Domingue. That's modern Haiti, right?

Marcus: The French colony that became Haiti — the richest colony in the world, worked by enslaved Africans under conditions so brutal the death rate outran the birth rate. In 1791 the enslaved population rose up. Eventually their leader was Toussaint Louverture, a formerly enslaved man turned brilliant general.

Maya: A slave revolt beating Napoleon's France? Really?

Marcus: It nearly wasn't. Napoleon sent his brother-in-law with around 50,000 troops to crush it. The Haitians fought them to a standstill, but the real killer was yellow fever — the disease tore through French troops who'd never encountered it, and tens of thousands died.

Maya: So mosquitoes beat Napoleon.

Marcus: Mosquitoes and Louverture's army together — don't shortchange the Haitians. But yes, by 1803 the French were completely finished. Louverture himself had been tricked into capture and died in a French prison, but the revolution went on. Haiti declared independence in 1804, the second independent nation in the Americas.

Maya: And Louverture himself?

Marcus: The French lured him to a parley in summer 1802, seized him, and shipped him to France in chains. He died in April 1803 in a freezing mountain fortress, Fort de Joux — the same month the Louisiana treaty was signed. He never saw the independence he'd made possible.

Maya: And Leclerc?

Marcus: Dead of yellow fever by November 1802. The island killed their general and their plan in the same season.

Maya: And Napoleon looks at Louisiana and thinks, what's the point?

Marcus: Exactly. Without Saint-Domingue, Louisiana's just empty land. Plus war with Britain was back on, and Napoleon needed cash fast. So the breadbasket went on the market.

Maya: But Spain had just handed Louisiana to France. Could France even sell it?

Marcus: Madrid said no: Spain returned it in 1800 on the understanding it wouldn't go to a third power, and protested when the sale leaked. France sold it anyway.

Maya: Three flags in three weeks: Spain's down in New Orleans on November 30, 1803, America's up twenty days later.

Marcus: Most people actually living there heard about it after the fact.

Maya: Checking box one: Saint-Domingue.

Marcus: Check it. Now the negotiation, the fun part. Jefferson sent Robert Livingston to Paris with one job: buy New Orleans. Just the city.

Maya: Why just the city?

Marcus: The Mississippi. Farmers west of the Appalachians floated grain, pork, and whiskey down to New Orleans and out to the world. Whoever held that port held the West's economy by the throat.

Maya: So New Orleans is the cork in the bottle.

Marcus: Good image. Jefferson sent James Monroe with authority to offer up to $10 million for the city and a strip around it. Then Napoleon's minister Talleyrand stunned them: buy all of Louisiana.

Maya: All of it? How big was it, even?

Marcus: Nobody knew. The price was $15 million, roughly three cents an acre, for about 828,000 square miles. It doubled the country in a day. Livingston and Monroe didn't have authority for anything close to this. They signed anyway.

Maya: Without asking? Brave or insane, maybe both.

Marcus: They figured if they waited, Napoleon might change his mind. So they signed and let Jefferson sort it out back home.

Marcus: Box three: the constitutional crisis. Jefferson was genuinely torn. He wrote that the Constitution made no provision for holding foreign territory, and he actually drafted an amendment to authorize it.

Maya: Did the amendment go anywhere?

Marcus: Nowhere. His advisors warned that by the time an amendment passed, Napoleon could walk away. So Jefferson simply swallowed his principles and sent the treaty to the Senate. It passed — and he admitted privately the whole thing stretched past his reading of the Constitution.

Maya: The strict constructionist stretched it like taffy.

Marcus: And the money part never makes the highlight reel. The United States didn't have fifteen million dollars. They borrowed it — from British and Dutch banks, at six percent annual interest. The republic went into debt to double in size.

Maya: Borrowing from the British to buy from the French. That's an awkward family dinner.

Marcus: Then the Senate vote: twenty-four to seven. He'd been racing a clock — miss the October deadline and the deal could die. An amendment would have taken months. So the amendment died in the desk — killed by a calendar, not a principle.

Maya: The Constitution got stretched by a deadline. The whole episode in one line.

Marcus: The irony writes itself: he did the thing he'd spent his career saying presidents couldn't do.

Maya: What did the Federalists say? Thrilled?

Marcus: Furious. Not about the Constitution, about power. New western states would vote Republican, burying Federalist New England forever.

Maya: Too much America. New complaint just dropped.

Maya: But what did they actually buy? Eight hundred twenty-eight thousand square miles of what, and where did it end?

Marcus: Nobody could say; the treaty drew no map. Spain insisted the Floridas weren't included, the Americans claimed West Florida was. Sixteen years of arguing, until the 1819 Adams–Onís treaty settled the border and Spain gave up Florida entirely.

Maya: Bought from whom, though? France didn't exactly live there.

Marcus: The purchase bought France's claim. Dozens of Native nations actually lived there, and had for centuries.

Maya: And the United States spent the next century clearing that title, treaty by treaty and war by war. Nobody asked the people who lived there.

Marcus: Then Jefferson sent Lewis and Clark to see what he'd bought. The Corps of Discovery, 1804 to 1806: up the Missouri, across the Rockies, to the Pacific and back, with the young Shoshone woman Sacagawea as guide and interpreter.

Maya: Sacagawea, the dollar coin.

Marcus: The gold one. The expedition came back with maps, journals, and crates of specimens nobody in Washington had seen. It was science and sovereignty in a single trip: we mapped it, so it's ours.

Maya: Did the expedition matter, or just the adventure story?

Marcus: It mattered. The maps and journals gave the United States ammunition against Britain and Spain: we were there, we mapped it, we named it. Science on the surface, sovereignty underneath.

Maya: Recap: three boxes, let's land them.

Maya: Box one: Saint-Domingue, the Haitian Revolution. Enslaved people rise up under Toussaint... Louverture, right? Yellow fever wipes out Napoleon's army, France quits, and that's what puts Louisiana on the market.

Marcus: Louverture, right.

Maya: Box two: the Louisiana Purchase. 1803, $15 million, three cents an acre, doubles the country overnight. Livingston and Monroe went to buy New Orleans and came home with everything. Plus Lewis and Clark under this box: 1804 to 1806, Sacagawea guiding them to the Pacific.

Marcus: Good.

Maya: Box three: strict construction. Jefferson's own rule said the government can only do what's listed. The purchase wasn't listed. He did it anyway, and the amendment he drafted never happened.

Marcus: All three boxes.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. First: a historian claims the Haitian Revolution was the decisive cause of the Louisiana Purchase. Does the evidence support that?

[5-second pause]

Maya: Yes. Without the disaster in Saint-Domingue, Napoleon keeps his breadbasket plan and never sells.

Marcus: Strong.

Maya: Second, stimulus style. You read this: "The Constitution has made no provision for our holding foreign territory, still less for incorporating foreign nations into our Union." Who wrote it, and what's the point of the source?

[5-second pause]

Maya: Jefferson, writing privately in 1803. Even the president who made the deal believed it went past his limits — the contradiction in his own hand.

Marcus: Nailed it.

Maya: Third: Lewis and Clark went up the Missouri in 1804, before the purchase ink was even dry. What was their real job?

[5-second pause]

Maya: Map and study the territory, and plant the flag for American sovereignty. Knowledge was the real claim.

Marcus: Three for three.

Maya: Check your three boxes. The small-government president bought half a continent — principles bend when the map gets bigger. Next time: John Marshall turns the weakest branch into the boss of everyone.
