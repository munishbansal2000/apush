# U4-E15 — Maya + Marcus + Rafael (Fish Audio)
# Episode 15: The Mexican-American War. ~8.5 min.
# Draft v1.
# Read note: Debate format. Maya moderates. Marcus defends Polk's case for the war; Rafael is a fictional composite voicing the Mexican civilian perspective. No pause tags in this episode. Strip this header and the read note before TTS.
# Pronunciation: Guadalupe Hidalgo (gwah-dah-LOOP-ay ee-DAL-goh), Veracruz (veh-rah-KROOS)

Maya: Last time: Texas joined the Union, and the border dispute was the fuse. This time: the explosion. A president tells Congress that American blood was shed on American soil. And a freshman congressman from Illinois stands up and asks: show me the spot. Eight minutes on the war Lincoln called a lie.

Maya: Marcus, you're Polk's defender. Rafael, you speak for Mexico. Opening statements. Marcus.

Marcus: [fierce] The facts first. On April twenty-fifth, eighteen forty-six, Mexican cavalry surrounded an American patrol under Captain Seth Thornton in Texas. Eleven Americans killed, dozens captured. Texas was American soil. Congress had annexed it, the people had ratified it. So when President Polk told Congress on May eleventh that Mexico had "invaded our territory and shed American blood upon American soil," he was describing what happened. Congress agreed. Two days later, it declared war. Nations don't get to ambush your soldiers and call it a misunderstanding.

Rafael: [solemn] Now the other facts. Whose soil? The patrol was in the strip between the Nueces River and the Rio Grande. Mexico's boundary was the Nueces. That strip was disputed, and Polk knew it. He ordered General Taylor's army into the strip and built a fort across from Matamoros. Then he waited. Mexican soldiers fought bravely for a government that couldn't pay or feed them. When the clash came, he had his headline. "American blood on American soil" is a great sentence. It isn't a true one. The blood was shed on contested ground, and Polk put it there.

Maya: Before the shooting, Polk tried to buy his way in, right?

Marcus: He sent John Slidell to Mexico City with an offer: buy California and New Mexico, settle the Texas border. Mexico refused to even receive him. Polk called that the last straw.

Rafael: Mexico refused because Slidell came to buy what wasn't for sale, after the United States had already absorbed Texas. Insult to injury. You don't negotiate with someone holding your province.

Maya: So the whole war turns on a hundred and fifty miles of riverbank. Clash one: the Thornton Affair. Walk me through the day.

Marcus: Taylor's men were sitting in the disputed strip, yes, but under orders to defend Texas. Thornton took about eighty dragoons on a scouting patrol near Rancho de Carricitos. Mexican cavalry, hundreds strong, surrounded them in the chaparral. Eleven dead, the rest captured. You can argue about the map all day. You can't argue that Mexican troops killed American soldiers.

Maya: Eighty against hundreds? Not a patrol. A target.

Rafael: I can argue about why they were there to be killed. Polk wanted California and New Mexico. He'd already tried to buy them. When Mexico wouldn't sell, he sent an army to the border Mexico didn't recognize and dared it to shoot. Not defense. A trap with a flag on it. Even Ulysses Grant, who fought in this war as a young officer, later called it one of the most unjust wars ever waged.

Maya: Who opposed the war at home? Somebody must have.

Rafael: Plenty. Thoreau spent his night in jail over it, refusing the poll tax. John Quincy Adams, the former president, called it a war to extend slavery. The Massachusetts legislature declared it unconstitutional. This was never a united country marching off.

Maya: Okay, so Lincoln. Eighteen forty-seven, he's a freshman congressman, a Whig from Illinois, and he doesn't buy it. What did he actually do?

Marcus: On December twenty-second, eighteen forty-seven, Lincoln introduced the spot resolutions. He demanded Polk show Congress "whether the particular spot of soil on which the blood of our citizens was so shed, was, or was not, our own soil." Eight resolutions, one question: where exactly? Congress never voted on them. An Illinois paper called him "Spotty Lincoln," and the name stuck. It followed him. The next month, the House passed the Ashmun Amendment, declaring the war "unnecessarily and unconstitutionally begun by the President." By a handful of votes.

Rafael: Think about that. While American armies were marching on Mexico City, the House of Representatives voted that the president had started the war unconstitutionally. The war's own Congress couldn't defend how it began.

Maya: Hold on, I need to fix something in my head. The Alamo was this war, right? The war ended at the Alamo?

Marcus: No, and that's the confusion to kill. The Alamo was eighteen thirty-six, Texas's revolution. This war ended eleven years later, with American troops in Mexico City. Different war, different decade.

Maya: Got it. Eighteen thirty-six versus eighteen forty-eight. I visited the Alamo on that Texas road trip, by the way. The basement was a gift shop. I bought a coonskin cap.

Rafael: We have gift shops at our battlefields too. Ours are older. The grief is the same. It hasn't closed.

Maya: And California? Did anybody fight for that?

Marcus: Barely. Fremont's men took California with hardly a fight, before most Californios even knew there was a war.

Maya: Clash two: the campaign and the treaty. Marcus, how did it go?

Marcus: Fast and lopsided. Taylor won at Palo Alto and Resaca de la Palma in May, took Monterrey in September, and crushed Santa Anna's army at Buena Vista the next February. Then Winfield Scott, "Old Fuss and Feathers" for his love of a uniform, landed at Veracruz, won at Cerro Gordo, and marched into Mexico City in September of eighteen forty-seven. Mexico had no answer for American artillery and logistics. Outgunned, outmanned, outmaneuvered. It was over in twenty-one months. And most of the dying wasn't from bullets. Disease killed far more soldiers than combat, on both sides. The war's real killer was the camp, not the battlefield. Thirteen thousand Americans died, most of disease. Mexico lost far more, soldiers and civilians both.

Maya: "Old Fuss and Feathers." Really what they called him?

Marcus: Really. The troops named him for the plumage. He didn't mind.

Rafael: Less than two years, and my country lost half its territory. At Chapultepec, outside Mexico City, teenage cadets died defending the castle. Mexico remembers them as the Niños Héroes, the Boy Heroes. Your history books skip them. Ours don't. The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, February second, eighteen forty-eight: Mexico recognized the Rio Grande boundary and sold California and New Mexico, the Mexican Cession, for fifteen million dollars, plus a few million more in American damage claims the United States took over. Nine days before the treaty was signed, gold was found at Sutter's Mill in California. Polk paid fifteen million for land about to make other men rich. The treaty promised that Mexicans living in the ceded lands could keep their property and become American citizens. Most of those promises evaporated. The land grants were challenged, the courts took decades, and families who'd held land for generations lost it to lawyers.

Marcus: Polk got what he wanted: a continental nation, coast to coast. He'd promised four goals in one term and hit all four.

Maya: Four goals? Name them.

Marcus: Lower the tariff. Restore the Independent Treasury, the government's own vaults. Settle Oregon with Britain. And take California. One term, four promises, all kept. But Rafael's right about the price nobody put in the treaty. The same year the war started, a congressman named David Wilmot attached a proviso to a war funding bill: slavery would be banned from any land taken from Mexico. It passed the House and died in the Senate. The war for land became a war over slavery's future. The detonation.

Maya: Closing statements. Marcus.

Marcus: [fierce] Judge it by results. The United States became a continental power. Secured borders. An open West. Polk saw what the country needed and took it. History rewards the bold.

Rafael: [quiet] Judge it by cost. Half my country, thousands dead, promises broken before the ink dried. You call it bold. We call it the intervention. The land is still there. So is the wound.

Maya: Verdict time. Militarily, this was a triumph: twenty-one months, two brilliant campaigns, a treaty that doubled the nation's size. Morally and politically, it poisoned everything. A president manufactured the cause. Congress nearly said so out loud. And the voters rewarded the general, not the president. In eighteen forty-eight the Whigs nominated Zachary Taylor, the war hero, and he won. The victory handed America a question it couldn't answer: would the new land be slave or free? The Wilmot Proviso asked it in eighteen forty-six. The Compromise of eighteen fifty would try to answer. Every territory became a battlefield over slavery's future, and the battlefield moved from Mexico to Congress. The war was won in Mexico and lost in Washington. That's the verdict.

Maya: That's Episode 15. Next time: the whole unit in one sitting. Ten questions, no mercy, Maya and Jay at full speed. The Unit 4 cram.

Marcus: Won the war, lost the argument.

Maya: And the bill came due in eighteen sixty-one. With interest.
