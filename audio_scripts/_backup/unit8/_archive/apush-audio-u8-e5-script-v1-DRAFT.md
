# APUSH Unit 8, Episode 5: Suburbia — The Postwar Boom
# Script v1 DRAFT — Interview (Maya + Marcus)

# Pronunciation: Levittown (LEV-it-town), Sunbelt, redlining, Galbraith (gal-BRAYTH), Kerouac (keh-roo-ACK)

Maya: A deed in Levittown, New York, nineteen forty-seven: a brand-new house for sixty-nine ninety, with a clause buried in the fine print saying a Black family can never live in it. Last time: McCarthyism, the scare that ate its own. This time: the machine that built modern America, and decided who got to live in it. The GI Bill, the assembly-line suburb, and the great tilt south. Three boxes on the sheet: the GI Bill, Levittown, the Sunbelt shift. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes, and you'll check all three off.

Marcus: Start with the money, because everything else flows from it. Nineteen forty-four, Congress passes the Servicemen's Readjustment Act: the GI Bill. Sixteen million Americans served in World War Two, and now the government makes them an offer: we'll pay for college, and we'll back your home loan at four percent interest, thirty years to pay, little or nothing down.

Maya: What an offer. Four percent, thirty years, and nothing down?

Marcus: College enrollments double. By nineteen forty-seven, veterans are half of every college campus in America. Homeownership jumps from four in ten families to more than six in ten. Some historians call it the largest wealth transfer in American history.

Maya: Okay, but "largest wealth transfer," to whom, exactly? Because that deed in the cold open is making me suspicious.

Marcus: Hold that suspicion. You're going to need it for box three's fine print. But first, yes: overwhelmingly, the money flowed to white veterans. For now, the scale: millions of families who'd grown up renting walk into homeownership in a decade. There's the demand. Now meet the supply.

Maya: William Levitt. The Henry Ford of houses.

Marcus: Levitt takes Ford's assembly line and aims it at a Long Island potato field. Twenty-six steps, crews moving lot to lot, each crew doing one job. At peak speed, a finished house every sixteen minutes. Seventeen thousand of them. The first ones sell in nineteen forty-seven for six thousand nine hundred ninety dollars, appliances included.

Maya: A house every sixteen minutes. That's not construction, that's manufacturing.

Marcus: No basement, tiny lot, two bedrooms. But yours. Veterans lined up for days just to buy one. And the houses fill with kids: the baby boom runs from nineteen forty-six to sixty-four, more than seventy million births, the biggest generation in American history.

Maya: Sixteen minutes. My grandpa built his own garage and it took him three summers. He'd have had feelings about this.

Marcus: Every grandpa did. And here's what the new life felt like from the inside: the house comes with a washing machine, refrigerator, television. The television is quietly the most important appliance in the house. Three networks: CBS, NBC, ABC. All beaming the same shows into millions of identical living rooms. Leave It to Beaver: the perfect suburban family, dad at work, mom in pearls doing dishes. I Love Lucy sneaks in a working woman with show-business dreams, but the message is conformity: this is what normal looks like.

Maya: Huh. Wait — "under God" in the pledge. That wasn't always there, was it? I said it every morning in elementary school.

Marcus: Good catch, and most people miss it. Congress adds "under God" in nineteen fifty-four, and a couple years later makes "In God We Trust" the national motto. It's the Cold War in the classroom: the Soviets are godless communists, so Americans get officially godly. Billy Graham's televised revivals are packing stadiums at the same time. Faith, family, flag — the suburban package deal.

Maya: And not everybody bought the package.

Marcus: Right. The Beats — poets and writers like Jack Kerouac, whose novel On the Road made wandering sound holier than a mortgage. The economist John Kenneth Galbraith, who wrote The Affluent Society in nineteen fifty-eight and argued America had private wealth and public squalor: shiny cars, crumbling schools. And the teenagers, who invented rock and roll as a weapon against their parents' living rooms. The conformity was real. So was the revolt against it.

Maya: Okay, so box two is the suburb machine. But the map itself moved too, right? The Sunbelt.

Marcus: Box three, and it's the biggest geographic reshuffle since the frontier closed. The Sunbelt: the South and the West, California and Florida and Texas leading the parade. Three engines. One: air conditioning. The hot states were miserable in summer until cheap cooling made them livable year-round. Two: the interstates. Nineteen fifty-six, Eisenhower signs the Federal Aid Highway Act: forty-one thousand miles of highway, sold as Cold War military necessity, used as suburban commute.

Maya: And three: just follow the Pentagon's money.

Marcus: Three: defense dollars. The Cold War is a jobs program, and the Pentagon builds it in the South and West: aircraft plants, bases, the space program. Millions follow the jobs south and west.

Maya: So it wasn't just retirees chasing sunshine in Florida.

Marcus: That's the wrong beat people always get, and you just made it, so good. Sunshine helped, but the money moved first. And here's the political aftershock: House seats follow population. By nineteen seventy-two, the Sunbelt holds more electoral votes than the Northeast and Midwest combined. The map of American power tilts south and west, and it never tilts back. That tilt will elect presidents. Americans have always moved: west after the Civil War, north in the Great Migration, now south and west again.

Maya: Which brings us to the fine print. That deed clause. Who didn't get the house?

Marcus: Now we cash your suspicion. The exclusions were built into the machine, not accidents. Start with the maps: banks and the Federal Housing Administration graded neighborhoods, and Black neighborhoods got marked as risky, redlined, literally outlined in red, which meant no loans, no home repairs, slow decay. Levitt himself refused to sell to Black families, with the Federal Housing Administration's blessing.

Maya: And Black veterans? They fought in that same war.

Marcus: They came home from the same war and got a fraction of the same loans. In city after city the pattern repeats: white families build equity in the suburbs while Black families are locked into neighborhoods the banks won't lend to. The wealth transfer had a color line.

Maya: And the women? Because somebody had to be in those kitchens in the TV shows.

Marcus: Pushed out of the wartime factories and back toward homemaking, that was the official mood. The economy wanted their labor in the war and their domesticity after it. Not every woman accepted it. A decade later, Betty Friedan gives that frustration a famous name, and the women's movement picks up where the fifties left off. But in the fifties, the suburban ideal had one shape, and it didn't include a woman with a career or a Black family on the block.

Maya: So the boom was real and the fine print was real. Both at once.

Marcus: Both truths at once. Prosperity on a scale America had never seen, engineered by the government. And engineered to skip people.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: the GI Bill.

Marcus: Nineteen forty-four. College money and four-percent mortgages for sixteen million veterans. Homeownership jumps from four in ten to six in ten families.

Maya: Two: Levittown. Assembly-line houses, one every sixteen minutes, six thousand nine hundred ninety dollars, and the conformity culture that came with the television.

Marcus: On the sheet.

Maya: Three: the Sunbelt shift. Air conditioning, interstates, defense dollars: the map tilts south and west, and by nineteen seventy-two the Sunbelt outvotes the old industrial states.

Marcus: And the fine print under every box: redlining, the color line in the loans, the women pushed back home. Checking that one too.

Maya: The map moved, and the power moved with it.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it, two seconds each.

[2-second pause]

Maya: One: the GI Bill to Levittown, what's the chain, in one sentence?

[2-second pause]

Maya: Government-backed cheap mortgages created millions of buyers, and Levitt's assembly line built the houses they bought. Demand, meet supply.

[2-second pause]

Maya: Two: why did the Sunbelt boom, and what does air conditioning have to do with it?

[2-second pause]

Maya: Cooling made the heat livable, interstates made the move easy, and Cold War defense spending made the jobs. Sunshine was the bonus, not the engine.

[2-second pause]

Maya: Three: who did the prosperity leave out, and how?

[2-second pause]

Maya: Black families, through redlining and deed clauses that barred them from the new suburbs. And women, pushed from wartime jobs back into the homemaker mold.

Maya: Check your three boxes, fine print included. The suburbs got built, the map got tilted, and the bill for who was excluded is still being paid. Next time: the people who refused to be left out. Montgomery, Birmingham, Selma — the movement that made America keep its promises.

Marcus: The boom built the houses —

Maya: and the fine print decided who got the keys.
