Marcus: September 11, 2001. A Tuesday. The sky over New York is impossibly blue, the kind of early fall morning where the light looks clean. People are doing what they always do. Grabbing coffee. Heading to work. Boarding planes. Nobody in those morning lines knows the skyline is about to change.

Marcus: To understand how that morning landed, you have to know what America believed the night before. The 1990s had felt like a holiday from history. The Cold War was over. The economy was booming. The Gulf War had lasted weeks and played out on television. The country still sent peacekeepers to the Balkans and fought small wars, but for most Americans those stayed on the television screen. The country that woke up that Tuesday believed, deep down, that the oceans protected it and that tomorrow would look like today.

Marcus: At 8:46 a.m., American Airlines Flight 11 hit the North Tower of the World Trade Center. Seventeen minutes later, United Flight 175 hit the South Tower. After the first crash, people thought it was an accident. The second plane ended that thought. At 9:37 a.m., American Flight 77 hit the Pentagon. At 10:03 a.m., United Flight 93 crashed in a quiet field in Pennsylvania.

Marcus: Flight 93 needs its own telling. The hijackers turned the plane toward Washington. Investigators believe the target was probably the Capitol or the White House. But the passengers and crew fought back. They'd made phone calls during the flight and learned what had already happened in New York. They forced their way toward the cockpit. The plane went down in a field in Somerset County, near the town of Shanksville. Everyone aboard died. The hijackers never reached their target.

Marcus: By the end of the day, both towers had collapsed into dust. Jet fuel started the fires. Burning offices kept them raging until the structure gave way. Almost 3,000 people were dead. Nineteen hijackers, organized by al-Qaeda and its leader Osama bin Laden. Four planes. Three targets reached. One never made it, because ordinary people fought back.

Marcus: And then, briefly, the country became something it hadn't been in years. Congress stood on the Capitol steps and sang "God Bless America." The French newspaper Le Monde ran the headline "We are all Americans." Strangers lined up silently for blocks to give blood. Volunteers drove toward New York with no assignment, just the need to help.

Marcus: Three days later, President George W. Bush stood on the rubble at Ground Zero with a firefighter and a bullhorn. Rescue workers shouted that they couldn't hear him. He answered that he could hear them, and that the people who knocked these buildings down would hear from all of them soon.

Marcus: The response came in two parts, at home and abroad. At home, Congress passed the Patriot Act just weeks after the attacks. It expanded surveillance powers in concrete ways. Roving wiretaps let investigators follow a suspect across phones instead of getting a new warrant for each line. A provision called Section 215 let the government collect business records, and years later Americans learned that had included bulk phone metadata, who called whom and when. Supporters said the law closed dangerous gaps. Critics said it traded liberty for security. That argument has never really ended.

Marcus: Congress also created the Department of Homeland Security, pulling twenty-two agencies under one roof, the largest reorganization since 1947. Airport screening was federalized under the TSA. A generation grew up taking off its shoes in line, with color-coded threat levels on the evening news.

Marcus: In 2004, the 9/11 Commission's final report called the attacks a failure of imagination. The warning signs had been there. The system just couldn't picture what they meant.

Marcus: Abroad, America went to war. In October 2001, the United States invaded Afghanistan. The Taliban government had sheltered al-Qaeda and refused to hand over bin Laden. The invasion worked with startling speed. American airpower and special forces, fighting alongside Afghan allies, drove the Taliban from power by December. But then the mission expanded. It became nation-building. Holding elections. Training an Afghan army. Building schools. American troops would stay for twenty years.

Marcus: Then Iraq. In 2003, the Bush administration argued that Saddam Hussein had weapons of mass destruction and ties to al-Qaeda. The evidence was thin. United Nations weapons inspectors asked for more time. The Security Council never authorized an invasion. America invaded anyway in March 2003, alongside a small group of allies the administration called the coalition of the willing. Baghdad fell within weeks. The weapons of mass destruction were never found. The intelligence case collapsed, and with it, much of America's credibility abroad.

Marcus: Toppling Saddam was the easy part. The occupation was the hard part. An insurgency rose against the American presence, and sectarian violence tore Iraqi communities apart. By 2006, the country was sliding toward civil war. In 2007, a surge of roughly thirty thousand more troops brought the violence down sharply, though critics said it bought only quiet at a price America couldn't keep paying.

Marcus: American combat forces left Iraq in 2010, and the last troops withdrew in 2011. But the country was fragile. Out of its wreckage, with help from the chaos of Syria's civil war, rose a new enemy. ISIS, the Islamic State, seized Mosul in 2014 and declared a caliphate across parts of Iraq and Syria. American forces returned, mostly in support roles, to help destroy it. A war launched to prevent terrorism had created the conditions for a new terrorist state.

Marcus: Let's be honest about the ledger, because the costs are the part history will judge. In lives: roughly forty-five hundred American service members died in Iraq, about twenty-four hundred in Afghanistan, plus contractors and allied troops. Iraqi civilians: the estimates vary widely, but the most common ones run into the hundreds of thousands. Afghan civilians: tens of thousands. Those are ranges, because war zones don't produce tidy numbers.

Marcus: In money: the direct spending ran well past two trillion dollars. The fuller estimates reach from four to six trillion, and those include the long tail, decades of medical care for wounded veterans and the interest on the borrowed money. The wars were paid for with debt, which is one reason the bills are still arriving.

Marcus: In trust: the intelligence failure on weapons of mass destruction damaged American credibility. The world had been told the case was certain, and the certainty turned out to be wrong. Then came the photographs from Abu Ghraib prison in 2004, American soldiers abusing Iraqi detainees, and America's moral standing took another blow. Each revelation made the next argument for action harder to believe.

Marcus: The wars ended slowly, and Afghanistan's ending was the hardest to watch. In 2020, the United States signed a deal with the Taliban to leave. In August 2021, the withdrawal turned chaotic. The Afghan government collapsed. The Taliban retook Kabul in days. At the airport, crowds pressed against the gates. A bombing killed thirteen American service members and scores of Afghans. Twenty years of war ended with the country back under the people America had gone in to remove.

Marcus: So what did twenty years buy? Al-Qaeda's core leadership was decimated. Bin Laden was killed in a 2011 raid in Pakistan. There was no second attack on American soil on the scale of 9/11. Those are real achievements. But the broader goals, stable democracies in Afghanistan and Iraq and a transformed region, those didn't happen. The Middle East stayed unstable. America is still arguing about what went wrong.

Marcus: The 1990s holiday ended first. The idea that America could stand apart from the world's troubles died that morning. Government power also expanded in ways we're still untangling, from surveillance to security to the sheer size of the war effort. And the unity of September 2001 didn't survive. The Iraq debate split the country, and by the 2010s, the split had hardened.

Marcus: Historians treat 9/11 as a hinge for exactly this reason. It closed the post-Cold War era, the decade when America imagined it could set the terms of world affairs without paying much for them. It opened the age of the war on terror, of polarization, of endless arguments about what security costs and who pays for it. One morning redrew the map of American life, and we're still living inside it.

Marcus: Three questions. [2-second pause] One: why did America invade Afghanistan in 2001, and why did the mission expand? [2-second pause] The Taliban sheltered al-Qaeda — the initial goal was counterterrorism. It expanded into nation-building, which kept troops there twenty years.

[2-second pause] Two: what was the connection between 9/11 and the Iraq War, and why was it controversial? [2-second pause] The administration claimed weapons of mass destruction and al-Qaeda ties. The weapons were never found, and the United Nations never authorized the war.

[2-second pause] Three: what did the ledger of the wars look like — in lives and in money? [2-second pause] Thousands of American dead, hundreds of thousands of Iraqi civilians, trillions in debt — and credibility that never fully recovered.

Marcus: One thing to carry forward. September 11th, told straight: the sky was blue, the facts are clear. What they mean, and what they justify, is the argument America keeps having with itself.
