# U1 — Cram Session: Maya + Jay (Fish Audio)
# Episode 9: Ten Questions, One Unit. ~8 min.
# Draft v1 (from scratch).
# Read note: Cram format — no boxes on the episode sheet. Ten rapid-fire questions, the period thesis, and the DBQ prediction. No pause marks appear in this script — self-test silence is added in production. Jay is the student: he retrieves, he guesses, he gets things wrong; Maya corrects and lands the memory hooks. The em dash in the closing tagline is a held beat: leave a full breath between Jay's setup line and Maya's landing line. Do not rush it. Strip this header and the read note before TTS.
# Pronunciation: poh-toh-SEE (Potosí), tor-deh-SEE-yahs (Tordesillas), lahss KAH-sahs (Las Casas), seh-POOL-veh-dah (Sepúlveda), MEE-tah (mita), ehn-koh-mee-EHN-dah (encomienda), kah-OH-kee-ah (Cahokia), mah-LEEN-cheh (Malinche), poh-PAY (Popé), oh-ter-MEEN (Otermín)

Maya: Last time: the Pueblo Revolt of 1680, the most successful Native uprising in North American history. The empire came back chastened. This time: no new material. Ten questions, the whole unit, rapid-fire. Answer all ten out loud and you're ready. About eight minutes. Go.

Jay: Ten questions? Last week my quiz had twelve and I blanked on three.

Maya: Then here's your redemption arc. Question one: name three Native culture regions, and how their environments shaped how they lived.

Jay: Southwest: the Pueblo peoples, irrigation farmers, adobe villages, dry land so they moved water to the crops. Plains: bison hunters, nomadic, followed the herds. Northeast: the Iroquois, farming plus hunting plus fishing, longhouses, and the women ran the clans.

Maya: Matrilineal, meaning clans traced through the mother's line. Good. One more region.

Jay: The Mississippians. Cahokia. Big mounds, trade networks everywhere.

Maya: ...around Monk's Mound — a city that rivaled London in size at the time. And the engine underneath it all: maize, traveling north from Mesoamerica. No maize, no settled towns, no Cahokia. Environment writes the menu, and maize decided who got to build a city. Question one, landed.

Jay: Question two: what made Europeans able to cross the Atlantic in the 1490s? Motives and technology, both.

Maya: Start with motives.

Jay: Gold, God, and glory. The three Gs. Gold: wealth, and the Ottomans squeezed the eastern trade routes in 1453. God: convert people, crusading energy. Glory: kings competing, Renaissance showing off.

Maya: And the technology? Why 1492 and not 1392?

Jay: The caravel, fast ships. The lateen sail catching wind from different angles. The astrolabe for latitude, the compass for direction, better maps, gunpowder.

Maya: Plus the moment: Spain fresh out of the Reconquista, warrior culture, centralized crowns, Portugal's navigation school already mapping the African coast. Then the pope draws a line. Tordesillas, 1494: everything west to Spain, east to Portugal. France and England ignored it completely. Motive, machine, moment. Miss any one of the three and the ships stay home.

Jay: Question three: what moved each way in the Columbian Exchange, and what was its deadliest cargo?

Maya: West: wheat, sugar, horses, cattle, pigs. East: maize, potatoes, tomatoes, tobacco. The deadliest cargo was invisible. Smallpox, measles, influenza sailing west into populations with no immunity. Virgin-soil epidemics. Up to nine in ten dead in the hardest-hit places.

Jay: Did anything deadly go east?

Maya: Maybe one thing. Some historians think syphilis traveled east, but nobody's fully sure. The ledger is brutally one-sided. Europe got the calories and the wealth. The Americas got demographic collapse. Africa got pulled into the slave trade to replace the dying Native labor. Crosby gave it the name in 1972: the Columbian Exchange. The biggest biological event in human history, and nobody planned it.

Jay: Question four: how did Cortés topple the Aztec Empire with, like, five hundred men?

Maya: Take a swing.

Jay: Steel, horses, guns. Technology, right? Five hundred against an empire, it's got to be the tech.

Maya: Myth. The real answer has four parts, and steel is the smallest. One: alliances. The Tlaxcalans hated the Aztecs, so hundreds of thousands of Native fighters joined Cortés. Two: disease. Smallpox hit Tenochtitlan during the siege. Three: timing, an empire under strain. Four: the technology, as garnish. Some historians argue the conquest was mostly Native allies doing the fighting, and Cortés rode the wave.

Jay: And the translator woman? Doña Marina?

Maya: La Malinche. An enslaved Nahua woman who learned Spanish and became his translator and intelligence chief. Traitor or survivor? Mexico still argues about her. Then the Noche Triste, the Night of Sorrows, when the Aztecs nearly wiped the Spanish out. August 1521: the city falls. Five hundred Spaniards didn't beat an empire. A coalition of the empire's enemies did, with smallpox as the silent partner.

Jay: Question five: why was Potosí the engine of the Spanish Empire?

Maya: One mountain in Bolivia. Silver discovered in 1545, the richest deposit in history. At its peak Potosí was one of the biggest cities on earth. The crown took twenty percent off the top, the royal fifth, and the rest flooded the world.

Jay: And it didn't stay in Spain.

Maya: The Manila galleons carried it west to the Philippines and on to China, where silver was currency. In Europe, the flood caused the price revolution. Too much silver chasing too few goods — inflation for a century. The human cost: the mita, the labor draft, mercury poisoning in the mines — one mountain funded an empire and wrecked an economy, at the cost of thousands of lives. If a question asks for the Exchange's economic effects, Potosí's your answer.

Jay: Question six, the chain one. Encomienda, then mita, then African slavery. What drove each shift?

Maya: Say the chain, then the why.

Jay: Encomienda: the crown grants a colonist a town's labor. The New Laws of 1542 try to kill it, the colonists revolt, the crown backs down. Then the mita, the rotating labor draft for the mines. Then enslaved African labor, the Middle Passage. Then the casta system ranking everybody by blood.

Maya: The driver, every single time: disease emptied the towns, and the empire needed workers. Demographic collapse meant labor shortage. Each system was the next answer to the same question. The New Laws failed because the colonists nearly went to war over them — in Peru, rebels killed the viceroy sent to enforce them. So the crown swapped the system instead of the goal. New fuel, same engine.

Jay: Question seven: what did Las Casas and Sepúlveda actually disagree about? Valladolid, 1550.

Maya: Whether the conquest was just. Las Casas: Native peoples are rational souls, fully human, with rights. Convert them, don't enslave them. Sepúlveda: Aristotle's natural slaves, a just war, Christianity the greater good.

Jay: And the judges picked?

Maya: Nobody won. 1550 into 1551, and the judges never issued a verdict. The debate changed the language. The words of universal human rights were invented here. It barely touched the practice. Both men had blind spots: Las Casas once proposed African labor as the humane alternative, a suggestion he'd later deeply regret. Sepúlveda built his case on Aristotle without ever setting foot in the Americas. If the prompt asks how much the debate changed policy, the answer is barely. That's the complexity point.

Jay: Question eight: the Pueblo Revolt. What caused it, and why didn't it last?

Maya: 1680. Eighty years of Franciscan missions: kivas banned, sacred objects burned, forced labor. Then drought and Apache raids the Spanish couldn't stop. Popé, a medicine man once whipped for sorcery, organizes across hundreds of miles with knotted cords. One knot per day. Everybody rises when the last knot comes loose. Santa Fe falls, the Spanish retreat to El Paso. The most successful Native revolt in North American history.

Jay: And it lasted twelve years?

Maya: Twelve years of Pueblo independence. Then it unraveled: Popé ordered everything Spanish destroyed, but the drought continued, old rivalries returned, and Popé died. In 1692 Vargas came back and retook New Mexico. But the Spanish returned chastened. Fewer missionaries, more tolerance, Pueblo religion surviving underground to this day. The revolt won by losing: it forced the empire to change how it ruled.

Jay: Question nine. The New Laws and the Valladolid debate both tried to restrain Spanish cruelty. Why did so little change on the ground?

Maya: Distance and defiance. The crown was four thousand miles away with no way to enforce anything. The colonists answered the New Laws with open rebellion and a dead viceroy, so the crown gutted the laws. The debate produced beautiful language and zero enforcement. A law the colonies ignore is a letter, not a policy. The machine kept running because everybody in the colonies needed it running.

Jay: Last one. Question ten: in one sentence, why does 1491 to 1607 shape everything that follows?

Maya: You try first.

Jay: Because everything after is built on what happened here? The exchange, the labor systems, the conquest?

Maya: Close. Say it like a thesis: contact created a new world on conquest's terms. The biology, the economy, and the labor systems of everything after were decided in this unit.

Jay: The period thesis, right?

Maya: The period thesis. Prediction beat: two Document-Based Questions I'd bet on. One: evaluate the extent to which the Columbian Exchange transformed the Americas. Two: evaluate the relative importance of disease, alliances, and technology in the Spanish conquest of the Aztec Empire.

Jay: And our answers are sitting right here. The biology changed, the economy changed, the pushback never stopped. And the labor machine ran straight through all of it.

Maya: Ten questions, landed. Next time: the English arrive for real. Jamestown, tobacco, and the rebellion that invented race as law.

Jay: One ocean made one world —

Maya: On conquest's terms.
