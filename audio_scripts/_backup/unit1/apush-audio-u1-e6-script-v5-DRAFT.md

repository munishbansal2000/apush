Maya: Last time: Potosí, the silver mountain that bankrolled an empire. All of it ran on one thing: workers. And Spain kept running out of them. Three boxes on your sheet: the encomienda, the Middle Passage, and the casta system. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes, and you'll check all three off.

Maya: Warm-up. Name every labor system the Spanish tried.

Jay: Uh. Encomienda. Slavery. The... mita?

Maya: Four, minimum, and the mita's Peru's. Deep question one: what was the encomienda?

Jay: The crown gives a colonist a bunch of Native people?

Maya: Close. The crown granted a colonist the labor and tribute of a Native town. Not the land, the people. The colonist gets the work and owes the town protection and Christianization in return.

Jay: So slavery with homework.

Maya: Slavery with paperwork, and the paperwork mattered to the crown. Legally these were free subjects of the king. In practice the work quotas were brutal and the protection was mostly theoretical. And then the system ate itself.

Maya: Because everybody died. Disease kept emptying the towns: fewer workers to grant, more work to do. The encomienda failed like a machine with no parts left.

Jay: Deep question two. Somebody tried to fix it?

Maya: Bartolomé de Las Casas. He'd held an encomienda himself, gave it up, and lobbied the crown for decades. In 1542, the big win: the New Laws. The encomienda dies out with its current holders. No inheritance.

Jay: And the colonists were cool with that.

Maya: The colonists nearly tore the empire apart. In Peru, Gonzalo Pizarro raised an army of encomenderos. The crown sent a viceroy to enforce the laws, Blasco Núñez Vela. Pizarro's rebels beat him at Añaquito in 1546 and cut off his head.

Jay: They killed the viceroy. The king's guy.

Maya: The king's guy, dead on a battlefield. In Mexico the viceroy stalled. Within a few years the crown caved and gutted the inheritance ban. The encomienda limped on.

Jay: So the fix failed.

Maya: The crown changed the system instead of the goal. It took the assigning power away from the encomenderos and kept it. Native towns owed a rotating share of workers, and a crown official assigned them to settlers for a few weeks at a time. Farms, mines, workshops. Your turn ends, you go home.

Jay: A draft. Like names out of a hat.

Maya: A draft is exactly right. In New Spain it was called the repartimiento, from repartir, to divide up. The crown decided the encomenderos couldn't be trusted, so it divided them up itself.

Jay: Was it better?

Maya: Paid, in law. A little, and intermittent. But in the mines it got slavery-like fast. Draft versus grant: in the grant, the colonist gets handed the town's labor outright. In the draft, the crown rents it out a few weeks at a time.

Jay: Got it, the draft. Deep question three: the Middle Passage.

Maya: The math problem first. The Native population kept falling, so the draft kept shrinking. Spain looked somewhere the workers wouldn't run out.

Jay: Africa. Because... they were already there? The Portuguese had the ships?

Maya: That's one reason, and it's the coldest one: the Portuguese were already running the route, so the ships could keep coming no matter how many died. The other reasons the Spanish told themselves: Africans carried some immunity to Old World diseases, and many came from farming societies that knew rice and tropical crops.

Jay: "No matter how many died." Say the number.

Maya: The crossing took weeks, below decks, in chains. Roughly one in seven died on the way over. The ship logs swing wider.

Jay: One in seven.

Maya: I used to play hide-and-seek where I'd squeeze into the hall closet and stay too long. Dark, coats everywhere, couldn't move. That was a game. Two months of that, in the dark, in chains.

Maya: Into plantation and mine labor that worked them hard and bought replacements. One of the largest forced migrations in human history. And Las Casas is in this story twice. Around 1516 he proposed bringing in enslaved Africans instead of working Natives to death.

Jay: So the great defender of the Indians helped start the African slave trade.

Maya: He didn't start it. Enslaved Africans were already being shipped before his proposal. What he did was lend it respectability. And decades later, in his history of the Indies, he took it back. Wrote that African slavery was every bit as unjust, and he wasn't sure his good intentions would excuse him.

Jay: An apology in a book nobody read for a century. Convenient.

Maya: Partly convenient. But he spent the rest of his life arguing both were wrong.

Jay: Two systems down. What's the third box?

Maya: The third box is the casta system, and it's not a labor system. It's what the labor systems built: a social ladder with legal teeth. Peninsulares, Spaniards born in Spain, at the top. Criollos, Spaniards born in the Americas, a step down. Below them: mestizos of mixed Spanish and Native ancestry, mulatos of mixed Spanish and African ancestry, then Indigenous peoples and enslaved Africans holding the bottom rung.

Jay: That's a lot of labels.

Maya: Sixteen categories in the paintings, part custom, part law. Your label decided who could hold office and who paid tribute. An empire terrified of mixture, trying to legislate race.

Jay: Wait, the paintings? Like, actual paintings of the categories?

Maya: Whole series. Parents of one label, child of another, every combination named and ranked. The bureaucracy followed you into your family tree.

Jay: Okay, quiz me. Mita versus encomienda. Go.

Maya: You tell me. You brought up the mita in the warm-up.

Jay: The mita's... the grant one? No wait. The mita is Peru's draft, for the silver mines. The encomienda is the grant of a town's labor to a colonist.

Maya: Clean on the mita. The repartimiento?

Jay: New Spain's draft. The crown's version.

Maya: Three for three — a clean sweep. Now the one that stuck: by the 1600s the countryside settles into the hacienda. No grant of people this time. The power was the land itself.

Jay: So who works it?

Maya: Peons. Technically free workers. The chain was debt: wages paid as an advance, or in credit at the estate store, where everything cost too much. The balance never cleared.

Jay: Peon. Like the little worker guys in Warcraft?

Maya: Same word. It passed through here, through the debt-bound laborers on these estates.

Maya: Lightning round. Four systems, one hunger. I name the trigger, you answer:

Maya: The crown grants a colonist a town's labor.

Maya: The crown takes the assigning power back.
[2-second pause]
Jay: Repartimiento — the crown's rotating labor draft.

Maya: The towns keep emptying, so Spain looks across the ocean.
[2-second pause]
Jay: Enslaved African labor. The Middle Passage.

Maya: The estates, the debt, the store you never leave.
[2-second pause]
Jay: Hacienda — debt peonage, the store's ledger.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: the encomienda. The crown grants a colonist Native labor and tribute, the colonist owes protection and conversion. In practice: brutal quotas, theoretical protection. Disease emptied the towns, the New Laws of 1542 failed, and when the grant broke, the crown's rotating draft, the repartimiento.

Jay: The grant, then the draft. Checking that one.

Maya: Two: the Middle Passage. Spain turned to enslaved African labor as the Native workforce collapsed: disease immunity, farming skills, Portuguese ships that kept coming. Roughly one in seven died crossing. The Las Casas irony: he proposed it in 1516, took it back decades later.

Jay: The apology nobody read. Checking that one.

Maya: Three: the casta system. Not a labor system, a ladder: peninsulares, criollos, mestizos, mulatos, Indigenous peoples, Africans. Part custom, part law. Behind the ladder, the hacienda: estates where the company store's ledger kept peons bound — technically free, practically trapped. Memory check: the sixteen categories were in... the paintings, right? The casta paintings.

Jay: The paintings. Every mixture named and ranked.

Maya: Two questions, AP-shaped. Answer out loud before I do. One: a crown official assigns your village's workers to a mine for three weeks, then sends them home. Which system, and who did the assigning before it?

[5-second pause]

Jay: The repartimiento?

Maya: The repartimiento, the crown's rotating draft. Before it, the encomenderos did the assigning.

Maya: Two: a historian hands you a hacienda account book. One worker's debt grows every year for a decade, even though he's working the whole time. What's the point of a source like that?

[5-second pause]

Jay: Uh. That the store was ripping him off?

Maya: Debt peonage. It shows the debt was the chain. The worker was technically free and practically trapped. The book is the trap, on paper.

Maya: Check your three boxes. Next time: 1550. Two scholars, one question. Are the Native peoples of the Americas fully human, with rights, or natural slaves? The Valladolid debate.

Maya: Four systems, one hunger —

Jay: and the workers paid for all of them.
