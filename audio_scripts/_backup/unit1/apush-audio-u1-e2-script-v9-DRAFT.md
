Maya: Last time: Cahokia, corn, and millions of people who'd never heard of Europe. This time it's the ocean. For a thousand years it sat there, a rumor with an edge, and then one generation of sailors solved it. Three boxes on your sheet: the three Gs, the toolkit, and the Treaty of Tordesillas. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes, and you'll check all three off.

Marcus: Start with the why, because the why decided everything else. Europe wanted Asian spices and silk, and the old land routes ran straight through Ottoman territory. After Constantinople fell in 1453, those routes got pricier and shakier. So motive one goes up on the board: gold.

Maya: Gold first. Because of the spices?

Marcus: Because of everything. Europe was living through a bullion shortage, not enough gold and silver coin for a growing economy. And here's the part the slogans skip: it wasn't greed in a vacuum. No metal, no armies. No armies, no crowns. For a Renaissance state, a pile of silver was power you could spend.

Maya: Okay, but God is word two, and God is not about spices.

Marcus: God is the Reconquista. For centuries, Christian kingdoms in Iberia had been pushing Muslim rule out of the peninsula, and that war ended at Granada in January of 1492. A crown fresh off a holy war feels owed new souls. Conversion was the mission statement. Conquest was the habit underneath it.

Maya: So they took the crusade's habits and aimed them west.

Marcus: How it played out. And glory is word three: Renaissance crowns competing for prestige the way schools compete for rankings. Portugal was getting rich off the sea road to Asia while Spain watched from the shore. No rival crown watches that quietly.

Maya: So the famous three Gs. But they weren't equal partners. Gold did the heavy lifting, and the other two were the excuse.

Marcus: Most historians would back you. Some argue the whole thing was economics wearing a religious costume. Others say the state rivalry mattered more, that crowns sailed because other crowns were sailing. Nobody's fully sure which one turned the ship.

Maya: I don't buy glory as a real motive, though. Nobody risks drowning for bragging rights.

Marcus: Crowns did. Regularly. But point taken — nobody writes the check without the gold.

Maya: Box one's basically the business plan. Checking it.

Marcus: Not yet. The why is only half the story. For a thousand years nobody crossed, because nobody could. Then four tools arrived and the ocean stopped being a wall. The caravel, small, quick, built to turn fast.

Maya: Give me the extremes.

Marcus: First, the sails. Lateen sails let a ship claw into the wind instead of waiting for it. The astrolabe read latitude off the stars, and the compass plus portolan charts — hand-drawn maps sailors trusted — did the rest.

Maya: Four tools. That's the whole revolution? A boat, a triangle, and a star-reader?

Marcus: Plus a prince who never sailed. Henry the Navigator spent decades funding voyage after voyage down the African coast. Now, quick correction to the legend, because you've probably heard he ran a navigation school at Sagres.

Maya: I literally just learned that from a video essay. It's not real?

Marcus: The school part is mostly myth. Modern historians say there was no academy with classrooms, just a wealthy patron paying for ships and hiring cartographers. The money was real. The school is the legend.

Maya: That one stings. I cited it in a discussion post last month.

Marcus: Cite the correction instead. Teachers love that move. Henry's captains kept pushing south anyway. In 1488, Bartolomeu Dias rounded the southern tip of Africa. In 1498, Vasco da Gama reached India. Portugal owned the sea road to Asia, and Spain needed another way in.

Maya: So Spain finds a Genoese sailor with a wild pitch. Niña, Pinta, Santa María. I know those from a second-grade song, and the tune's still in my head.

Marcus: Three little ships, and the worst math that ever paid off. Columbus figured Asia sat a few thousand miles west of the Canaries. The real number was closer to ten thousand, with a continent in the way. He got the shape of the earth right and the size of the trip wildly wrong.

Maya: And Portugal said no first, right? Because their guys did the math correctly?

Marcus: They did. Portugal's experts ran the numbers, saw the distance was impossible, and turned him down. Spain's court said yes anyway — flush from Granada and eager to catch Portugal.

Maya: October 1492, the Bahamas. He thinks it's Asia.

Marcus: He called the people he met Indians because he was sure he'd reached the East Indies, and the name stuck for five centuries. His journal from that first voyage says the mindset out loud. The people he met on the beach at Guanahani, quote, "would make fine servants," and with fifty men he could subjugate them all. We have it through Las Casas's copy of the log. The plan, on the record, from day one.

Maya: He wrote that down. On day one.

Marcus: Four voyages after that, the Caribbean and the mainland coast, and he died still insisting he'd really found Asia.

Maya: So the toolkit's checked. That leaves the pope's line.

Marcus: Spain's holding islands, Portugal's furious, and they call the referee. The part people miss: the referee himself was Spanish. Pope Alexander the Sixth, born Rodrigo Borgia in Valencia. His 1493 bull handed Spain nearly everything west of a line in the Atlantic. Portugal howled.

Maya: So the pope split it down the middle, nice and fair?

Marcus: Not close. The first line was a gift to Spain. Portugal had the better navy and all the African charts, so it had leverage to demand a redo. In 1494 the two crowns signed the Treaty of Tordesillas. The new line sat three hundred and seventy leagues west of the Cape Verde islands, a league being roughly three miles. West of the line went to Spain. East went to Portugal.

Maya: A Spanish pope drawing the line, and Portugal accepts the redo because it had the ships to argue with.

Marcus: Exactly. And between the two of them, the line mostly held, which is why Brazil speaks Portuguese today. The bulge of Brazil sticks out east of the line, onto Portugal's side. One imaginary line, and it decided the language of half a continent.

Maya: But France and England just ignored it.

Marcus: Completely. The line bound two crowns and nobody else. Now the irony: the most famous border in exploration history, and half of Europe never signed it.

Maya: Checking box three.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: the three Gs. Gold, the spice hunger plus the bullion shortage, and after 1453 the Ottoman squeeze on the old routes. God, the Reconquista's crusading energy, fresh off Granada. Glory, crowns racing each other. Gold did the heavy lifting.

Marcus: Economics first, the rest as accelerants. That's the ranking to argue.

Maya: Two: the toolkit. Caravel, lateen sails, the astrolabe, compass and portolan charts. Henry the Navigator supplied the money, not the mythical school. Memory check: Dias rounded Africa in fourteen eighty-eight, da Gama reached India in fourteen ninety-eight.

Marcus: Ten years apart, one sea road, and Spain locked out of it completely.

Maya: Three: Tordesillas. Fourteen ninety-four. The Spanish pope's first line was a gift, Portugal forced the redo, and the new line sat three hundred and seventy leagues west of Cape Verde. Spain took the west, Portugal took the east, Brazil ended up speaking Portuguese, and France and England never signed.

Marcus: The deal was rigged, and it still redrew the map.

Maya: Two questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer out loud before I do. First, stimulus style: a Portuguese captain's log from the 1480s lists new gear aboard, an astrolabe and updated portolan charts. What's the bigger pattern this source points to?

[5-second pause]

Maya: The toolkit. Iberian sailors weren't braver than everyone else. They had the instruments to sail beyond sight of land and find their way back. The tools are what made fourteen ninety-two possible instead of thirteen ninety-two.

Maya: Second: a Spanish priest writes in 1493 that the new lands will bring souls to the church and silver to the crown. A classmate says God mattered more than gold. How do you answer?

[5-second pause]

Maya: Both, but ranked. The crown wrote checks for gold: the spice trade, the bullion shortage, the Ottoman squeeze. God gave the mission its language, and the Reconquista gave Spain its habits. Glory kept the race going. If the test asks for the most significant cause, lead with economics.

Maya: Check your three boxes. Next time: the exchange. What crossed the Atlantic in both directions, and the cargo nobody chose.

Marcus: They wanted a shortcut to Asia —

Maya: and found the long way to everything.
