Maya: Last time: the labor chain. Encomienda, the repartimiento draft, African slavery, the hacienda. This time, the empire stops to argue with itself. Spain has conquered half a hemisphere. In 1550 the crown calls a debate on one question: can conquest ever be just? Arguing that the Native peoples are fully human: Bartolomé de las Casas. A Dominican friar, decades in the Indies — argued here by Marcus. Arguing for the empire: Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda. A court scholar, in his own voice. I did mock trial in eighth grade: the side with the burden of proof has to earn it, and a hung jury means nobody wins. Just saying.

Marcus: My case starts with who counts as human. The Native peoples are rational souls: fully capable of the Christian faith, and fully capable of governing themselves. These are ordered peoples, with towns and laws and customs. There are no natural slaves in my America. Understand my standing to say it: I once held an encomienda myself. I gave it up, because I saw what it did to the people under it. And I argue from your own doctors. Aquinas said dominion comes from natural law, not from grace: unbelievers can hold true title to their lands. Deny that, and you deny Thomism.

Sepúlveda: I argue from Aristotle, who wrote two thousand years before any of us that some peoples are slaves by nature: born to be ruled, incapable of governing themselves. I have read the chronicles of the Indies with care. The peoples there fit the description. They are, as I have written, homunculi: little men, in whom you will barely find the vestiges of humanity. Spanish rule is not cruelty. It is order, brought to those who cannot produce it themselves. Four just causes. The first, you've heard. The second: their idolatry and human sacrifice offend nature itself. The third: the innocent, slaughtered on their altars, cry out for rescue. The fourth: the faith, which they cannot receive unless we bring it.

Maya: Two cases, fully opened. First clash: the evidence. Marcus, you wrote it down.

Marcus: I wrote it down. A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies, finished in 1542, published ten years later. Village by village: the mines, the burnings, the forced labor. Testimony from a man who watched it happen, not a scholar who read about it.

Maya: And the numbers in that book? Episode three mentioned eight or nine out of ten dead from disease. What's your number?

Marcus: My numbers are my numbers, and that is the warning label: I won't hide from it. I claimed Hispaniola went from over three million souls to a couple hundred. Modern estimates put the starting figure far lower, and most historians say disease did most of the killing. But the cruelty I described was real. That is why the book traveled as far as it did.

Sepúlveda: I do not dispute that cruelties occurred. I dispute what they prove. Excesses happen in every conquest, as they happen in every kingdom. They do not prove the conquest unjust, any more than a crime in Seville proves Spain unjust. Weigh the excesses against what conquest brings: the true faith, real law, and an end to idolatry and human sacrifice. The scale balances.

Marcus: He read a Greek philosopher and wrote off a hemisphere. That is not an argument. That is a permission slip with Latin on it.

Maya: Strong words. Second clash: the blind spots, and I'm coming for both of you. Marcus: early on, you proposed African slavery to spare the Indians. Your own words.

Marcus: My own words, and the worst I ever put my name to. I spent the rest of my life taking it back. If my case is that no people are natural slaves, then it holds for Africans too, or it holds for no one. The man who wrote the Short Account had to learn that from his own shame.

Maya: Sepúlveda: you never crossed the Atlantic. You wrote off a hemisphere you never saw.

Sepúlveda: True, and I do not hide it. I relied on the chronicles, above all Fernández de Oviedo's General History. But my argument does not depend on which village I visited. It depends on Aristotle, on natural law, on what reason tells us about peoples given to idolatry and human sacrifice. Reports vary. Principle does not.

Maya: Before the verdict that was not, the context both sides lived in. The New Laws.

Marcus: 1542. The crown tried to kill the encomienda slowly: no new grants, and when a holder died, the grant returned to the crown instead of passing to his children. Las Casas pushed for exactly that, and for once the crown listened.

Sepúlveda: And the colonists revolted. In Peru, Gonzalo Pizarro, the brother of Francisco, raised an army against the viceroy sent to enforce the laws. An empire cannot be governed against the men who hold it.

Marcus: Charles the Fifth caved. In 1545 he revoked the inheritance ban, and the encomienda survived. So five years later, the crown tried asking the question a different way: not with laws, with a debate. This is the debate you're hearing.

Maya: So who were these judges? Theologians, lawyers, then nothing. Why no ruling?

Marcus: Because a ruling either way was dangerous. Condemn the conquest, and you unwind the empire's title to half a hemisphere. Bless it, and you hand every conquistador a license. So the junta scattered, and both sides claimed victory.

Sepúlveda: Or the question proved larger than any verdict.

Maya: One irony neither of you can dodge. Marcus: the book.

Marcus: He wrote it to move the Spanish king. Decades later, English and Dutch printers ran it in translation as proof of Spanish cruelty. The friar who fought the conquest armed Spain's rivals with their best propaganda. The Black Legend, built from his pages. I wanted reform. I got a weapon, aimed at my own country, by men who cared nothing for the Indians.

Maya: Marcus, one more push before the closings. Ordered peoples, towns and laws: did you ever romanticize them? Were they really as peaceable as your book needed?

Marcus: Perhaps I painted them gentler than they were. But I described what I saw, and what I saw was humanity enough. The question was never whether they were perfect. It was whether they were people.

Maya: Third clash: the just war itself. Sepúlveda, make it plainly.

Sepúlveda: Idolatry and human sacrifice offend natural law, which binds all peoples whether they acknowledge it or not. A war that ends those practices is not cruelty but mercy. Conquest brings the true faith and real law to peoples who cannot govern themselves. Order through conquest: that is my case, and it is just.

Marcus: So killing people to stop the killing. Just war with better branding. The faith must be offered, never forced. Force only makes fake Christians. The peoples of the Indies are, as I have written, by nature the most humble, patient, and peaceable people: they deserve persuasion, not conquest. That is my case, and it is just.

Maya: Closing statements. Sepúlveda, one paragraph.

Sepúlveda: The question was never whether the conquest was gentle. It was whether it was just. Peoples who are slaves by nature, given to idolatry and sacrifice, cannot be left to themselves without offense to God and to reason. Spain brought law where there was custom, faith where there was idolatry, and order where there was none. That is my whole case, and on that ground I rest it.

Maya: Marcus.

Marcus: The question was never whether Spain was powerful. It was whether power can make a war just. It cannot. The Native peoples are rational souls, capable of faith and of self-government, and no reading of Aristotle changes what I saw with my own eyes. Offer the faith. Do not force it. That is my case.

Maya: The judges heard both cases, through 1550 and into 1551, then scattered without issuing a ruling. No verdict. A hung jury, and both sides declared victory anyway. On the ground, almost nothing changed: the encomienda survived, the mines kept running. So to what extent did the debate change Spanish colonial policy? Barely. That's the complexity point. Take that to the exam.

Maya: But the question survived. Before Valladolid, no empire had formally asked whether winning a war could still make the war unjust: whether conquest can be judged by a universal moral law. Las Casas lost the policy and won the argument's afterlife. Every later claim that peoples have rights no conquest can ever erase descends from the question asked in that room. That question is what historians see as the seed of natural-rights language, the idea that would outlive every empire in this course. The empire is gone. The question outlived it.
