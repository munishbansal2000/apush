Maya: Last time: Bacon's Rebellion, 1676. Poor whites and enslaved Black rebels side by side, and planters answering with slave codes. This time we head north. Colonists building a godly society. Within a generation they're banishing their own people and fighting the bloodiest war in American history, per capita. Three boxes: the Great Migration and the covenant towns, the dissenters who got kicked out, King Philip's War. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Give me eight minutes and you'll land all three.

Marcus: They were running from Archbishop Laud's squeeze on Puritan preachers in the 1630s, and from a souring economy too. In 1630 Winthrop's fleet lands around seven hundred people, and the decade doesn't stop. Something like twenty thousand cross the Atlantic by 1640.

Maya: Twenty thousand? Wait, not the Pilgrims? The Mayflower people?

Marcus: Different decade, different boat. Plymouth, 1620: Separatists who wanted out of the Church of England entirely. The Massachusetts Bay Puritans wanted to purify it.

Maya: So the funny-hat Thanksgiving people and the city-on-a-hill people are two different outfits.

Marcus: The city-on-a-hill people would have thrown a book. But yes.

Maya: The Puritan towns weren't just church, though. They were covenant. Walk me through that.

Marcus: A covenant was a contract with God, and it organized everything. Believers sign a church covenant, then the town itself is covenanted. Dedham, 1636: the whole town signs one about how they'll live, settle disputes, tax themselves. The meetinghouse sits at the center. Worship Sunday, town meeting Monday.

Maya: I toured a reconstructed meetinghouse in sixth grade and thought it was a barn. The Puritans' whole universe was a building I'd have walked right past.

Marcus: The sermon that set the tone: Winthrop's "A Model of Christian Charity," preached to the fleet in 1630. The famous line: the colony will be as a city upon a hill, with every eye upon them. It's aspiration and warning both. If we succeed, the world watches. If we fail, the world watches.

Maya: Kennedy used it. Reagan used it. I'd heard "city upon a hill" in a graduation speech. Never knew it was a 1630 sermon threat.

Marcus: A threat is right. Why presidents keep borrowing it, nobody's fully sure. It's a great sentence that means whatever the speaker needs.

Maya: One more thing that set the migration apart: families.

Marcus: Yes. Chesapeake ships were packed with young men chasing tobacco money. Puritan ships carried ordinary people, old and young, and nearly half the passengers were women. A cross-section of England, not a labor crew.

Maya: One down, two to go. Now the part where the hill starts kicking people off it.

Marcus: It didn't take long. Roger Williams, minister in Salem: sharp, brilliant, impossible. He said two things Boston couldn't stand: the colony had no right to Native land unless it bought it fair and square, and the government had no business meddling in religion. Soul liberty: every conscience answers to God alone.

Maya: Banished.

Marcus: October 1635, the General Court banishes him. They try to ship him back to England in January; he slips away in a blizzard, gets sheltered by the Wampanoags, and by spring 1636 he's bought land from the Narragansett sachems Canonicus and Miantonomi and founded Providence. Rhode Island.

Maya: When he bought that land, did the Narragansetts think they were selling it the same way he thought he was buying it?

Marcus: Honestly? Nobody's fully sure. Land meant different things on each side. Williams meant a deed; the sachems may well have meant permission to share it.

Marcus: Then the bigger shock: Anne Hutchinson. Arrives in 1634 and starts hosting Bible meetings in her home, women at first, then men, then the governor himself. She accused most of Boston's ministers of preaching a covenant of works instead of a covenant of grace. In 1637 she was put on trial before the General Court, several months pregnant, defended herself with Scripture, and lost. She and her family left in March 1638 for Rhode Island.

Maya: She was exiled while pregnant, and Boston kept coming for her?

Marcus: Winthrop never let go. When her pregnancy ended in a stillbirth that June, he called it God's judgment. She fled beyond Boston's reach, Dutch Long Island, and in 1643 she and most of her family were killed in a Native raid. That leaves the question the colonists couldn't answer: heretic or the first American feminist? Some historians say the label matters. Others say she was neither and both. I don't think that's settled.

Maya: Last box. The war.

Marcus: King Philip's War, 1675 to 1676. Metacom, the Wampanoag sachem the English called King Philip, Massasoit's son. The grievances had piled up: English cattle trampling Native cornfields, decades of land pressure, missionary towns pushing Natives to convert. The spark: the trial and execution of three Wampanoags for the murder of John Sassamon. He was a Praying Indian, a Native convert living in one of the mission towns, who'd warned the English about Metacom's plans.

Maya: And then it gets savage.

Marcus: On both sides: more than half of New England's towns came under attack, and a dozen burned to the ground. The English rounded up captives and sold them. Metacom's wife and son were sold into slavery in the West Indies. When Metacom was killed in August 1676, his head was displayed at Plymouth for something like twenty years.

Maya: The English bestseller of the war was written by a captive, right?

Marcus: Mary Rowlandson, taken at Lancaster in February 1676. Her account of the forced march, hunger, cold, watching the towns burn, became the book everyone in New England read. It ran through edition after edition, America's first bestseller by a woman. The war's most famous English voice, and she'd lived it from the other side of the musket.

Maya: A bestseller written on a forced march. That's the war in one image.

Maya: Deadliest war per capita in American history. Real, or the dramatic version?

Marcus: It's the figure historians repeat, and the numbers back it: roughly eight percent of English New England's adult men died. The Civil War took something like four or five percent by the same measure. For Native peoples it was worse. Thousands dead in the fighting, thousands more of hunger and disease, around a thousand shipped off as slaves. The Wampanoags and Narragansetts were shattered. The mission towns emptied. Native power in New England was broken, and it never came back.

Maya: And the debate. Was it inevitable?

Marcus: Some say English expansion made it unavoidable. Others call it a tragedy of miscalculation on both sides. I lean toward nobody planned it, and everybody paid for it.

Maya: One line before the recap: the exam angle. New England versus Chesapeake. Family migration and covenant towns up north, young men and tobacco down south, rice and indigo even further south, both taking Native land in the end.

Marcus: That's the comparison. Bank it.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: the Great Migration, 1630s, around twenty thousand people. Not the Pilgrims, I know, Marcus. Covenant everything: churches, towns, Dedham signing a deal with God. Meetinghouse at the center. Checking that one.

Maya: Two: the dissenters. Williams, banished in 1635, founded Providence in... 1635?

Marcus: Banished in October of '35, slipped away that winter, bought the land and founded Providence in the spring of '36.

Maya: Right. Soul liberty. Buys the land from the Narragansetts, or tries to. And Hutchinson: the home Bible meetings, the trial in '37 while pregnant, exiled in '38, and the label question: heretic or feminist. Checking that one.

Maya: Three: King Philip's War, 1675 to '76. Metacom's grievances: land, livestock, missionaries. A dozen towns burned, his head on a pike at Plymouth, deadliest war per capita because the colonies were so small. Aftermath: Native power in New England, broken. Checking that one off.

Maya: All three landed.

Maya: Two questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One: a 1630 sermon calls the colonists "a city upon a hill," with all eyes watching. What's the point of this source, and which trial from this episode does it collide with?

[5-second pause]

Maya: The point is the aspiration: Winthrop warning the colony would succeed or fail on a public stage. It collides with Hutchinson's 1637 trial. The city upon a hill banishing a woman who disagreed with its ministers. Aspiration versus reality.

Maya: Two: name the comparison. One difference, one similarity, New England versus the Chesapeake.

[5-second pause]

Maya: Difference: New England ran on family migration and covenant towns, nearly half the passengers women, while the Chesapeake ran on young single men and tobacco. Similarity: both took Native land by force. Frontier in one, King Philip's War in the other.

Maya: Check your three boxes. Episode three in the books. Next time: Pennsylvania, the colony that advertised in German.

Marcus: The hill was the promise —

Maya: The war was the price.
