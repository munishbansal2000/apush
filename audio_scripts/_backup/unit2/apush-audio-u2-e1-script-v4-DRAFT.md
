Maya: Last time: the Unit 1 cram: one ocean made one world, on conquest's terms. Tonight it's the rematch nobody asked for: four empires, one continent, four completely different business plans. Jay, quiz me. If I can't explain all four by the end, I owe you fries.

Jay: Deal. Warm-up, rapid: four empires, one sentence each. Spain?

Maya: Spain: crown-run, souls and silver. Missions to convert Native peoples, mines to enrich the crown. The encomienda handed a colonist a whole town's labor, and every time the towns emptied, the crown rebuilt the machine instead of changing the goal. Of the four, Spain ruled the most people and ran the most paperwork.

Jay: France?

Maya: France: beaver. Europe wanted felt hats, felt hats needed beaver pelts, so the whole colony bent around the fur trade. Thin on the map, deep in the woods.

Jay: Holland?

Maya: Holland: a company colony. New Netherland belonged to the Dutch West India Company, not really a king. A company town with a flag.

Jay: England?

Maya: England: company money, settler families. Joint-stock companies paid the bills, religious refugees and servants did the work, and families on farms replaced soldiers in forts. Spain folded Native peoples into its labor machine. England pushed them off the land.

Jay: Thesis line first, then we go deep. What's the one sentence that wins the comparison essay?

Maya: Collide, don't list. Spain: crown-run, extractive, missions and mines, Native peoples folded into the labor machine. England: company-funded, settler-driven, families and farms, Native peoples pushed aside. Two different hungers, same continent.

Jay: Deep question one: Spain. Missions and mines I get. But the casta thing, the labels?

Maya: Same machine, new paperwork. Spain loved a category. Peninsulares at the top: Spaniards born in Spain. Then criollos: Spaniards born in the Americas. Then a whole vocabulary for mixture: mestizos, mulatos, and dozens of finer slices below them, with indios and Africans holding the bottom rung. Your label followed you into court, into church, into every appointment. The paperwork didn't just track labor. It ranked people.

Jay: A whole government of who your grandparents were.

Maya: And the mission towns ran the borderlands, Florida and New Mexico, for the better part of a century, until the Pueblos answered in 1680.

Jay: Deep question two: France. If they weren't after land, what were the guys in the woods actually doing?

Maya: Running beaver pelts past the legal trading posts. The coureurs de bois, the runners of the woods, paddled deep into the interior, learned the languages, and married into the trading towns. The crown licensed the fur trade. The woodsmen mostly ignored the license.

Jay: So smugglers.

Maya: In everything but name. And here's the confession: I own a beaver-felt fedora. Vintage shop, twelve dollars. I've been wearing colonial currency on my head.

Jay: You're telling me your hat funded an empire.

Maya: Your hat's ancestors did. Champlain planted Quebec in 1608, a trading post with a flag, not a colony of farms. That tells you the whole French plan in one date.

Jay: Okay, but were the French the nice ones? Trading instead of conquering?

Maya: The convenient ones. The fur trade needed partners, so France picked sides: guns and trade goods to the Huron, the Wendat, against the Iroquois. Partnership on the ledger, strategy on the map. Ask the Iroquois how nice it felt.

Jay: Okay, okay. Nobody was nice, everybody had interests.

Maya: Now why was New France so empty? Roughly sixty thousand French against over a million English. How does an empire run that thin?

Jay: Nobody wanted to go?

Maya: Four reasons, and they stack. The crown barred the Huguenots, French Protestants, so France's natural refugees sailed for the English and Dutch instead. The land went out as seigneuries, big feudal grants, and most seigneurs never paid the passage they'd promised their tenants. The crown held the reins directly, no self-government to attract anyone. And a fur economy never needed farmers. Historians argue about which mattered most. Together, they explain the empty map.

Jay: Wait, back up. The Jesuits. Were they the soul business or the fur business?

Maya: Both, and that's the French trick. The Jesuits lived in Wendat villages for years, learned the language, and wrote it all down. The Jesuit Relations are some of the most detailed European writing about Native life anywhere in North America, and the missionaries doubled as diplomats for the trade. You can't write that book from inside a fort.

Jay: Stimulus check: they hand you a page from the Jesuit Relations, a Jesuit describing Wendat village life in detail. What's the point of the source?

Maya: The point isn't just the facts about the Wendat. It's that the source exists at all. The French lived close enough to write it. A trade empire needed allies, so its missionaries doubled as diplomats. That book doesn't get written from inside a fort.

Jay: Deep question three: Holland. Patroons. That was the Dutch thing, right? The lords?

Maya: The Dutch thing, yes. 1629: the company told its investors, ship fifty settlers across and we'll hand you a feudal estate on the Hudson, you as patroon, lord of the manor. Sixteen miles of riverfront. The one that actually worked was Rensselaerswijck, up around present-day Albany. Most of the other lords couldn't find tenants.

Jay: Feudal lords on the Hudson. In the sixteen hundreds. Backwards.

Maya: Backwards is the word. But the town worked. New Amsterdam drew merchants from everywhere, a jumble of languages on the docks. Its doors stood open to people other colonies shut out, because the company needed customers.

Jay: Lightning: which empire would you rather live under as a Native person?

Maya: Honestly? None of them. But rank the damage. France wanted your furs, so it needed you alive and allied, until the alliance turned. Spain wanted your labor, so it folded you into the machine. England wanted your land, so it moved you off it. Different appetites, different ruins.

Jay: So the fur trade was the kindest machine?

Maya: Kindest is doing a lot of work there. It still ran on guns and smallpox. But it needed living partners, which is more than the other machines offered.

Jay: And then England just takes it. 1664?

Maya: 1664. An English fleet sailed into the harbor, Governor Stuyvesant looked at his defenses, and surrendered without a shot fired. The English renamed it New York, after the king's brother. The Dutch grabbed it back in 1673, then traded it away for Suriname the next year. One English coastline, New England to Carolina.

Jay: I know something here. Yankee: there's a theory it comes from a Dutch name, Jan Kees.

Maya: Nobody's fully sure. It's the kind of etymology that sounds too good to check. Probably tied to the Dutch in New York, but the paper trail runs cold.

Jay: The student knows one the teacher doesn't. Noted.

Maya: Deep question four: England. Why did their model actually fill the map?

Jay: Families? Like, actual families, not just guys?

Maya: Families. The Chesapeake ships were packed with young men chasing tobacco money at first, but the model that stuck was households: farms, towns, kids. Fifty acres a head for every person a planter imported, the headright, so importing people literally bought you land. And joint-stock companies meant the crown risked nothing and the investors risked everything.

Jay: And the Natives?

Maya: Pushed aside. That's the collision line for the essay: Spain folded Native peoples into its labor machine; England pushed them off the land. Two hungers, same continent.

Jay: So the English model wins? More people, more towns?

Maya: Wins at reproducing itself. The map filled up, the towns stuck, and three centuries later we speak English. But winning depends on whose ledger you're reading.

Jay: Lightning round. Four signatures, match the empire. Missions, fur trade.

Maya: Spain, France.

Jay: Patroons, settler families.

Maya: Holland, England. Four for four. Now the three-question check, exam-shaped. You answer, I judge. One: a textbook prints a Jesuit's detailed account of Wendat village life and asks what the source shows about the French model. Go.

Jay: That the French lived close enough to write it. Trade empire, allies, missionaries as diplomats. Doesn't get written from a fort.

[5-second pause]

Maya: Doesn't get written from inside a fort. Exactly. Two: why did New France stay thin while the English colonies filled up?

Jay: Barred the Huguenots, seigneuries that never delivered tenants, crown control, and fur doesn't need farmers.

[5-second pause]

Maya: All four, in order. Three: the essay asks you to compare Spanish and English colonization. The move?

Jay: Collide, don't list. Spain: crown, missions, mines, Natives in the labor machine. England: companies, families, farms, Natives pushed off the land.

[5-second pause]

Maya: That's the comparison. Episode one in the books. Next time: from servitude to slavery. 1676, Bacon's Rebellion: poor farmers and enslaved workers rising side by side, and the planters who swore never again.

Jay: Four empires, four appetites —

Maya: and one continent that paid the bill.
