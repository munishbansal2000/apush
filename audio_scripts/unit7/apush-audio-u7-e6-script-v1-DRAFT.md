# U7-E6 — Maya + Marcus + Guest (Fish Audio)
# Episode 6: The New Deal — Did It Work? ~8 min.
# Format: Debate (Maya moderates; Marcus vs. Guest)
# Read note: Debate format. Maya moderates, frames the question, and delivers the closing verdict. Marcus argues the New Deal worked; the Guest argues it failed and overreached — a serious skeptic with real numbers, never a caricature, and never returning in another episode. Maya tracks three boxes on her episode sheet: the three R's in action, the opposition, the verdict. The em dash in the closing tagline is a held beat: leave a full breath between Maya's setup line and Marcus's landing line. Do not rush it. Strip this header and the read note before TTS.
# Pronunciation: HYOO-ee LONG (Huey Long), KOFF-lin (Coughlin), kaynz (Keynes)

Maya: Last time: the crash pulled the trigger, but the rot had loaded the gun. Now it's March 1933. The banks are shut, a quarter of the country is out of work, and a new president tells the nation the only thing we have to fear is fear itself. Tonight, a debate: did the New Deal work? Three boxes on your sheet: the three R's in action, the opposition, the verdict. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes. Let's go.

Maya: The question before us: did the New Deal work? Arguing yes: Marcus. Arguing no, that it failed or went too far trying: our guest. Marcus, your opening statement.

Marcus: Look at what Roosevelt inherited: banks closed, a quarter of the country jobless, hunger marches on Washington. Within a hundred days: the bank holiday, the Emergency Banking Act, and the first fireside chat: Roosevelt explained the banks on the radio, and deposits flowed back. Then the three R's: relief, recovery, reform. The CCC hired young men, eighteen to twenty-five, and planted three billion trees. The WPA put millions to work: the post office murals, the schools and roads. The AAA paid farmers to grow less and lifted crop prices. The TVA brought cheap power to a whole region. And the reforms stuck: the FDIC insures your bank deposits, the SEC polices Wall Street, Social Security still sends the checks, the Wagner Act still protects unions. Before Roosevelt, Washington delivered mail and fought wars. After him, it insured your savings and hired the unemployed. Don't call it failure. Call it the modern American state being built.

Guest: Seventeen percent. Six years into the New Deal, in 1939, unemployment was still seventeen percent, and Roosevelt's people called that a recovery. In 1937 he cut federal spending to balance the budget, and the economy collapsed again: the 1937-38 recession, unemployment back at nineteen. The programs were a blizzard of improvisation. The AAA paid farmers to plow crops under while people stood in breadlines. The NRA told businesses what wages to pay and what prices to charge, until the Supreme Court killed the whole thing. And the bill came due as a federal government that never shrank back. Relief? Yes. People ate. But the Depression didn't end until the war. If the medicine worked, why did the patient need a world war to get up?

Maya: Two strong opens. Marcus — seventeen percent in 1939. Answer it.

Marcus: I won't dodge it. Seventeen percent is real. But direction matters: twenty-five percent when Roosevelt took office, fourteen by 1937. Then he cut spending and proved the Keynesian point. The spending was the recovery. Cut it, and the patient relapses. And "the war ended it" concedes my point: it was government spending at wartime scale that finished the job. The New Deal built the pump. The war just primed it ten times harder.

Guest: Or the pump was the wrong machine. Twenty-five to seventeen in six years, that's not a recovery, that's a flatline. Business leaders formed the American Liberty League in 1934 because Washington was dictating business through the NRA codes. The Supreme Court agreed and struck the codes down, then struck down the AAA. When the Court blocked him, Roosevelt tried to pack it: six new justices, one for every sitting justice over seventy. Even his own party said no. Democrats who loved the New Deal wouldn't break the Court to save it. This isn't building a state. It's a president who wouldn't take no for an answer.

Maya: Court-packing: six seats, one for each justice over seventy, his own party killed it. Marcus — the guest says Roosevelt wouldn't take no for an answer. Fair?

Marcus: On court-packing, fair. It was overreach. But watch what happened next: the Court stopped killing New Deal laws, justices retired, and Roosevelt replaced them. The Wagner Act survived. Social Security survived. And the Second New Deal of 1935 answered his left-wing critics.

Guest: The ones he was scared of. Huey Long: "Every Man a King," cap fortunes at fifty million dollars, a five-thousand-dollar homestead for every family, two thousand a year income. And Father Coughlin, the radio priest, millions of listeners, angrier every week. Roosevelt didn't move left from conviction. Long polled like a threat; Coughlin preached to millions. The Second New Deal was a bid to keep the pitchforks down.

Maya: The critics drove the policy, then. The AAA paid farmers to plow crops under while the Dust Bowl buried Oklahoma in black blizzards. Marcus — defend that image.

Marcus: I can't make it pretty. The logic was prices: a decade of overproduction had crushed farmers, so Washington paid them to grow less. But the checks went to landowners, and sharecroppers — Black tenants especially — got evicted instead of relieved.

Marcus: Two different AAAs. The Court killed the first one in 1936, so Congress wrote a second that fit the Court's rules. Same for the NRA: the codes died in 1935, but the impulse behind them came back in later laws. Losing in court didn't end the agenda. It edited it.

Guest: Edited it into something the Court would swallow. And the count the history books skip: the people the New Deal wrote out entirely. Domestic and farm workers were excluded from Social Security at the start — which meant most Black workers in the South got nothing. Southern Democrats in Congress demanded those holes, and Roosevelt paid the price to keep his coalition. The CCC ran segregated camps. WPA wages flowed through local officials who shorted Black workers. You call it the modern American state. For millions of Black Americans, the modern American state arrived with a whites-only sign on the relief office.

Maya: Hardest hit of the night. Let me sit with it a second. Marcus?

Marcus: It's true. The exclusions were the price of Southern votes, and it's the ugliest line in the New Deal's ledger. But here's the other half: Black voters noticed anyway. In 1936 they left the party of Lincoln for Roosevelt's Democrats, drawn by the relief that reached them and by people like Mary McLeod Bethune, who ran the National Youth Administration's Black division. A flawed lifeline is still a lifeline. And that 1936 coalition ran American politics for a generation.

Maya: Closing statements. One paragraph each. Guest, you first.

Guest: Judge it by its own promise: recovery. After six years and billions spent, one worker in six still had no job, and only a world war finished what the New Deal couldn't. It fed people, and I grant that. But it taught Washington that spending fixes everything, saddled the country with debt, stretched the Constitution until the Court snapped back, and built its winning coalition by writing Black workers out of the deal. Relief is not recovery. And a program that needs a world war to succeed is not a success.

Marcus: Judge it by what lasted. The CCC's three billion trees are still standing. The TVA still powers the valley. The FDIC sticker is still on the bank door, Social Security still arrives, and unions still bargain under the Wagner Act's protection. The New Deal didn't end the Depression. The war did that, with New Deal-scale spending. But it ended the idea that the government watches suffering from the sidelines. Your post office mural, the bronze plaque at the trailhead: the New Deal is still in the walls.

Maya: My verdict. Three boxes, let's land them. One: the three R's in action. Relief: the CCC, the WPA, millions working. Recovery: the AAA, the TVA, the NRA's codes while they lasted. Reform: Social Security, the Wagner Act, the FDIC, the SEC. First New Deal, 1933: relief and recovery. Second, 1935: reform. The bank holiday, the fireside chats.

Guest: No objection.

Maya: Two: the opposition. Long's Share Our Wealth and Coughlin on the left; the Liberty League and the Conservative Manifesto on the right; the Court killing the NRA and the AAA; the court-packing plan dead because his own party wouldn't break the Court. And seventeen percent unemployment in 1939 — the number that keeps this debate alive.

Marcus: Conceded. The number's real.

Maya: Three: the verdict. Did it work? It didn't end the Depression. The war did that. But it rewrote the government's job description, it built the 1936 coalition, and it left holes it can't defend: farm and domestic workers shut out, Southern Democrats holding the pen. So: relief, yes. Recovery, no. Reform: the part we're still living inside. Checking all three.

Marcus: There's the debate.

Maya: Check your three boxes. Episode six in the books. Next time: the road to war — neutrality laws, cash-and-carry, and a quiet December morning in Hawaii.

Maya: The New Deal didn't end the Depression —

Marcus: but it changed what the government is for.
