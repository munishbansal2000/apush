# APUSH Unit 7, Lesson 7: The Cuban Missile Crisis
# Script v3 DRAFT — Maya + Marcus interview, directed for Fish Audio
# Read note: Maya (host, student-surrogate) + Marcus (expert). Tone tags in brackets at turn starts are Fish Audio direction — strip all bracketed tags, this header, and every pause tag before TTS; convert pause tags to real silence. [10-second pause] marks are production silence for prediction beats, self-test questions, and the fast bonus — they never go to the voice. Cold open runs ~30 seconds. Intended duration ~11 min at ≤180 WPM, experienced runtime (spoken words plus every scripted pause second).
# Pronunciation: Khrushchev (KROOSH-chef), ExComm (EKS-kom)

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time, Marcus promised you Cuba, Soviet missiles, and the closest the Cold War ever came to going hot. This is that episode. October 1962: a spy plane's photographs land on the president's desk, and for thirteen days the world holds its breath. Four boxes today: the Bay of Pigs, the thirteen days, the secret Turkey trade, and the hotline and the Test Ban that came after. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now — and give me about eleven minutes.

Marcus: The setup fits in one breath. Fidel Castro took power in Cuba in 1959, and by 1961 his government was leaning on Moscow. That April, the US backed an invasion by anti-Castro exiles, the Bay of Pigs, and Castro's army crushed it in days.

Maya: [confident tone] So American troops stormed the beaches at the Bay of Pigs, right?

Marcus: [firm] The fighters were Cuban exiles. Washington trained and armed them, and they went in as exiles. A public humiliation for the new Kennedy administration. That failure is what pushed Cuba toward Moscow for protection.

Maya: [conversational] I know this episode from the movie, by the way. Thirteen Days, where Kevin Costner plays one of Kennedy's guys. My history teacher showed it in tenth grade.

Marcus: The movie gets the mood right: the waiting, the rooms going quiet. The ending it hands you? We'll come back to that.

Marcus: Mid-October 1962. American U-2 reconnaissance flights photograph Soviet missile sites under construction in Cuba. The analysts circle the shapes, the photos go to President Kennedy's desk, and the room goes quiet.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Wait. Missiles in Cuba? How close are we talking?

Marcus: Cuba sits just off the Florida coast. And the books give the Soviets two reasons for putting them there: protecting the Castro government after the Bay of Pigs, and putting pressure on the United States.

Marcus: And from Moscow's side of the table, there was a mirror image. The United States had stationed its own nuclear missiles in Turkey back in the late fifties. Turkey sits as close to the Soviet Union as Cuba sits to us. So to Khrushchev, Cuba just evened the board.

Maya: [incredulous] Hold on. The US had nuclear missiles parked on the Soviet Union's border first? And we were shocked when they returned the favor?

Marcus: The symmetry is uncomfortable, and it's worth sitting with. Washington was genuinely shocked, though historians still argue about how surprised they should have been.

Marcus: Kennedy pulls together a small crisis team, the Executive Committee of the National Security Council. ExComm, for short. For days they argue in secret while the public knows nothing. The options on the table: air strikes on the missile sites, or a full invasion of Cuba.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Why not skip the debate and just invade? Land the troops, grab the missiles, done.

Marcus: They debated that very option. And one of the books puts it plainly: the Soviets were weighing using their missiles if American troops invaded. So the invasion meant to remove the threat could have been the thing that triggered it.

Maya: Okay, your turn. You're Kennedy. Bomb the sites and risk missing one, or move slowly and risk looking weak. What do you pick?

[10-second pause]

Maya: I'm picking slow. Because if you bomb and miss even one missile... that's Florida.

Marcus: That instinct is the one Kennedy followed. Pressure without the point of no return.

Marcus: So Kennedy goes with the navy. On October 22 he goes on national television, tells the country about the missiles, and orders American ships to stop Soviet vessels headed for Cuba. He called the move a quarantine.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So "quarantine" is just a nicer word for the same thing?

Marcus: [firm] Same ships, different legal meaning. Princeton's book makes this exact point: under international rules, a blockade is an act of war. "Quarantine" lets Kennedy squeeze Cuba without declaring one.

Marcus: And on TV he draws the line: any nuclear missile fired from Cuba will be answered as a Soviet attack on the United States.

Maya: [confident tone] So this is where the Marines land in Cuba, right?

Marcus: [firm] The action stayed on the water. American ships stopped Soviet ships. That was the entire American move.

Maya: And if I write "blockade" on the exam?

Marcus: [firm] You still earn the point. The books call it that too. Quarantine is Kennedy's word, and it's the word that kept the move short of war.

Marcus: For almost two weeks, the world balances on the brink, the phrase the books keep coming back to. The quarantine holds. The missiles stay. Nobody blinks, and many of the orders went by slow letter while two nuclear arsenals sat on alert.

Maya: [confident tone] Okay, so the ending I know: Kennedy stares Khrushchev down, Khrushchev blinks, the missiles come out. Toughness wins.

Marcus: [firm] The movie version, sure. The archives tell a different story. In public, the Soviets agree to pull their missiles out of Cuba, and the United States promises never to invade the island. In secret, the United States agrees to pull its own missiles out of Turkey within months.

Maya: [sheepish] So the public saw resolve, and the real story was a trade. The Turkey missiles nobody was supposed to know about.

Marcus: Exactly. And the trade worked because each side got what it needed most. Khrushchev protects Cuba and gets the Turkey missiles gone; Kennedy gets the Cuban missiles out without firing a shot. Days later, Moscow announces the missiles are coming home, and the crisis ends.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Wait, so Khrushchev got the Turkey missiles and the no-invasion promise? He did pretty well for the guy who lost.

Marcus: The irony is real. On television it looked like an American win. At the table, Khrushchev walked away with Cuba protected and the Turkey missiles gone. Princeton's book puts it dryly: the secret deal made it look like the United States had won.

Maya: Let me check my sheet: that's three boxes down. Bay of Pigs, the thirteen days, the Turkey trade. One to go.

Marcus: [serious tone] Box four is what the scare built. Both leaders walked away shaken. The machinery had nearly run on its own, with the letters moving slowly across the ocean while the clock kept running. That was the scare.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So what changed after? They couldn't just go back to letters.

Marcus: They built two things. First, a direct hotline between the White House and the Kremlin.

Maya: The red telephone from the movies?

Marcus: The movies love the red telephone. What Washington and Moscow actually built was a direct line. The point was speed: leaders could talk in minutes instead of waiting on slow letters while nuclear weapons sat on alert.

Marcus: Second, in 1963, the Limited Test Ban Treaty: the United States, the Soviet Union, and Britain agree to stop testing nuclear weapons in the atmosphere, in space, and underwater.

Maya: [confident tone] So the treaty ended the arms race. Crisis solved, everybody stops testing.

Marcus: [firm] The arms race kept going, and underground tests stayed legal. Premium2027 says that flat out. What the treaty cut was atmospheric testing, which mattered because of fallout fears.

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] So the verdict. Containment, the American idea since 1947 of stopping Soviet power wherever it spread, got stretched to the absolute brink here. What finally worked was the trade. The missiles came out of Cuba and the invasion never happened. Nobody fired. Each side got what it needed most.

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] And notice what changed. Every earlier containment crisis, the Berlin blockade, Korea, stayed conventional. This one put Soviet nuclear weapons just off the Florida coast. Containment held, and the price of holding it was thirteen days on the nuclear brink.

Maya: And the closest-the-world-ever-came part? Kennedy himself put a number on it. One in ten?

Marcus: [speaking slowly] The historian Graham Allison, who wrote the book on this crisis, says Kennedy put the chances of the whole thing ending in war at between [emphasis] one in three and even. One in three. Allison's reported version of Kennedy's own estimate.

Maya: One in three. [beat] Wow. A lot closer than the movie made it feel.

Maya: Four boxes, let's land them. Box one: the Bay of Pigs, April 1961: the US backs an exile invasion of Cuba, Castro's army crushes it, and the humiliation pushes Cuba toward Moscow for protection. Checking that one.

Marcus: The setup the whole crisis grows out of.

Maya: Box two: the thirteen days. October 1962: U-2 photos catch Soviet missile sites going up in Cuba. ExComm argues in secret: air strikes or a quarantine. Kennedy picks the quarantine on October 22, because calling it a blockade would have meant war. And the quarantine ran... into December?

Marcus: A few more weeks. It lifted once the missiles were confirmed out. Keep going.

Maya: Right. Box three: the secret trade. Publicly, Soviet missiles out of Cuba and a US promise never to invade. Secretly, American missiles out of Turkey within months. The public saw toughness; the real story was a deal. That one's checked.

Maya: Box four: the safety net. The hotline between the White House and the Kremlin, and the Limited Test Ban Treaty of 1963: no more tests in the atmosphere, in space, or underwater, while the arms race kept going and underground tests stayed legal. Checked.

Marcus: [confident tone] Four for four. Episode landed.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One more, fast, at the end for the fourth box.

Maya: Question one, stimulus style. It's October 1962, and a U-2 photograph lands on your desk showing Soviet missile sites going up in Cuba, just off the Florida coast. Two parts: why did the Soviets put them there, and why did Kennedy call the American response a "quarantine" instead of a "blockade"?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The Soviets wanted to shield the Castro government after the Bay of Pigs and put pressure on the United States, straight from the books. And Kennedy said "quarantine" because a blockade is legally an act of war; the word let him use the navy without starting one. The reasoning lives in the word choice: same ships, but the name decides whether it's war.

Maya: Question two. What was the actual deal that ended the crisis, and why did most Americans at the time believe Kennedy had simply won by being tough?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Publicly: Soviet missiles out of Cuba, and America promises never to invade the island. Secretly: American missiles out of Turkey within months. Most Americans believed the toughness story because the Turkey half stayed secret. The archives told the real story later.

Maya: Question three. Why do historians call this the closest the world has come to nuclear war, and what number did Kennedy reportedly put on the chances of war?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Because two nuclear arsenals spent thirteen days running on orders that traveled by letter. Historian Graham Allison reports Kennedy's own estimate of the chances of war: between one in three and even. That number comes from Kennedy himself, as Allison reports it.

Maya: One more, fast. After the crisis, the two sides build two things so the next one doesn't run on slow letters. Name them.

[10-second pause]

Maya: The hotline between the White House and the Kremlin, and the Limited Test Ban Treaty.

Marcus: [calm] Thirteen days. One photograph, one quarantine, and a deal nobody was supposed to know about.

Maya: Check your boxes. Next time: we come home. Lyndon Johnson, the Great Society, and what a government does when it decides to end poverty itself.
