Maya: Last time: the progressives tried to fix America from the inside. This time the world explodes. 1914, Europe goes to war with itself, and America spends three years insisting it's not our fight. Then it becomes our fight. The debate: was jumping in justified? Three boxes on your sheet: the U-boats, the Zimmermann telegram, and the money and the mind-change. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes, and you'll check all three off.

Marcus: And I'll warn you, this debate has no clean winner. Both sides have real ammunition.

Maya: Set the scene first. 1914, who was fighting whom?

Marcus: Two alliance blocs. The Central Powers: Germany, Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman Empire. The Allies: Britain, France, Russia, and later Italy. When it erupted, President Woodrow Wilson proclaimed American neutrality. He sympathized with the Allies privately, but he had zero interest in sending Americans into trench warfare. After the Lusitania sinking in 1915 he gave a speech saying America was, quote, "too proud to fight."

Maya: "Too proud to fight." Then he won reelection in 1916 on "he kept us out of war," and five months later asked Congress for war. What changed?

Marcus: That question is the whole episode. Let's do the causes layer by layer, because no single one did it alone. Start with box one: the U-boats.

Maya: Unrestricted submarine warfare. Germany sinking ships without warning.

Marcus: Right. International law said a warship had to surface, stop a merchant ship, and let civilians off before sinking it. A submarine that surfaces is a dead submarine, so Germany just didn't do it. In May 1915, a U-boat torpedoed the Lusitania, a British passenger liner, off the coast of Ireland. Nearly twelve hundred people died, a hundred twenty-eight of them Americans.

Maya: I get seasick on the Catalina ferry. I can't imagine a torpedo.

Marcus: The country was outraged, and plenty of people wanted war right then. But Wilson squeezed a deal out of Germany: no more attacks on passenger liners. The Sussex pledge, 1916, same idea, broader. Germany backed down because it dreaded American entry more than it loved the U-boats.

Maya: So the crisis cooled. Then what reignited it?

Marcus: German desperation. By early 1917 the war was a stalemate, and the German high command made a cold calculation. Resume unrestricted submarine warfare, starve Britain into surrender within months, and bet that America couldn't mobilize fast enough to matter. They announced it in January 1917 knowing it would probably bring America in. The gamble was speed. Wilson answered with armed neutrality: real guns on American merchant ships, a halfway house between peace and war. It lasted barely a month.

Maya: Box one, then. The U-boats: the Lusitania in 1915, the pledges that paused it, the 1917 resumption as a deliberate gamble.

Marcus: On to box two now, and this one's my favorite to teach: the Zimmermann telegram.

Maya: I've actually seen this one read aloud on YouTube. It's wild.

Marcus: It still lands. February 1917, British intelligence intercepts a coded telegram from Arthur Zimmermann, Germany's foreign minister, to the German ambassador in Mexico. The offer: if America enters the war, Mexico should attack the United States, and Germany will help Mexico reconquer the territory it lost, Texas, New Mexico, Arizona. Quote: "make war together, make peace together."

Maya: Germany promising Mexico the American Southwest. That's insane.

Marcus: It was published in American newspapers on March first, and the public went from angry to furious. Now here's the isolationist counter, and it's strong: Mexico was in the middle of a revolution, in no shape to invade anyone. The telegram was a fantasy. It changed no military facts.

Maya: So was the telegram the reason we went to war? Like, the single reason?

Marcus: Careful, that's the trap. It was the last straw, not the whole haystack. The U-boats were already sinking American merchant ships. The telegram just made the threat feel personal. Layered causes, not a single trigger.

Maya: Okay, so what about the French side? What did 1917 look like from Paris?

French Guest: From Paris, spring 1917: the French army has just lived through mutinies, Russia is collapsing into revolution, and Britain is bleeding out at sea. The Allies are exhausted. Without fresh American troops and American money, they lose. That's not propaganda. That's our general staff's actual math. We are asking for soldiers, not sympathy, and fast.

Maya: So box two: the Zimmermann telegram. The text, the timing, and why it landed like a bomb though Mexico couldn't have delivered.

Marcus: Now box three: the money and the mind-change. And the money part is uncomfortable.

Maya: Because we weren't really neutral, economically.

Marcus: Exactly. American banks had loaned the Allies more than two billion dollars, versus something like twenty-seven million to Germany. That's not a typo, it's a ratio of nearly a hundred to one. American factories were feeding the Allied war machine. So the isolationists ask: was neutrality ever real, or was it a fiction we told ourselves while cashing Allied checks?

Maya: And if Germany had won, those loans evaporate. So America had a financial stake in an Allied victory.

Marcus: The loans explain the tilt, not the decision. Add British propaganda, which was excellent, and German sabotage, which was real but clumsy, and the information environment tilted hard toward the Allies.

Maya: So clash round two: the principles. Washington's Farewell Address said avoid entangling alliances. Then Wilson says "the world must be made safe for democracy." Those don't fit together.

Marcus: They really don't, and the preparedness fight at home was vicious. Interventionists said America was defenseless in a world of U-boats. Isolationists said preparedness itself was a march toward war, that arms races cause the wars they're meant to prevent. Both sides claimed to be the true heirs of the founders.

Maya: And Wilson? He goes from "too proud to fight" to a war message in two years. What actually changed his mind?

Marcus: Nobody's fully sure, which is why historians still argue. The best reading: the January 1917 resumption convinced him Germany would never respect neutral rights, and the telegram convinced him Germany was an active threat to the hemisphere. His war message to Congress on April second, 1917, doesn't read like a man who wanted war. It reads like a man who ran out of alternatives.

Maya: Closing statements. One paragraph each — make your case.

Marcus: Interventionist verdict: Germany attacked American ships, killed American citizens, and schemed with Mexico to dismember the United States. No great power absorbs that and stays home. Wilson tried every alternative, and the war he asked for was framed as a war to end war itself.

Marcus: Isolationist verdict: America entered a European blood feud it didn't understand, driven by bankers' loans and British propaganda, abandoning a century of avoiding foreign quarrels. A hundred sixteen thousand Americans died, and the peace Wilson promised collapsed within a generation.

Maya: My verdict: the debate doesn't settle, and that's the point. What it set up is the next fight, over the peace. Because winning the war was the easy part. Deciding what the victory meant, that broke the country in half.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: the U-boats. The Lusitania in 1915, twelve hundred dead, the pledges that bought time, and Germany's 1917 gamble that speed would beat American mobilization.

Marcus: Checked.

Maya: Two: the Zimmermann telegram. Zimmermann's offer to Mexico, Texas, New Mexico, Arizona in exchange for war on America, published March 1917. The last straw, not the whole haystack.

Marcus: On the sheet.

Maya: Three: the money and the mind-change. Two billion in Allied loans versus pocket change to Germany, the tilt nobody called neutral, and Wilson's journey from "too proud to fight" to the war message of April 1917.

Marcus: And that's the set.

Maya: Two questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One: a textbook prints this line from the Zimmermann telegram: Germany proposes Mexico "make war together, make peace together" in exchange for help reconquering lost territory. The question asks: why did this message, more than the loans or the propaganda, push the American public over the edge?

[5-second pause]

Maya: Because it made the threat personal and territorial. Loans were abstract, but a German scheme to carve up the Southwest felt like an attack on the country itself. It turned a European war into an American one in the public mind.

Maya: Two: list three steps on the road from neutrality to war in 1917.

[5-second pause]

Maya: January: Germany resumes unrestricted submarine warfare. March: the Zimmermann telegram goes public. April: Wilson asks Congress for war, and the U.S. declares it on April sixth.

Maya: Check your three boxes. Next time: the war comes home, and it brings handcuffs. The home front, where the fight for democracy abroad met repression at home.

Marcus: Three years of "not our fight" —

Maya: ended by a telegram and a torpedo.
