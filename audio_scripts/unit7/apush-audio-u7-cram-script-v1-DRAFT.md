Maya: Unit 7 cram. 1890 to 1945, progressivism to the bomb, ten questions, eight minutes. I'll fire, you'll answer in your head, then I'll give you the model answer. Say it before I do. Go.

Maya: One. Name the four Progressive amendments and what each did.

[3-second pause]

Maya: 16th: the income tax, 1913, giving Washington a revenue stream that made everything later possible. 17th: direct election of senators, 1913, taking the choice from state legislatures after years of bribery scandals. 18th: Prohibition, 1919. 19th: women's suffrage, 1920, after seventy years of organizing. The trick is not mixing up 18 and 19. Alcohol before votes.

Marcus: And the pattern: each one took power from a back room and handed it to voters, or tried to legislate virtue. Progressivism in four strokes.

Maya: Two. Three steps from neutrality to war in 1917. List them.

[3-second pause]

Maya: Unrestricted submarine warfare, Germany sinking American ships without warning. The Zimmermann telegram, Germany offering Mexico its lost territory back if Mexico attacked the United States, intercepted and printed on the front page. And the money: billions in American loans to Britain and France, which made neutrality a fiction. Lusitania was 1915, it outraged the country, but it wasn't the trigger. 1917 was submarines, the telegram, and the loans.

Marcus: If you only remember one, remember the telegram. An actual German offer, intercepted, on the front page. That's the one that made it personal.

Maya: Three. The Espionage Act and Schenck. What precedent did wartime speech law set?

[3-second pause]

Maya: That the government can punish speech that creates a "clear and present danger," and in wartime the Court lets Washington define danger broadly. Schenck went to jail for handing out anti-draft leaflets in 1919. The precedent: civil liberties shrink in wartime, and the Court steps aside. You'll see this exact pattern again with Korematsu in 1944. Two wars, same move.

Maya: Four. Why did the Senate reject the League of Nations, and which group mattered more: the irreconcilables or the reservationists?

[3-second pause]

Maya: The irreconcilables wanted nothing to do with it, period. The reservationists, led by Lodge, would join with conditions, mostly protecting Congress's power to declare war. Wilson refused every compromise, toured the country for public support, had a stroke, and the treaty died in the Senate twice. The reservationists mattered more, because their votes were gettable and Wilson wouldn't take them.

Marcus: Wilson's rigidity is the whole story. Compromise was on the table. He left it there.

Maya: Five. Two drivers of 1920s prosperity, two signs it was fragile.

[3-second pause]

Maya: Drivers: mass production and mass consumption, cars and appliances rolling off assembly lines, plus installment credit letting everyone buy now and pay later. Fragile signs: the farm economy never recovered from the wartime boom and stayed depressed all decade, and wealth piled at the top while workers' wages stalled. Add a stock market running on margin, borrowed money on borrowed confidence. Prosperity with a cracked foundation.

Maya: Six. Crash versus Depression: what's the difference? Name two structural causes beyond the crash.

[3-second pause]

Maya: The crash was October 1929, the stock market collapsing in days. The Depression was the decade-long collapse underneath it: a quarter of workers jobless, banks failing by the thousand, the Dust Bowl on top. Structural causes: overproduction, factories making more than buyers could buy, and the banking system's fragility, thousands of small banks with no safety net. Plus the tariff walls and the farm crisis. The crash was the spark, not the fuel.

Marcus: That's the distinction the exam wants. Crash is an event. Depression is a system.

Maya: Seven. New Deal: one relief program, one recovery program, one reform program. Go.

[3-second pause]

Maya: Relief: the CCC, the Civilian Conservation Corps, young men paid to plant trees and build parks. Also FERA and later the WPA putting millions to work. Recovery: the AAA, the Agricultural Adjustment Act, paying farmers to cut production and lift prices, or the NRA trying to stabilize industry through codes. Reform: Social Security, 1935, old-age pensions and unemployment insurance, or the FDIC insuring bank deposits, or the SEC policing Wall Street. Relief feeds, recovery restarts, reform prevents.

Maya: Eight. Why did Japan attack Pearl Harbor, and what was America doing to Japan in 1941?

[3-second pause]

Maya: Japan needed Southeast Asia's oil and couldn't take it with the Pacific Fleet intact at Hawaii, so Yamamoto gambled on a knockout blow to buy a year or two to fortify the conquests. What America was doing: after Japan moved into Indochina, Roosevelt froze Japanese assets and cut off oil in July 1941, then the Hull Note in November demanded Japan quit China entirely. Tokyo read it as an ultimatum. Embargo, ultimatum, then December 7th, then Hitler declares war on America four days later.

Marcus: And the carriers survived. The gamble's math broke on day one.

Maya: Nine. Korematsu: what did the Court decide, and why does it still get cited?

[3-second pause]

Maya: The Court upheld internment in 1944, deferring to military judgment in wartime. It still gets cited as the twin of Schenck: the pattern of the Court stepping aside when the government claims national security, in two different wars. And it gets cited because the country reversed itself. The 1988 redress, the apology, the $20,000 payments, all admitted it was race prejudice, not security. A precedent and a warning in one case.

Maya: Ten. The bomb. Strongest one-sentence argument for each side.

[3-second pause]

Maya: For: the invasion would have cost hundreds of thousands of lives, and the bombs ended the war in days. Against: Japan was already defeated and the Soviet invasion may have been the real trigger, which makes the bomb look like a warning to Stalin. Historians still fight about it, and that's the point. The fight teaches you how evidence works.

Maya: Ten questions, done. Lightning round, one breath each, no pauses. Nye Committee: 1934, senators concluding we'd been conned into the First World War, the fuel for the Neutrality Acts. Quarantine speech: 1937, FDR's trial balloon against aggression, popped by isolationist backlash. Cash-and-carry: 1939, buy our weapons if you pay cash and haul them, which only Britain and France could use. Destroyers-for-bases: 1940, fifty old ships for British bases. Lend-Lease: March 1941, the arsenal of democracy, weapons as loans. Atlantic Charter: summer 1941, FDR and Churchill's war aims, self-determination for all peoples.

Marcus: Rosie the Riveter: women in manufacturing up 140 percent by 1944. Double V: the Pittsburgh Courier's demand, victory over fascism abroad and racism at home. Bracero program: 1942, Mexican farmworkers brought north. Executive Order 9066: February 1942, the order that interned around 120,000. Manhattan Project: the secret bomb program since 1942, Los Alamos, Oppenheimer. Potsdam Declaration: July 1945, unconditional surrender or "prompt and utter destruction."

Maya: The period thesis, one breath: between 1890 and 1945, America became a world power and the federal government became the central force in American life. Progressivism, world wars, the New Deal. Every box in this unit is that thesis wearing a different costume.

Marcus: And if you're predicting the DBQ: evaluate how much the New Deal changed the federal government's role, with the court-packing fight and the Second New Deal as your evidence. Or a wartime civil liberties prompt, Schenck versus Korematsu. Both are this unit's greatest hits.
