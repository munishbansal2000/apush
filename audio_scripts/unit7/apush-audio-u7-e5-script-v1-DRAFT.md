# Pronunciation: HAW-lee SMOOT (Hawley-Smoot), huh-ROO-vihl (Hooverville)

Maya: Last time: the roar: assembly lines, jazz, buy-now-pay-later. Now it's October 1929. The ticker tape can't keep up with the sell orders, and a crowd is gathering outside the Exchange on Broad Street. Three boxes on your sheet: the rot beneath the roar, from crash to Depression, Hoover. Circle the ones you still couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes, and you'll have checked all three off.

Marcus: Box one starts before the crash, because the crash didn't come from nowhere. The twenties economy had dry rot. First: overproduction. Factories and farms were making more than Americans could buy, and when supply outruns demand, prices fall, profits thin, and somebody stops hiring.

Maya: But the twenties were the boom. Who was overproducing during a boom?

Marcus: Farmers, for one, and they'd been hurting the whole decade. During the war they ramped up to feed the armies. When the war ended, the buyers vanished but the harvests didn't. Prices collapsed, debt piled up, and the farm depression never ended. It just waited for company.

Maya: So the countryside was already in a depression while the cities partied.

Marcus: The party was narrow at the top. The richest one percent held roughly a third of the nation's wealth. When that much money sits in that few hands, most families can't buy what the factories make. Economists call it maldistribution: underconsumption wearing a tuxedo.

Maya: So the economy needed shoppers it didn't have.

Marcus: Economists still argue it: if wages had been higher, spending holds, and the spiral never starts. Maybe.

Maya: My grandpa saves every rubber band and twist tie. Jars of them. My mom says he's a Depression kid.

Marcus: It rewired a generation. Scarcity that deep doesn't leave you. Keep that jar in mind; we're about to fill it.

Maya: Okay, box two: the crash itself. Black Tuesday, October 29th. The market fell off a cliff, and that caused the Depression. Right?

Marcus: The poster version, not the archive version. The crash was the trigger. The rot was the cause. But the trigger matters, so walk through it. All decade, stocks looked unstoppable, so people bought on margin: putting down five or ten percent and borrowing the rest. When prices dipped, brokers demanded their money back, margin calls, and borrowers had to sell at any price, which pushed prices lower, which triggered more calls.

Maya: A machine for turning a dip into a landslide.

Maya: So who was actually in the market? Was the whole country day-trading?

Marcus: Most weren't. Only a fraction of Americans owned any stock at all. The crash wiped out the investors — but the Depression reached everyone else through the banks and the layoffs. You didn't need a portfolio to lose your savings or your job.

Marcus: By Black Tuesday the market had lost nearly forty percent from the September peak. And then came the second shoe: the banks. Americans panicked and rushed to withdraw their savings, and the banks couldn't pay, because there was no deposit insurance yet and most banks were tiny single-town operations. One bad week could kill them.

Maya: Wait — no deposit insurance at all? Like, your money was just... trust?

Marcus: Trust and a prayer. When a bank failed, its deposits died with it. Thousands failed, and every failure shrank the money supply: it collapsed by about a third. Less money, less spending, more failures, less money. A spiral with no floor.

Maya: Give me the bottom. How bad?

Marcus: By 1933, about a quarter of American workers had no job. Thousands of banks were gone. Breadlines in city after city, and the prosperity of 1929 looked like a rumor.

Maya: So that's what made it Great. Panics had happened before. This one ate the money itself.

Marcus: And then it went global. Europe's recovery ran on American loans, and when the loans stopped, Europe seized up. Washington made it worse: the Hawley-Smoot tariff jacked up import taxes, so trading partners retaliated and world trade shrank.

Maya: So we exported the crash.

Marcus: With interest. The war debts tangled everything: Europe owed Washington, Germany owed Europe reparations. When American lending froze, the entire global payment chain seized. And the gold standard locked everyone into the same sinking ship: countries couldn't print money to fight the slump without breaking the rules.

Maya: We read the first chapter of The Grapes of Wrath in English — the Joads, the dust, the bank taking the farm. Steinbeck, right? Okies?

Marcus: Steinbeck, 1939. And he's your bridge: the Dust Bowl made the farm crisis visible: black blizzards rolling across Oklahoma, while the banks failed in New York. Same rot, two weathers.

Maya: Box three: Hoover. The do-nothing president. That's the label. Is it fair?

Marcus: Start with what he believed. Hoover's answer was voluntarism: businesses, charities, and local governments should fix this, not Washington. The market would correct itself. He had genuine sympathy for the suffering. This wasn't cruelty; it was theory.

Maya: Theory doesn't fill a soup line.

Marcus: The soup lines agreed. Families foreclosed on their homes built shantytowns from scrap lumber and tin — and named them Hoovervilles, so the president would know exactly what they thought of his theory. When even Hoover saw voluntarism failing, he created the Reconstruction Finance Corporation to lend to banks and businesses.

Maya: Lend to banks — not people?

Marcus: Banks, railroads, big employers. The idea was: help would flow downhill. It mostly pooled at the top.

Maya: So he did do something.

Maya: Did he believe his own theory to the end?

Marcus: He called it rugged individualism: the idea that government help would rot American character. Voters disagreed. In 1932 they fired him in a landslide for Franklin Roosevelt, who promised the opposite — a government that would act.

Marcus: Then came the anecdote that ended him: the Bonus Army. Thousands of World War I veterans marched on Washington in 1932 demanding early payment of a promised bonus. Hoover sent in the Army, MacArthur with tanks, and routed them. Burning tents on the newsreels.

Maya: American soldiers attacking American veterans. On camera.

Marcus: A young Dwight Eisenhower was there too, on MacArthur's staff — he advised against the whole thing. Twelve years later he'd be ordering whole armies across Europe, and the contrast wasn't lost on him.

Marcus: So is "do-nothing" fair? Half. Hoover did create the RFC, and he was working inside the laissez-faire consensus of his party. But voluntarism was a garden hose on a house fire, and he never let go of the hose.

Maya: Check my chain: rot, trigger, banks, Hoover?

Marcus: Rot, trigger, banks, world, Hoover. The world goes in the middle — don't drop it.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: the rot beneath the roar: overproduction, a farm depression that never ended, and the richest one percent holding roughly a third of the wealth. A boom most families couldn't buy into.

Marcus: Rot confirmed.

Maya: Two: from crash to Depression: buying on margin, Black Tuesday, then the bank runs. No deposit insurance, tiny banks, and the money supply collapsing by about a third. Then Hawley-Smoot and the gold standard carried it worldwide.

Marcus: Landed.

Maya: The money supply fell by a third because... the banks failed, and the deposits just vanished? That's legal?

Marcus: It was, then. No insurance meant a failed bank took its deposits to the grave. Less money chasing goods, more failures, less money. The spiral.

Maya: Three: Hoover: voluntarism, the RFC's too-little-too-late loans, the Bonus Army routed by MacArthur's tanks, and the Hoovervilles. Do-nothing is half fair: he acted, but never dropped the theory.

Marcus: All three, checked.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One: a textbook prints a 1930 photograph: a crowd packed into a bank lobby, everyone demanding their deposits at once. The prompt asks: why did scenes like this turn a market crash into a decade-long depression?

[5-second pause]

Maya: The crash broke the market; the runs broke the banks. With no deposit insurance and thousands of tiny banks, each failure vaporized its deposits, and the money supply shrank by about a third. Panic became arithmetic: less money, less spending, more failures.

Maya: Two: Hoover, do-nothing president? One sentence, with evidence.

[5-second pause]

Maya: Not nothing. He built the RFC. But voluntarism was a garden hose on a house fire, and the Bonus Army's burning tents say the rest.

Maya: Three: a textbook calls the twenties boom rotten underneath. Name two weak spots the crash exposed.

[5-second pause]

Maya: Overproduction outran demand while wages lagged, and farms were already hurting. Plus speculation on margin, a market stacked on borrowed money. The boom was borrowed. The bill came due.

Maya: Check your three boxes. Episode five in the books. Next time: March 1933, a bank holiday, a hundred days, and a president who talks to you on the radio.

Marcus: The crash pulled the trigger —

Maya: but the rot had loaded the gun.
