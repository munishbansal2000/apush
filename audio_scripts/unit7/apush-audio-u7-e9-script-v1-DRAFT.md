Maya: Last time: December 7th, 1941, the war arrives, and America goes in. This time the war comes home. The government spends like never before, the country moves like never before, and then it cages a hundred and twenty thousand of its own people. Three boxes on your sheet: the mobilization miracle, the great moving, internment. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes, and you'll check all three off.

Marcus: The thesis for this one is a paradox. The war made the federal government the most powerful force in American life, and made America more free and less free at the same time.

Maya: More free and less free. Pick one.

Marcus: You can't, that's the point. The war ended the Depression, pulled women and Black Americans and Mexican workers into jobs they'd never been offered, and locked up American citizens by ancestry. Same war, same government.

Maya: Okay, box one. The mobilization miracle. Sell it.

Marcus: In 1940, unemployment sat around fourteen or fifteen percent. The Depression never really left. By 1944 it was about one percent, the lowest ever recorded. The economy grew by roughly half in four years, and the federal government did it by spending money at a scale nobody had ever seen. War production, not the New Deal, is what finally killed the Depression.

Maya: Wait. So the New Deal didn't end the Depression? I've written "New Deal ends Depression" on like three essays.

Marcus: It's the most common wrong answer in American history. The New Deal relieved the Depression. It didn't end it. The government ordering more tanks and planes and ships than anyone thought possible, and paying for all of it, did. Roosevelt called America the arsenal of democracy. He meant it literally.

Maya: And Rosie. The poster woman.

Marcus: Rosie the Riveter. The reality behind the poster was bigger than the poster. By the summer of 1944, around nineteen million women were working, up roughly half from 1940. In aircraft plants, women went from about one percent of the workforce to roughly two-thirds, because somebody had to build everything.

Maya: I actually know something here. That famous "We Can Do It!" poster, the one with the bandana? That was a Westinghouse in-house thing. Barely anybody saw it until the eighties. The Rosie people actually knew was Norman Rockwell's, the Saturday Evening Post cover.

Marcus: The Westinghouse poster really was an internal memo. The symbol outgrew the history.

Maya: Did it stick, though? Did women keep the jobs?

Marcus: Mostly no. When the men came home, the factories laid the women off and the culture told them to want the kitchen. But the door didn't fully close, and the daughters of Rosie would remember the poster, not the pink slip.

Maya: Who actually ran all this? Who decided what got built?

Marcus: Alphabet agencies. The War Production Board told factories what to make, the Office of Price Administration ran rationing and held down prices, war bonds soaked up savings. By 1944 American industry outproduced all the Axis powers combined. And compare it with last unit's war: in 1917 the government sold the war with propaganda and jailed the critics. In this war it ran the economy. Different machine, different muscle.

Maya: Okay, the great moving. Who moved where?

Marcus: Start with the biggest current inside the country, the Second Great Migration. During the war years, roughly a million Black Southerners left the South for the North and West. The push: the boll weevil eating the cotton, Jim Crow, lynching. The pull: factory wages double or triple what sharecropping paid, and a Black newspaper, the Chicago Defender, running a recruiting campaign. Come north.

Maya: A million people following a newspaper.

Marcus: Second current: the bracero program. In 1942 the United States signed a deal with Mexico, bracero means hired hands, to bring in Mexican farmworkers on temporary contracts. It was supposed to be a wartime emergency. It lasted until 1964, twenty-two years.

Maya: Supposed to be temporary, lasted twenty-two years. Classic.

Marcus: Third current: soldiers and sailors and their families pouring into the West, the shipyards, the aircraft plants.

Maya: So the country's map gets redrawn. Did the war actually make things better for Black Americans?

Marcus: Better and not better. Over a million Black Americans served, in a segregated army. The Tuskegee Airmen flew as the first Black military pilots. And at home the Black press ran the Double V campaign: victory against fascism abroad, victory against racism at home. They came back asking why they'd fought for democracy in Europe and still couldn't vote in Mississippi.

Maya: Fighting for democracy abroad, then coming home to Jim Crow.

Marcus: Not yet. But the question never went away again. Movement built the civil rights movement's audience.

Maya: Okay — internment. I went to Manzanar once, the camp in California, it's a historic site now. The barracks and the guard tower are still there. It doesn't look real until you're standing in it.

Marcus: February 1942, two months after Pearl Harbor: Roosevelt signs Executive Order 9066, and the whole West Coast becomes a military zone. About a hundred and twenty thousand people of Japanese ancestry, two-thirds of them American citizens, get forty-eight hours to pack two suitcases and report for removal. Homes, farms, businesses, sold for pennies or just lost.

Maya: Hold on. Were these prisoners of war? Like captured soldiers?

Marcus: No, and that's the confusion to kill. These were civilians. American families, kids. The government's stated reason was military necessity. No evidence of it. Not one case of espionage by a Japanese American was ever proven. The policy was ancestry, not evidence.

Maya: So how did the Supreme Court let that stand?

Marcus: Korematsu versus the United States, 1944. Fred Korematsu refused to go and took it to the Court. The Court upheld the internment, six to three, saying wartime necessity allowed it. The pattern isn't new: in 1919, Schenck versus the United States let the government jail a man for a pamphlet, "clear and present danger." Different war, same habit. The Court defers to the military when the country feels afraid.

Maya: Did anyone on the inside fight back? Not the courts, the people.

Marcus: The 442nd Regimental Combat Team. Japanese American soldiers, many with families behind the barbed wire, volunteered and fought in Europe. Motto: "Go for Broke." The most decorated unit for its size and length of service in American military history. Twenty-one Medals of Honor. Fighting for a country that had caged their parents.

Maya: That sentence should be illegal to say out loud.

Marcus: It's the whole episode in one unit. And the ending: in 1988 the government apologized and paid twenty thousand dollars to each surviving internee. The Civil Liberties Act. Forty-six years later.

Maya: Forty-six years. Internment: 1942, the order, the camps. Korematsu says the Court allows it, the 442nd fights anyway, and Congress apologizes in... 1988?

Marcus: 1988. Checked.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: the mobilization miracle. Government spending finally ends the Depression, unemployment from about fifteen percent to about one, women flood the factories, and the alphabet agencies run the economy. Two: the great moving. The Second Great Migration, the bracero deal, the Double V. Three: internment. Executive Order 9066, ancestry treated as evidence, Korematsu, the 442nd, and the 1988 apology.

Marcus: And that's the set.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One: a textbook prints the "We Can Do It!" poster next to a photo of women riveting airplane wings. The prompt asks what the pairing tells you about the war's effect on women. What's the point of the source?

[5-second pause]

Maya: The point is the gap. The poster sells liberation, the photo shows real labor, and the real story is both. Millions of women proved they could do industrial work, and most got pushed back out when the war ended.

Maya: Two: one similarity and one difference between the World War One home front and this one.

[5-second pause]

Maya: Similarity: both wars grew federal power and squeezed civil liberties, Schenck in 1919, Korematsu in 1944. Difference: the first war punished speech with the Espionage and Sedition Acts. This one caged people by ancestry, while opening jobs to women and Black Americans on a scale the first war never did.

Maya: Three: why do some historians push back on calling this "the Good War"?

[5-second pause]

Maya: Because the phrase hides the price: the internment camps, the segregated army, the jobs that vanished after the victory parades. Studs Terkel gave the war its nickname in the eighties, and the historians who push back say a good war doesn't cage its own citizens.

Maya: Check your three boxes. The war at home, in the books. Next time: the hardest decision of the twentieth century. August 1945, and whether to drop the bomb.

Marcus: The government at its most powerful —

Maya: and its least accountable. Same war.
