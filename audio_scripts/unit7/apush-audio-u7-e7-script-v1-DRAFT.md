# Episode 7: The Road to Pearl Harbor. ~8 min.
# Format: Story Mode (Marcus solo)
# Read note: Story Mode — Marcus narrates solo, no Maya. Present-tense scenes carry the story; the history lands inside the scenes, never as lecture. The em dash in the closing line is a held beat: leave a full breath before the final sentence. Do not rush it. Strip this header and the read note before TTS.
# Pronunciation: yah-mah-MOH-toh (Yamamoto), oh-PAH-nah (Opana Point), NY (Nye, rhymes with "sky")

Marcus: Seven o'clock on a Sunday morning, December 7th, 1941. Two privates on a hilltop on Oahu's north shore, manning a brand-new radar set. Private Joseph Lockard and Private George Elliott. Their shift ended at seven, but the truck to breakfast is running late, so they stay on for extra practice. At two minutes past seven, the screen fills with the largest blip either of them has ever seen. A mass of planes, a hundred and thirty-two miles out, coming fast and coming straight.

Marcus: They call it in. The officer on duty listens, and tells them not to worry about it. A flight of American bombers is expected in from California that morning. Probably just those. Forget it. So the two privates keep watching the blips close in, until the signal dissolves into the island's ground clutter. Less than an hour later, the first wave hits Pearl Harbor.

Marcus: To understand how two kids on a hilltop ended up holding the morning in their hands, rewind ten years. America in the early thirties wants one thing: never again. Never another 1917. A Senate investigation called the Nye Committee tells the country it was tricked into the First World War — that bankers and arms makers, the so-called merchants of death, pushed America in for profit. Most of its findings don't hold up. Doesn't matter. The mood is set. The last war was a con, and the country won't be conned twice.

Marcus: The mood hardens into a movement. In 1940, the America First Committee fills stadiums: Charles Lindbergh, the most famous man in America, telling crowds the last war was a mistake and the next one isn't America's business. Roosevelt calls them naive. They call him a warmonger. The country isn't just neutral by law. It's neutral by conviction.

Marcus: So Congress builds a wall of laws. Three Neutrality Acts, 1935 through 1937. No selling weapons to warring nations. No loans. Americans can't even sail on belligerent ships. The idea is simple: cut every rope that dragged America into the last war, and the next war can't pull America in.

Marcus: And then the thirties happen. Japan invades Manchuria in 1931 and keeps marching into China. Italy invades Ethiopia. Germany rearms, remilitarizes the Rhineland, and starts swallowing its neighbors. The neutrality laws treat every warring nation the same, which means they punish the victim exactly as much as the aggressor. When China begs for American help against Japan, the Neutrality Acts say no. Neutrality on paper favors whoever is already winning.

Marcus: In 1937, President Roosevelt tests the waters. In Chicago, he gives a speech saying aggressive nations should be quarantined — cut off, like a sick patient isolated from the well. The country hears it and hates it. The backlash is so strong that Roosevelt backs off for years. Weeks later, Japanese planes sink the American gunboat Panay on a Chinese river. Even that gets brushed aside, settled with an apology and an indemnity. Isolationism is that strong.

Marcus: Then 1939. Germany invades Poland, and Congress loosens one bolt. Cash-and-carry: a warring nation can buy American weapons if it pays cash and hauls them away on its own ships. And here's the quiet part: only Britain and France have the cash and the ships. Germany doesn't. So-called neutral law, deeply un-neutral result.

Marcus: 1940. France falls in six weeks. Britain stands alone. Roosevelt gets creative again: fifty old American destroyers for Britain, in exchange for bases in the Western Hemisphere. Then March 1941: Lend-Lease. America will lend Britain the weapons, no cash required. Lend, in air quotes. Nobody expects the stuff back. Roosevelt sells it to Congress as self-defense — an arsenal for democracy, on America's tab.

Marcus: By the summer of 1941, Roosevelt has met Winston Churchill off the coast of Newfoundland and signed the Atlantic Charter: no territorial grabs, self-determination for all peoples, a joint statement of war aims with a country America isn't at war with. And in the Atlantic, the fiction is already gone. American destroyers are escorting British convoys. In September, a German submarine fires on the destroyer Greer. In October, a U-boat sinks the Reuben James, and about a hundred American sailors die. Shooting, being shot at, losing ships — an undeclared naval war, months before Pearl Harbor, while Congress still says America is neutral.

Marcus: Now the Pacific. Japan has been at war in China since 1937, bogged down in a quagmire. In 1940 and 1941, Japan moves into French Indochina, reaching for the resources of Southeast Asia. Roosevelt answers by freezing Japanese assets and cutting off oil. This is the vise: Japan imports the overwhelming bulk of its oil from the United States, and the freeze means no oil money moves either. Washington figures economic pressure will force Japan out of China. Instead it backs Japan into a corner with two exits: give up the empire, or seize the oil of Southeast Asia by force. And seizing it means the American fleet at Pearl Harbor sits across the whole plan.

Marcus: In October, a war government takes power in Tokyo under General Tojo. The debate inside Japan is over: the moderates who want a deal with Washington lose. On November 26th, the fleet sails, six carriers, two battleships, a screen of cruisers and destroyers, under sealed orders. Most of the sailors don't know where they're going until they're at sea.

Marcus: In November, Washington sends the Hull Note: Japan must quit China entirely, a full withdrawal. Tokyo reads it as an ultimatum — the end of talking. Washington holds one more card: American codebreakers have been reading Japan's diplomatic mail all through 1941. The intercepts say a strike is coming. The smart money in Washington says the blow lands in the Philippines, or Malaya, or the Dutch East Indies. Nobody expects Hawaii.

Marcus: That is Admiral Isoroku Yamamoto's gamble, and he knows the odds better than anyone in Tokyo. Yamamoto studied in America — Harvard, then naval attaché in Washington. He has seen the factories. He warns that Japan can run wild for six months, maybe a year, and after that America's industrial weight crushes them. So the plan: sail six carriers across the Pacific in total radio silence, hit the fleet at dawn on a Sunday, cripple it in one blow, and buy Japan a year or two to fortify its conquests. A knockout punch, thrown at the one target that could block the whole southern advance.

Marcus: Back to the hilltop. Opana Point, dawn. Two privates, a brand-new set, the largest blip anyone has ever seen. Expected bombers. Forget it. They keep the set on anyway. The blips keep coming until the island swallows the signal. Then the sky fills: the first wave, a hundred and eighty-three planes, coming in low over the water. Torpedo bombers first, then dive bombers, then fighters strafing the airfields. The Arizona takes a bomb straight into its forward magazine and blows up, more than a thousand men aboard. The Oklahoma rolls over. Ford Island, Hickam Field, Wheeler — planes burning on the ground. Then the second wave. Around twenty-four hundred Americans dead. The battleship fleet wrecked. And the carriers, the real targets, are out at sea.

Marcus: The deeper miss: the pilots wreck the battleships but leave the fuel tanks, the repair yards, and the submarine base untouched. Within months, the yards raise the sunken ships. And by June, the surviving carriers ambush Japan's fleet at Midway. Six months. Just what Yamamoto predicted.

Marcus: The next day, Roosevelt goes before Congress. Yesterday, December 7th, 1941: a date which will live in infamy. Congress declares war on Japan within the hour, with a single dissenting vote. And here's the part people get backwards: Roosevelt doesn't then declare war on Germany. Germany declares war on America, four days later, with Italy close behind. Hitler solves Roosevelt's hardest political problem for him. Why? Nobody's fully sure. Arrogance, the alliance with Japan, a belief America was already a belligerent in the Atlantic. Whatever the reason, it hands Roosevelt the two-front war without a second congressional fight.

Marcus: One thing to carry forward. For twenty years, America tried to make war illegal by statute — neutrality acts, quarantine speeches, a legal fiction stretched thinner every month, until American destroyers were fighting an undeclared war in the Atlantic while the law still said neutral. It took one Sunday morning to prove that statutes don't keep you out of the world's wars. Choices do. And the choice America woke up to on December 8th reshaped the next half century: the arsenal, the alliances, the atomic age. Isolationism died at Pearl Harbor. Everything after was the world America couldn't leave alone — and couldn't be left alone by.
