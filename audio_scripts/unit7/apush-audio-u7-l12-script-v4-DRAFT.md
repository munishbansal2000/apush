# Pronunciation: OPEC (OH-pek), stagflation (stag-FLAY-shun), Khomeini (koh-MAY-nee), Ayatollah (eye-uh-TOH-luh), Sadat (sah-DAHT), Begin (BAY-gin), Sinai (SY-ny), Tehran (teh-RAHN)

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] You're in a gas line that wraps around the block. Your plate ends in an odd number, so you can buy today. Tomorrow you can't. Last time: the trust broke here — Watergate, and the cover-up that ate a president. This time: the decade America ran out of cheap everything, and started doubting itself. Four boxes on your sheet: stagflation, the OPEC energy weapon, the malaise speech, the hostages. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eleven minutes, and you'll check all four off.

Marcus: Start with the word that broke the economics textbooks. [emphasis] Stagflation. Stagnation plus inflation. The old rule said you got one or the other: a hot economy with rising prices, or a cold one with rising unemployment. In the seventies America got both at once. Prices climbing, jobs vanishing. Economists had no playbook, because the playbook said it was impossible.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So what do the presidents actually do? There's got to be a lever somewhere.

Marcus: They grab every lever and none of them work. Nixon freezes wages and prices by decree in nineteen seventy-one, for ninety days. It holds for a while, then collapses. Ford hands out WIN buttons, Whip Inflation Now, as if a slogan could fix it — and unemployment touches ten percent on his watch. When Carter leaves office inflation is still twelve percent with unemployment at eight. Economists even invent a new number for the pain: the misery index, inflation plus unemployment. It becomes the number no president wants attached to his name.

Maya: My econ teacher says the seventies broke every graph on his classroom wall. Now I believe him.

Maya: [sheepish] Okay, but the fix looks obvious. Spend the money, create the jobs, eat the inflation. Done, right?

Marcus: [confident tone] The cures fight each other. Spend to create jobs and inflation gets worse. Cut spending to fight inflation and unemployment gets worse. That helplessness is what made stagflation brutal: every fix fed the other fire.

Maya: Your turn? No wait, that's my line. Okay, your turn, listener. You're the president. Prices climbing, jobs vanishing — and the cures fight each other. What's your move?

[10-second pause]

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] No clean move. The trap itself is the whole box. Stagflation was an economic crisis and a crisis of the experts. Nobody had the answer, and everybody could see it.

Maya: [sheepish] Carter's decade, so Carter's mess. I'm putting stagflation on him.

Marcus: [measured] Stagflation started under Nixon and outlasted Ford. Carter inherited a crisis already underway.

Marcus: Ford is the bridge between the scandal and the malaise. Ford takes over when Nixon resigns, declares the long national nightmare over, pardons Nixon a month later. He remains the only president never elected to either office. He loses a narrow one in seventy-six to Jimmy Carter: a peanut farmer from Georgia, a Washington outsider, promising the post-Watergate country one thing. He'll never lie to you.

Maya: A peanut farmer beats the sitting president. Only in the seventies.

Marcus: Box two is the hammer. October of nineteen seventy-three. Egypt and Syria attack Israel in the Yom Kippur War, America backs Israel, and the Arab members of OPEC answer with an embargo: no oil for the United States. The price of a barrel roughly quadruples in months. Gas stations run dry. And America learns, all at once, how much of its life was built on cheap imported oil. Washington's answer: a national speed limit of fifty-five, to save fuel.

Maya: [incredulous] Wait, OPEC just turned off the tap for the whole planet?

Marcus: The embargo was aimed at the United States, for backing Israel. The rest of the world just paid the higher prices. But the damage was global, because oil prices are oil prices everywhere.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And the lines from the cold open — the odd-even days — that's this embargo?

Marcus: Then comes the second shock. In seventy-nine, the Iranian Revolution cuts supply again, and the whole nightmare repeats. Odd-even rationing: your plate ends in an odd number, you buy on odd days. Thermostats go down everywhere. And the humiliation is the point: the country that put men on the moon, idling in a gas line at an oil cartel's pleasure.

Maya: My grandpa still turns off every light in the house like it's a personal war. I finally get why.

Marcus: And there's a nuclear footnote in seventy-nine. March, a reactor in Pennsylvania, Three Mile Island, partially melts down. Nobody dies, but radioactive material escapes, and new nuclear plant orders dry up for decades. So America wants energy independence and fears every path to it: oil from a cartel, nuclear from its own backyard.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Carter puts solar panels on the White House roof, right? I remember that image.

Marcus: He does. And a cardigan. He goes on television in a sweater and preaches conservation, and the country resists him for it. The country wants a hero, and he hands them a thermostat. That gap is what the next election explodes.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So the economy's on fire and the tank is empty. When does the word "malaise" show up?

Marcus: July fifteenth, nineteen seventy-nine. Gas lines, inflation strangling the country. Carter retreats to Camp David for ten days, reading letters from ordinary Americans like a doctor with a patient. Then he gives a televised speech. There's a real energy program buried in it. The part that lands is the sermon about the country's soul. He says America is suffering a crisis of confidence.

Maya: The malaise speech.

Marcus: [firm] It's the legend. [beat] He never said the word. Not once. His opponents slapped the label "malaise" on the speech, and the label stuck so hard that people remember Carter saying a word he never said.

Maya: [sheepish] I would have gotten that wrong on the exam. I was sure he said it.

Marcus: [confident tone] Most people are. Which is why it's the box. Carter's own phrase is "crisis of confidence." The speech diagnosed the country's spirit, and the country heard an insult.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And this is the same Carter who pulled off Camp David, right? That feels like a different presidency.

Marcus: Same man, same decade. A year earlier he locks Egypt's Anwar Sadat and Israel's Menachem Begin in a room at Camp David for thirteen days and walks out with the Camp David Accords: Israel gives back the Sinai Peninsula, Egypt recognizes Israel's right to exist. He gets Panama Canal treaties through the Senate, the Canal Zone going back to Panama by two thousand. He makes human rights the centerpiece of his foreign policy. Real wins. And none of them fix the feeling.

Maya: Three boxes down. One to go.

Marcus: Box four is the image the decade ends on. November fourth, nineteen seventy-nine. Iran's revolution has overthrown the Shah, and Carter lets the ailing Shah into the United States for cancer treatment. In Tehran, revolutionaries storm the American embassy. Sixty-six Americans are seized. Fifty-two of them will be held for four hundred forty-four days.

Maya: Over a year. Every night the news counts the days.

Marcus: [serious tone] Carter freezes Iranian assets, cuts off trade, tries to negotiate. In April of nineteen eighty he authorizes a rescue mission, and it dies in the Iranian desert. Sandstorms ground the helicopters, two aircraft collide, eight servicemen are killed. [sigh] Carter goes on television and takes the blame himself. The hostages become the symbol of everything that had gone wrong. America held hostage, literally and figuratively.

Maya: [sheepish] My notes say the rescue mission freed the hostages. Is that right? Stop me before I memorize it.

Marcus: [serious tone] The rescue died in the Iranian desert. The hostages came home through negotiation, freed on inauguration day as Reagan took office.

Maya: [thoughtful tone] So the verdict on the decade.

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] Exactly. Stagflation broke the economic rules. OPEC broke the illusion that American power ran on American fuel. The hostages broke the idea that nothing could touch us. The postwar boom's confidence, the feeling that tomorrow would always be richer than today, cracked here. And nineteen eighty is coming.

Maya: Four boxes, let's land them. One: stagflation.

Marcus: Stagnation plus inflation, the impossible combo. The oil shocks broke the old economic rules, and the cures fought each other.

Maya: Two: the OPEC energy weapon.

Marcus: The seventy-three embargo, aimed at the US over Israel, with prices roughly quadrupling — and a fifty-five-mile-an-hour speed limit to save fuel. Then the seventy-nine shock: gas lines, odd-even days.

Maya: Three: the malaise speech, July seventy-nine. His opponents' label stuck to his "crisis of confidence" address.

Marcus: His opponents named it for him, and the label won.

Maya: Four: the hostages. Fifty-two Americans, four hundred forty-four days, home on inauguration day. Checking that one.

Marcus: All four landed.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it.

[10-second pause]

Maya: One. A textbook prints a nineteen seventy-four photo: cars wrapped around a gas station, a hand-painted sign that says NO GAS. An SAQ asks what this photo proves about the seventies economy. What's your claim?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The embargo made oil a weapon. The mechanics back it up: the Arab OPEC members cut off the United States after the Yom Kippur War, and the price of a barrel roughly quadrupled. And it matters because cheap energy had underwritten the whole postwar boom. When the tap closed, the confidence cracked with it.

[10-second pause]

Maya: Two. Why did stagflation leave presidents with no good moves?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Because the cures fought each other. Spend to cut unemployment, and inflation gets worse. Cut spending to fight inflation, and jobs vanish. No playbook, no clean move: the impossible combo.

[10-second pause]

Maya: Three. Carter's own phrase was "crisis of confidence." So why did the word "malaise" win?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Because it named the feeling his speech described — a country doubting itself. The opponents' word beat the president's phrase, and the label became the history.

[10-second pause]

Maya: One more, fast. Four hundred forty-four days in captivity, and they walk free on inauguration day. Why does the exam care about that timing?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Because the timing fuses the crisis to the transfer of power. The rescue failed. Negotiation freed them on inauguration day, as the new president took the oath. The decade closed on that image.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Check your boxes. Stagflation, the energy weapon, the malaise speech, the hostages. Four boxes, one unraveling decade. The cram is next: the whole unit, containment to the hostages, one sitting.

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] The easy years ran out of road.

Maya: [conversational] Right here, in the seventies.
