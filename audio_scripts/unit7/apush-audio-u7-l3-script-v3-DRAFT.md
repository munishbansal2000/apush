# APUSH Unit 7, Lesson 3: The Korean War
# Script v3 DRAFT — Maya + Marcus interview format
# Read note: [10-second pause] marks are production silence for the prediction beat and the self-test; they never go to the voice. Maya tracks four boxes on her episode sheet: the invasion and the UN's answer, the war's phases, the general versus the president, and the verdict. She checks each off as the episode earns it. The em dash in the closing tagline is a held breath — do not rush it. Strip this header and the read note before TTS. Intended duration ~12 min at ≤180 WPM (spoken words plus all pause silence).
# Pronunciation: Pusan (poo-SAHN), Inchon (in-CHON), Chosin (choh-SIN), Yalu (yah-LOO), Syngman Rhee (SING-man REE), Kim Il-sung (kim il-SUNG), Panmunjom (pahn-moon-JUHM), Chiang Kai-shek (CHANG ky-SHEK)

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: containment got its instruments, the doctrine, the dollars, the alliance, the memo that militarized the whole thing. This time: containment goes to war. Korea: the line gets tested with real guns. Four boxes on your sheet: the invasion and the UN's answer, the war's phases, the general versus the president, and the verdict. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Twelve minutes, and you'll check all four off.

Marcus: Start with the line. After World War Two, America and the Soviet Union split Korea at the thirty-eighth parallel, and the split was supposed to be temporary. It wasn't. By 1948 there were two Koreas: Kim Il-sung's communist North, backed by Moscow, and Syngman Rhee's South, backed by Washington. Each one claimed the whole peninsula.

Maya: [deadpan] Two governments, one country, both saying "all of it is mine." What could go wrong?

Marcus: [dramatic] Dawn, June twenty-fifth, nineteen fifty. Artillery opens along the parallel, and about ninety thousand North Korean soldiers pour across it, tanks in the lead. The South Korean army folds in days. Seoul, the capital, falls within the week.

Maya: [incredulous] Within the week? The whole capital?

Marcus: [serious tone] The invasion nearly won the war before America could get there.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So what does Truman do? Congress, a declaration?

Marcus: [firm] Truman moves without asking Congress. He sends planes and ships first, then ground troops, and calls the whole thing a police action. The label was doing work: this is enforcement.

Maya: And Congress just let him?

Marcus: He went to the United Nations instead. The break that made the exam point: Moscow was boycotting the Security Council over China's seat, so with the Soviet chair empty, nobody could veto. The UN authorized the defense of South Korea.

Maya: [surprised] So the Soviets handed America the legal cover by not showing up.

Marcus: [confident tone] [emphasis]The empty chair did the authorizing. Two exam traps here. One: an MCQ will hand you "the Soviets vetoed the UN resolution on Korea." They couldn't. They weren't in the room. Two: essays writing that "the UN sent troops." The UN authorized. America supplied the bulk of the troops.

Maya: [speaking slowly] June twenty-fifth, nineteen fifty. Boycott, no veto, UN flag. Got it.

Marcus: The first Americans in were Task Force Smith, about five hundred men from occupation duty in Japan. On July fifth, outside Osan, they met North Korean tanks with weapons that couldn't stop tanks. Overrun in hours. By August the Americans and South Koreans were squeezed into the Pusan perimeter, a pocket around the port. If it fell, Korea fell.

Maya: Your turn. You're Truman, late June. Your planes and ships are already moving, Congress never voted, and every option on your desk either looks weak or risks a wider war. Do you go back and ask Congress for a declaration?

[10-second pause]

Marcus: You don't, because a declaration debate costs the one thing you don't have: time. It also names Korea a full war, the thing you're trying to avoid. The UN vote gave you cover, the troops were moving. Speed was the strategy.

Maya: Box one is checked. The invasion and the UN's answer.

Marcus: September. The North has stretched its supply lines south to Pusan, and its rear is exposed at Inchon, a port on the west coast. Douglas MacArthur sees the gamble nobody else sees: land behind them, cut the army in half, take Seoul back.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] The North's whole rear exposed at a port sounds like a free landing. What's the catch?

Marcus: Inchon has some of the most extreme tides on earth. At low tide the harbor is a mudflat, and the whole landing has to squeeze into a few hours of high water. Seawalls to climb, and a channel so narrow one sunk ship could block the fleet. Every other general in the room said no.

Maya: And MacArthur?

Marcus: He pushed it through. September fifteenth, nineteen fifty, the landing goes in, and it works. Within two weeks the Marines retake Seoul, and the North Korean army in the south disintegrates, cut off from its supplies and destroyed. In one month the war reverses completely.

Marcus: October. MacArthur pushes past the parallel, driving north deep into North Korea, toward the Yalu, the river that marks the Chinese border. The troops are told they'll be home by Christmas. But China has been warning, loudly, that it will not tolerate an American army on its border.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And Washington just let him march to China's border?

Marcus: Truman authorized the crossing. The goal shifted from repelling the invasion to unifying Korea, and that shift is what the rubric hunts: the new goal is what brought China in. MacArthur's intelligence said the Chinese wouldn't come. It was wrong. Beijing kept up the fiction: it sent "volunteers" across the Yalu, and the fiction mattered. It kept the war from becoming officially China versus America.

Maya: [incredulous] "Volunteers." Hundreds of thousands of volunteers.

Marcus: [serious tone] The estimates run from around a hundred and fifty thousand past a million. In late November the trap springs. The First Marine Division, surrounded at the Chosin Reservoir in cold like minus thirty, fights its way to the sea, carrying its wounded.

Maya: I wear a coat when it's sixty degrees. Minus thirty, with people shooting at you? I would have simply perished.

Marcus: Many did. By the spring of nineteen fifty-one the front had frozen near the thirty-eighth parallel, and the map would barely move again for two years.

Maya: So all that motion, and the line ends up where it began.

Marcus: The motion's done, but the dying isn't. Peace talks open in the summer of nineteen fifty-one at Kaesong. They'd move to Panmunjom that fall, and they drag on for two years while the fighting grinds on. The sticking point is prisoners: thousands of North Korean and Chinese captives refuse to go home, and the UN won't force them back.

Maya: They'd rather stay prisoners?

Marcus: Than return to the regimes they'd fought for. Men keep dying over hills that change hands and change back while diplomats argue about who goes home. [beat] The armistice finally comes July twenty-seventh, nineteen fifty-three, months into Eisenhower's presidency. He'd campaigned on a famous pledge: "I shall go to Korea." The line ends up almost exactly where it started.

Maya: And the general at the center of all that is the next box.

Marcus: Humiliated by the Chinese offensive, MacArthur starts publicly demanding a bigger war: blockade China's coast, bomb the bases across the border in Manchuria, bring Chiang Kai-shek's Nationalists in from Taiwan.

Maya: [incredulous] In public? While he's still the commander?

Marcus: [firm] Letters to congressmen, statements to the press, attacking the president's policy by name. Truman had told him, through the Joint Chiefs, to stop freelancing policy in public. He did it again. On April eleventh, nineteen fifty-one, Truman fired the most famous general in America, in the middle of a war.

Maya: [surprised] He fired MacArthur? THE MacArthur?

Marcus: [dramatic] The country exploded: ticker-tape parades, then a joint session of Congress where he closed with a line borrowed from an old West Point ballad, "Old soldiers never die, they just fade away." To a Senate committee: in war, there is no substitute for victory. Much of the country decided Truman had fired a hero for trying to win.

Maya: [sheepish] Okay, but hadn't he? MacArthur wanted to win, and Truman fired him.

Marcus: [stern] That's the confusion, and it's exactly backwards. Truman fired MacArthur because MacArthur wanted a different war than the one America had chosen. Truman's war was limited: stop the invasion and restore the line without starting a wider war. MacArthur wanted total victory, whatever the cost, and he said so in public, against orders.

Maya: So the firing wasn't about strategy at all. It was about who gets to choose the strategy.

Marcus: [confident tone] The president does.
Maya: [confident tone] The president does.

Marcus: The settled principle, and the exam answer: in America, civilians control the military. The president decides what the war is for. If an SAQ asks about the firing, the principle is the answer they're hunting.

Maya: Full disclosure before the verdict: until this episode, everything I knew about Korea came from M*A*S*H reruns at my grandma's house.

Marcus: The field-hospital comedy. Set in Korea.

Maya: Comfort TV with field hospitals. Love that for her.

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] The verdict, then: Korea was containment's first hot test, and the doctrine passed. The invasion was stopped. South Korea stayed non-communist. A line was drawn and held by force.

Maya: At what price?

Marcus: [serious tone] Around forty thousand American dead. The Korean and Chinese dead ran far higher, well over a million by most reckonings. [sigh] Korea stayed divided. It still is, and American troops are still there.

Maya: And the veterans came home to what? Parades?

Marcus: [serious tone] No parades, no national moment. The war fell between the big one and Vietnam: too small to be glorious, too inconclusive to celebrate. They called it the Forgotten War. The memorial didn't go up until nineteen ninety-five.

Maya: So what changed? If the map barely moved, what moved?

Marcus: The American way of war. The big wars ended in total victory: unconditional surrender, ticker-tape parades. Korea broke the pattern. Historians call the replacement [emphasis]the limited war: fight to a line, negotiate from there, and stay.

Maya: And Vietnam is the sequel.

Marcus: The first draft.

Maya: [sheepish] Korea looks like a loss. The map barely moved.

Marcus: [firm] Map's the wrong scorecard. A limited war has a limited goal, and Korea met it: the invasion stopped and the South stayed free.

Maya: Four boxes, let's land them. One: the invasion and the UN's answer. June twenty-fifth, nineteen fifty: about ninety thousand North Koreans cross the parallel, Seoul falls within the week. Truman skips Congress, calls it a police action, and with Moscow boycotting, the UN authorizes the defense. America supplies the bulk of the troops.

Maya: Two: the phases. Inchon, September fifteenth: the gamble through the tides, Seoul retaken within two weeks. The drive north, "home by Christmas," then China's "volunteers," the Chosin retreat, the front freezing near the thirty-eighth. Two years of talks at Panmunjom over prisoners who won't go home. Armistice, July twenty-seventh, fifty-three.

Maya: Three: the general versus the president. MacArthur keeps demanding a bigger war in public, against orders. Truman fires him in April of fifty-one.

Marcus: April eleventh.

Maya: April eleventh, fifty-one. See, I had it. The parades and the speech were color; what settled is the principle: civilians control the military.

Maya: Four: the verdict. The invasion stopped, the South stayed free: containment passed its first hot test. Around forty thousand Americans dead, Korea still divided, and the limited war was born here. Checking all four.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One, stimulus. MacArthur told a Senate committee: "In war, there is no substitute for victory." The line sounds like a strategy. Why does it prove nothing about what Truman's war was actually for?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Because it's about how MacArthur thought wars should be fought, a goal Truman never chose. Truman's goal was limited: stop the invasion, restore the line, avoid a wider war. "No substitute for victory" argues with a goal Truman never chose.

Maya: Two. The Soviets boycotted the Security Council over China's seat. Why did their absence matter more than their presence?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Because a Soviet delegate in the room could have vetoed the resolution. The boycott emptied the chair that held the veto, so the vote went through.

Maya: Three. The war ended where it started. Did containment work in Korea?

[10-second pause]

Maya: By containment's own logic, yes. The invasion was stopped, South Korea stayed non-communist. The confusion is grading a limited war on a total-war report card.

Maya: One more, fast. What did the firing settle?

[10-second pause]

Maya: That civilians control the military. The president sets the war's goal; the generals fight the war they're given.

Maya: Check your four boxes. Next time: the war comes home. A senator from Wisconsin starts naming names.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] They stopped the invasion, fired the legend —
Marcus: [professional broadcast tone] and the line is still there.

## Beyond Tier 1–2 (disclosed)
- "Police action" framing: Truman's label for the undeclared war (the exam point per the lesson brief) — standard history, not named in the Tier-1 texts; taught flat, disclosed here.
- Battle-level detail not in the Tier-1 texts — Task Force Smith (~500 men, Osan, July 5), Pusan perimeter, Inchon tides/mudflats/seawalls (Sept 15, 1950), Seoul retaken within two weeks, "home by Christmas," Chosin Reservoir retreat, armistice talks opened at Kaesong (July 1951) and moved to Panmunjom that fall, POW repatriation as the talks' sticking point, Eisenhower's "I shall go to Korea," the April 11 firing date, the December 1950 directive requiring MacArthur to clear public statements, Rhee/Kim Il-sung names, US troops still in Korea, the 1995 memorial; further standard-history detail — June 25 invasion date, ~90,000 North Korean invaders, Seoul's fall within the week, China's pre-intervention warnings, MacArthur's intelligence failure on Chinese entry, the generals' opposition to Inchon, blockade-of-China and Manchuria-bombing specifics, the Martin letter to congressmen, ticker-tape parades, both Koreas claiming the whole peninsula, the M*A*S*H aside — cross-checked against general sources (DOD 70th-anniversary piece, HISTORY, NARA/Korean War Memorial materials); hedged in dialogue where the sources hedge.
- "Limited war" as Korea's invention: interpretive historiographical framing, hedged in dialogue as historians' characterization ("Historians call the replacement the limited war"); not in the Tier-1 texts.
- "Old soldiers never die, they just fade away": MacArthur quoting an old West Point ballad (U.S. Capitol Visitor Center / Library of Congress) — taught as borrowed, never as his coinage (registry F-U7-011).
- China's "volunteers" fiction: standard history, taught as the standard framing.
