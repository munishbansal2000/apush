# APUSH Unit 7, Lesson 5: Suburbia — The Postwar Boom
# Script v3 DRAFT — Interview (Maya + Marcus)

# Pronunciation: Levittown (LEV-it-town), Kerouac (keh-roo-ACK), Galbraith (gal-BRAYTH), Reynolds, Friedan (free-DAN), Sunbelt

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] A lease in Levittown, New York, nineteen forty-seven: a brand-new house for about eight thousand dollars, with a clause in the fine print saying a Black family could never live there. Last time: McCarthyism, the scare that ate its own. This time: the machine that built modern America. The suburbs, the boom, the backyard, and the fine print nobody advertised. Four boxes: the GI Bill, Levittown, the Sunbelt tilt, and the fine print. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. About eleven minutes, and you'll land all four.

Marcus: Start with the money, because everything else in this episode flows out of it. Nineteen forty-four, while the war is still on, Congress passes the Servicemen's Readjustment Act. The GI Bill. Most students hear the name and picture the mortgage program. It was bigger than that.

Maya: So what did it actually give a returning veteran?

Marcus: College money, low-interest home loans with little or nothing down, and business loans. The mortgage piece is the one everyone remembers, because it built the suburbs.

Maya: And the college part actually worked? Veterans just flooded campuses?

Marcus: They did. And the housing money hit even harder. In nineteen forty, about four in ten American families owned their home. By nineteen sixty, about six in ten. That jump is the GI Bill plus a wartime generation sitting on savings and desperate for its own front door.

Maya: But if nobody's building, where do all these buyers go?

Marcus: Into a shortage. At the end of the war, demand for housing far outstripped supply. In some cities, two families were living in an apartment built for one. The buyers are ready. The houses aren't.

Maya: [surprised] Four in ten to six in ten in twenty years — and it starts with a housing shortage?

Marcus: Roughly, and right on cue, the babies arrive. The baby boom: nineteen forty-six to the mid-sixties, something like seventy-six million births. In nineteen fifty-seven alone, more than four million.

Maya: So is the boom the cause of the suburbs, or the effect?

Marcus: Both, and that pairing is the engine. The prosperity made bigger families possible, and the babies made bigger houses necessary. Cheap mortgages, wartime savings, and a generation of kids who all need bedrooms.

Marcus: Your turn. You've got millions of buyers holding cheap government-backed loans, and not nearly enough houses. What fixes it? Think.

[10-second pause]

Maya: [conversational] Somebody figures out how to build houses the way Ford built cars.

Marcus: Exactly like Ford built cars. One man, one potato field, and the assembly-line playbook.

Marcus: William Levitt, Long Island, nineteen forty-seven. He aims Ford's assembly line at a potato field: twenty-six steps, crews moving lot to lot, each crew doing one job. Between forty-seven and fifty-one, more than seventeen thousand houses go up, roughly nine a day. Nearly identical, but well made and reasonably priced.

Maya: [surprised] Nine a day? My grandpa took three summers to build a garage.

Marcus: Every grandpa had feelings about this. The houses are small, two bedrooms, no basement, but they're yours — roughly eight thousand dollars, nothing down. And once the houses go up, the spending follows: furniture, the latest appliances. The boom feeds itself.

Maya: So Levitt invented the suburb?

Marcus: [firm] The suburb, no. The suburb at assembly-line speed, yes. Suburbs go back to the commuter railroads of the late eighteen hundreds. Levittown aimed the assembly line at the same idea.

Maya: Then the house, the TV, the highway. It's all one package?

Marcus: It only works as a package: the loan, the house, the commute. Pull one out and the whole thing stalls.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Hold on. A television in nineteen forty-seven? Wasn't that fancy?

Marcus: The fanciest thing in the house, and quietly the most important appliance. By the end of the fifties, nearly nine in ten American homes owned a television set. A handful of networks beams the same shows into millions of identical living rooms, and those shows keep saying: this is what normal looks like. Dad at work, mom in the kitchen.

Maya: [playful] Wait, wait. "Little boxes on the hillside"? Is that song about Levittown? My mom hums it.

Marcus: Malvina Reynolds, nineteen sixty-two. She wrote "Little Boxes" and took aim at the whole suburb for looking the same. The conformity was real enough to get its own protest song.

Maya: Wait. "Under God" in the pledge. That wasn't always there, right? I said it every morning in elementary school.

Marcus: Right. Congress adds it in nineteen fifty-four, and two years later makes "In God We Trust" the official motto. Same package deal: the Soviets are godless communists, so America gets officially godly.

Marcus: And the thing that makes the whole commute possible: the Federal-Aid Highway Act, nineteen fifty-six. Forty-one thousand miles of interstate, sold to the country as Cold War defense, moving troops and equipment, fast. Used as the suburban commute.

Maya: [incredulous] So the highways were sold as military and used as driveways?

Marcus: Roughly.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And nobody pushed back on the whole package? The whole country just agreed this was the dream?

Marcus: Plenty bought it. But the discontents got famous too. Kerouac's Beats made wandering sound holier than a mortgage, and Galbraith called America an "affluent society." His argument: the country had money for cars in every driveway but not for the schools.

Maya: [sheepish] Quick gut-check: the GI Bill was the mortgage program. The college money came from a different law, right?

Marcus: [firm] All one bill: college money, housing loans, and business loans. The mortgage is the famous piece, because it built the suburbs.

Maya: [conversational] Two boxes down. The engine and the machine.

Marcus: Box three, and it's the map itself moving: the Sunbelt. The South and the West, with California, Florida, and Texas out front. The fastest-growing part of the country.

Maya: Because of the weather? Everyone just wanted sunshine?

Marcus: [firm] The story people tell is sunshine, and it's the wrong one. What actually moved people was technology and money: affordable air conditioning finally made the hot states livable year-round, the new interstates made the move easy, and federal spending kept flowing into the region's growing economy. The exam trap is blaming the weather. The weather was always there. The jobs and the cooling are what changed.

Maya: [sheepish] So my whole "retirees chasing sunshine" theory is wrong?

Marcus: Sunshine helped. But the jobs moved first, and the people followed the jobs. Then the political map follows the people — House seats and electoral votes go where the population goes. The country's center of gravity tilts south and west, and it hasn't tilted back since.

Marcus: [serious tone] Now the fine print. [beat] Remember that lease from the cold open? That clause came from a whole system. Banks drew maps with red lines around Black neighborhoods and called them "investment hazards." That's where "redlining" comes from, literally. The FHA refused to insure mortgages there, which meant no loans, no repairs, slow decay.

Maya: And the cities they left behind?

Marcus: [serious tone] Watched their tax money leave with them. As middle-class families moved to the suburbs, cities lost the taxes that paid for policing and schools. Services got cut, and by the sixties, whole sections of cities had become slums.

Maya: And Levitt's suburbs? Same deal?

Marcus: [serious tone] Same deal, by the book. Levitt followed the FHA's rulebook, and the rulebook said no homes sold to Black families. Restrictive covenants in the leases. Black veterans came home from the same war and got a fraction of the same loans.

Maya: [serious tone] Same war, same uniform, different mortgage.

Marcus: [serious tone] The Premium review book puts it plainly: the program was "less successful in extending benefits to African-American veterans." The suburbs were the prosperity, and the segregation was built into the fine print.

Maya: Wait. The women. The factories needed them during the war. They all just... went home?

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] And the women got their own version. The wartime factories needed them; the peacetime suburbs wanted them home. The ideal on every TV screen was the working father and the stay-at-home mother. A decade later, Betty Friedan gives that frustration a name, The Feminine Mystique, nineteen sixty-three, but that's a later unit's story. Redlining came straight from bank maps and federal policy. Banks drew the lines, and the federal government backed the play.

Maya: [conversational] The suburbs got their houses. But the people the fine print locked out? They didn't stay quiet.

Maya: Four boxes, let's land them. One: the GI Bill. Nineteen forty-four. College money and low-interest home loans for veterans, and homeownership jumps from about four in ten to about six in ten.

Marcus: On the sheet. And the baby boom fills the new bedrooms. Forty-six to the mid-sixties, something like seventy-six million births.

Maya: Checking that one. Two: Levittown. Assembly-line houses, twenty-six steps, roughly nine a day, seventeen thousand by fifty-one. About eight thousand dollars, no deposit. Plus the interstates: fifty-six, forty-one thousand miles, sold as defense, used as commute.

Marcus: On the sheet.

Maya: Three: the Sunbelt tilt. Air conditioning, interstates, and federal money pulling people south and west, and the power map follows. Four: the fine print. Redlining. Wait, did the FHA draw the red lines?

Marcus: [firm] The banks drew the maps; the FHA refused to insure the redlined neighborhoods. Same result.

Maya: [catching] Right. Banks drew, FHA wouldn't insure. And Levitt's covenants kept Black families out, Black veterans got a fraction of the loans, and women got pushed back toward the kitchen. Checking that one too.

Marcus: All four, fine print included.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it.

Maya: One: the GI Bill to Levittown. What's the chain, in one sentence?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The GI Bill's cheap loans created millions of buyers, and Levitt's assembly line built the houses they bought. Demand on one side, supply on the other. Without Levitt, the loans just buy a housing shortage.

Maya: Two: why did the Sunbelt grow after World War Two?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Jobs first, weather second. Federal spending and new industry pulled workers south and west, affordable air conditioning made the heat livable, and the interstates made the move easy. People follow work they can reach, and the sunshine sweetened the move.

Maya: Three, a source. A Levittown lease has a clause limiting who may live in the house. Why would an exam pair that source with a question about postwar prosperity?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Because the lease shows the prosperity had a color line. The same suburbs the GI Bill built were closed to Black families by covenant, so the document proves the boom and its limits at the same time.

Maya: One more, fast. Name the federal agency that refused to insure mortgages in redlined neighborhoods, and the nineteen sixty-three book that named women's frustration with the suburban ideal.

[10-second pause]

Maya: The FHA. And Betty Friedan's The Feminine Mystique.

Marcus: [professional broadcast tone] The boom built the houses —

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] and the fine print decided who got the keys.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Check your boxes. Next time: the people who refused to be left out. Montgomery, Birmingham, and Selma.
