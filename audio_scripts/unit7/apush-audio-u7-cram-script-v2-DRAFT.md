# CED: Period 8, 1945–1980.
# Pronunciation: Kennan (KEN-nun); Khrushchev (KROOSH-chef); Diem (dee-YEM); Saigon (sy-GON); Tet (tet);
# Ruckelshaus (RUCK-elz-house); Jaworski (ja-VOR-skee); Sadat (sa-DOT); Begin (BAY-gin); Khomeini (koh-MAY-nee); Pettus (PET-us).

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: December 7th, 1941, and America never came home again. This time: no new material. Twelve lessons, eleven questions, 1945 to 1980, in about eleven minutes. The Cold War, abroad and at home. I'm firing the questions. You answer out loud before Marcus does. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now.

Maya: [energetic] One. February 1946: a Moscow cable, eight thousand words. Walk the chain from that cable to American troops in Europe.

[2-second pause]

Marcus: [measured] Kennan's Long Telegram, from Moscow: Soviet power only understands counterpressure. Washington calls it containment. Truman Doctrine, March twelfth, 1947: America aids nations resisting subjugation, starting with Greece and Turkey. The Marshall Plan, 1948: billions to rebuild Western Europe before communism can capitalize. NATO, 1949: a permanent peacetime alliance, an attack on one counting as an attack on all.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And the first time containment gets tested, Berlin? The blockade ran fifteen months, right?

Marcus: [firm] Not quite. One review book's summary says fifteen months, but that's the airlift's number, not the blockade's. The blockade ran June forty-eight to May forty-nine, about eleven months; the airlift flew on about fifteen months, outlasting the blockade. Teach the two durations separately, or the exam will separate them for you.

Maya: [energetic] Two. June 25th, 1950: North Korea crosses the 38th parallel. Truman sends the military without a war vote. What does he call it, and why?

[2-second pause]

Marcus: [confident tone] A police action. The label does the work: enforcement, not war, so no declaration, and the war stays limited. The UN authorizes the defense while Moscow boycotts; America supplies the troops. Inchon flips the map; UN forces drive north, China crosses the Yalu, and the war freezes at the parallel.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And MacArthur gets fired for losing?

Marcus: [firm] For insubordination, April 1951: he wanted to widen the war, Truman wanted it limited. The exam point is civilian control. Armistice, July 1953, and the book line: a formal treaty ending the war was never signed. The armistice paused it; it didn't end it.

Maya: [energetic] Three. February 9th, 1950, Wheeling, West Virginia: McCarthy says the State Department is full of communists. What does he produce?

[2-second pause]

Marcus: [serious tone] Nothing. The number shifts with the telling, two-oh-five, eighty-one, fifty-seven, and no list ever surfaces. The machinery was already running. Truman's loyalty program, 1947. HUAC's Hollywood hearings, 1947. The Hollywood Ten go to prison for contempt of Congress, contempt, not spying. Blacklists spread through whole industries.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And the Rosenbergs, their trial was in fifty-two?

Marcus: [firm] That's not right: fifty-one. 5 Steps prints fifty-two; the correction is on the record. Convicted March 1951. Then Army-McCarthy, 1954, and the Senate censures him that December. The scare eats its own.

Maya: [energetic] Four. 1944: the GI Bill. Everyone remembers the mortgages. What's the fuller list?

[2-second pause]

Marcus: [measured] College money, home loans with little or nothing down, and business loans: not just mortgages. Veterans carry the loans to Levittown: seventeen thousand homes, 1947 to 1951, eight thousand each, nothing down, built assembly-line. The baby boom fills the new schools. The date trap: one review book's timeline misprints fifty-four for the Interstate Highway Act. Its own prose says fifty-six; Congress passed it: forty-one thousand miles, June 1956.

Maya: [sheepish] I always want to say the suburbs just happened. Cheap land, nice weather.

Marcus: [firm] The fine print says otherwise. Banks drew red lines around Black neighborhoods and labeled them investment hazards; the FHA refused to insure mortgages inside them. Federal money built the suburbs, and fenced them too.
Maya: [energetic] Five. Recite the chain. Nineteen fifty-four to nineteen sixty-five, no skipping.

[2-second pause]

Marcus: [driving] Brown, fifty-four: unanimous, separate is inherently unequal. The rule dies; classrooms take another decade. Montgomery, fifty-five to fifty-six: Rosa Parks, a trained NAACP organizer, and a year-long boycott. Little Rock, fifty-seven: Faubus blocks nine students with the Guard; Eisenhower nationalizes the Guard and sends in federal troops. Greensboro, 1960: sit-ins, then SNCC.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Birmingham's the one with the TV cameras, right?

Marcus: [driving] Birmingham, sixty-three: Bull Connor's hoses and dogs on national TV, King's Letter from the Birmingham Jail. The March on Washington, August sixty-three: more than two hundred thousand. Then the laws: Civil Rights Act, sixty-four, public places and jobs; Voting Rights Act, sixty-five, the ballot, after Bloody Sunday at Selma.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So sixty-four is jobs and lunch counters, sixty-five is voting. Wait, the March was sixty-three and Selma sixty-five? I always flip those.

Marcus: [confident tone] You had it right. Selma's Bloody Sunday, March sixty-five, is the trigger for the VRA, not a victory lap after it. Two fronts, two laws. Never swap them.

Maya: [energetic] Six. April 1961: the Bay of Pigs collapses; a year and a half later, thirteen days that nearly end the world. Your turn: air strikes or a naval blockade. Eight seconds, which one is the act of war?

[8-second pause]

Marcus: [firm] The blockade: under international rules it's an act of war. So Kennedy picks the ships and calls it a quarantine. Same squeeze, no war declared. U-2 photos had caught Soviet missile sites going up ninety miles from Florida; for thirteen days in October 1962, the fate of the world rode on letters: Kennedy and Khrushchev traded notes while the navies held their positions.

Maya: [sheepish] October sixty-two? I always file it in sixty-three.

Marcus: [firm] Mix-up. Sixty-two, never sixty-three: one review book's chapter eleven misprints sixty-three; its own chapter ten says sixty-two. Kennedy didn't just stare Khrushchev down. The public saw resolve; the archives show a trade. Missiles out of Cuba, a no-invasion pledge, and secretly the Jupiters out of Turkey. Toughness plus a deal, never toughness alone.

Maya: [energetic] Seven. Nineteen sixty-four: Johnson wins big, then spends 1965. What's on the list, and which pair does every student flip?

[2-second pause]

Marcus: [measured] The War on Poverty, Medicare and Medicaid, the Elementary and Secondary Education Act, Hart-Celler, the Voting Rights Act: all sixty-five. The flipped pair: Medicare covers Americans sixty-five and older; Medicaid covers the poor. Hart-Celler: it kills the national-origins quotas but replaces them with caps. Quotas gone, limits in, never an open door.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Wait, Medicare's the old-people one? I had it backwards.

Marcus: [confident tone] Had. Sixty-five and up for Medicare; Medicaid for low income. The exam's verdict: it cut the poverty rate by about forty percent, but it didn't end poverty. Cut, not ended.

Maya: [energetic] Eight. August 1964: the Gulf of Tonkin. Congress hands Johnson a blank check, on what evidence?

[2-second pause]

Marcus: [serious tone] Thin evidence. The first clash was real but minor; the second attack, the one Johnson took to Congress, almost certainly never happened. The resolution passes anyway. Tet, January 1968: the Communists hit everywhere at once. The reversal students miss: Tet was a military win and a political earthquake. Forty thousand North Vietnamese and Vietcong dead against sixteen hundred Americans. The battlefield said winning; the television said otherwise.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So the public's war and the generals' war were two different wars?

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] Say it that way. Cronkite calls it mired in stalemate that February; Johnson drops out. Then Vietnamization: the fighting goes to the South Vietnamese as American troops draw down. Paris, January 1973; Saigon falls in seventy-five. The exit failed its goal.

Maya: [energetic] Nine. 1962: a book about birds starts a rebellion. The chain, go.

[2-second pause]

Marcus: [measured] Silent Spring, 1962: Rachel Carson's pesticide warning. Earth Day, April twenty-second, 1970: about twenty million Americans, one of the largest single-day protests in the country's history. Washington: the EPA in 1970, the Clean Air Act the same year, the Clean Water Act in seventy-two, the Endangered Species Act in seventy-three.

Maya: [playful] Wait, Nixon created the EPA? The Watergate Nixon?

Marcus: [conversational] The same Nixon: the environment was the popular cause no president could leave to the other side. Even the rollback couldn't kill the laws; under Reagan the EPA got starved and squeezed, and the statutes survived.

Maya: [energetic] Ten. June seventeenth, seventy-two: five burglars in the Watergate. The chain to a resignation, go.

[2-second pause]

Marcus: [dramatic] June seventeenth. One review book says the sixteenth evening, but the arrests came after two a.m. Then the cover-up: the Saturday Night Massacre, October 1973, Richardson and Ruckelshaus resign rather than fire the prosecutor. United States v. Nixon, eight to nothing: executive privilege doesn't cover criminal evidence. The smoking-gun tape, June twenty-third, seventy-two, lands August fifth. Resignation announced August eighth, effective the ninth, the first and only resignation of an American president.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Is the lesson that the system worked?

Marcus: [confident tone] The system held: Congress investigated and the Court ruled unanimously. Nixon resigned. Trust collapsed, but the machinery didn't.

Maya: [energetic] Eleven. 1973: the economy breaks impossibly. Name it.

[2-second pause]

Marcus: [serious tone] Stagflation: stagnation plus inflation, a pair that wasn't supposed to coexist. Nixon's 1971 wage-price freeze fails; the disease outlasts Ford. Then OPEC's Arab members embargo oil to America in October 1973 after the Yom Kippur War. The price roughly quadruples: gas lines, a fifty-five-mile-an-hour limit. A second shock hits in seventy-nine with the Iranian Revolution — and odd-even rationing days.

Maya: [sheepish] The malaise speech? Carter, seventy-nine.

Marcus: [firm] He never said malaise. July fifteenth, seventy-nine: Carter gives a televised address about a crisis of confidence. His opponents hung the word on him and it stuck. He leaves with inflation at twelve percent, unemployment at eight; the misery index, inflation plus unemployment, becomes the decade's signature number.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Any bright spot in the seventies?

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] Camp David, 1978: thirteen days, Sadat and Begin, the Sinai for peace. Then November 1979: the Tehran embassy seized, sixty-six Americans taken, fifty-two held the full four hundred forty-four days. The April 1980 rescue fails in the desert; the hostages walk free on January twentieth, 1981, as Reagan takes the oath.

Maya: [conversational] Eleven beats. The sentence underneath: containment was the foreign policy; the Cold War was the domestic one.

Marcus: [building] Test it. Containment built the alliances and fought the hot wars: Korea, Vietnam. But the fight remade home too: the blacklists, the suburbs, the movement. America contained communism abroad, and the containing remade America.

Maya: [confident tone] Containment was the foreign policy. The Cold War was the domestic one. Say it in your sleep.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. First: a U-2 photo, long white cylinders on launch pads, ninety miles from Florida, October 1962. What's the point of this source?

[18-second pause]

Maya: The photos are the proof: Soviet missiles going up in Cuba. Which is why Kennedy had to answer, and why the crisis is the closest the Cold War came to a hot war.

Maya: Second: a student writes that the Civil Rights Act of 1964 finished the movement's work. What's wrong?

[15-second pause]

Maya: Sixty-four finished the public-places and jobs half; the ballot half still stood. Literacy tests, registrars, Bloody Sunday: the movement needed a second law for the second front. The Voting Rights Act, sixty-five.

Maya: Last one, fast: in one sentence, why did Truman send the army to Korea but call it a police action?

[8-second pause]

Maya: Because he wanted to fight without asking Congress for a war, and to keep the war limited: no declaration, no wider war with China and the Soviets. The label was the limit.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Twelve lessons, one cram, nothing dropped. The Cold War held the line, and the line remade America. Sleep. Water. Go get the five.
