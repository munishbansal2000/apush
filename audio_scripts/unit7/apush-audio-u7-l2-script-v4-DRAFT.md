# APUSH Unit 7, Lesson 2: Containment Doctrines
# Script v4 DRAFT — Maya + Marcus interview format
# Read note: Maya tracks three boxes on her episode sheet: Kennan's containment idea, the Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan, NATO and the military turn. She checks each off as the episode earns it. [10-second pause] marks are production silence for prediction beats, self-test questions, and the fast bonus — they never go to the voice. The em dash in the closing tagline is a held breath — do not rush it. Strip this header and the read note before TTS.
# Pronunciation: Kennan (KEH-nun), NATO (NAY-toh), Ismay (IZ-may), Nitze (NIT-see), Dardanelles (dar-duh-NELZ)

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: the wartime alliance fell apart, and everybody blamed everybody. We promised containment would get its instruments. The delivery: the doctrine, the dollars, the alliance, and the memo that militarized the whole thing. Three boxes on your sheet: Kennan's containment idea, the Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan, NATO and the military turn. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eleven minutes, and you'll check all three off.

Marcus: Start in Moscow, February 1946. An American diplomat named George Kennan cables Washington a telegram so long it gets a nickname: the Long Telegram. His read on the Soviets: ideology plus old Russian insecurity would keep them pushing outward, and the answer was patient, firm, long-term pressure. Containment. Hold the line, and Soviet power collapses under its own weight eventually.

Maya: [incredulous] Hold on — "eventually"? That was the plan? Just wait them out?

Marcus: [firm] Patience with teeth. Kennan meant political and economic pressure: diplomacy, trade, aid. Squeeze the system, and let the collapse do the rest.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Okay, and the X Article, the magazine piece, that came first, right? Kennan going public?

Marcus: [firm] The telegram came first: February of '46, a private cable. The article lands in Foreign Affairs in July of '47, signed with a single letter: X. Private thinking first, public argument second.

Maya: [playful] "X." The 1947 version of an anonymous op-ed. I love that.

Marcus: The piece was called "The Sources of Soviet Conduct." Overnight, one diplomat's memo becomes the blueprint of American foreign policy. Every president for roughly the next forty years runs some version of it.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Draw me the line, then. Containment versus rollback.

Marcus: Containment holds the line where communism already stands. Rollback pushes it back, rolls Soviet power out of Eastern Europe entirely.

Maya: [confident tone] Rollback, then. The late forties: America pushed Soviet power out of Eastern Europe, atomic arsenal and all. Decisive, final. That's the move.

Marcus: [firm] America held the line. As I read it, the bet in Washington was that pushing meant a shooting war with Moscow, and Truman wasn't taking that bet. That's the answer the exam wants.

Maya: Your turn. Put yourself in Truman's chair: rollback sounds decisive and final, and he's holding the world's only atomic arsenal. Why doesn't he push?
[10-second pause]

Marcus: In Washington's reading, pushing meant a shooting war. Containment existed to avoid that war.

Maya: [confident tone] One down, Kennan's containment idea. Checking that one.

Marcus: [firm] Exam tip, and it's a chronology trap: telegram before article. Private memo in '46, public argument in '47. The order matters because the idea was Washington's working theory before it was anyone's official policy.

Marcus: An idea needs instruments. March 1947: Truman walks into a joint session of Congress and asks for four hundred million dollars, for Greece and Turkey.

Maya: [playful] I was Turkey at a Model UN conference sophomore year. I spent a whole weekend arguing about a waterway, the Dardanelles, out of the Black Sea. I still have opinions about that strait. [chuckle]

Marcus: Then you know the stakes. Greece was sliding into a civil war, communist rebels trying to take the country. Turkey was staring down Soviet pressure over those straits, the waterway from the Black Sea to the Mediterranean. Britain had been bankrolling both, and that February London said it was done. Broke.

Maya: So the domino logic before it had a name. Lose Greece, Turkey wobbles, the whole eastern Mediterranean tilts red.

Marcus: [dramatic] And Truman pairs the money request with the Truman Doctrine: it must be American policy, his actual words, "to support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or outside pressures." No country names. No borders.

Maya: So Greece and Turkey were the excuse, and the doctrine was the point.

Marcus: [firm] The excuse funded the doctrine. It started "about Greece and Turkey," and the promise went everywhere.

Maya: Congress bought it?

Marcus: The money, yes. Greece stayed non-communist, Turkey stayed western. On Truman's terms, containment's first win.

Marcus: Then the dollars. June 1947, Secretary of State George Marshall proposes the plan; Congress funds it in '48. Almost thirteen billion dollars to rebuild Western Europe, flowing from '48 to '51. Seventeen countries took the aid.

Maya: [playful] Thirteen billion dollars. I'm calling it strategy with a bow on it. Generosity doesn't move thirteen billion.

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] The rubric wants both, and it's right. The bread was real: food, fuel, machinery, people ate. And the strategy was real: a recovered Europe buys American goods, and fed people don't vote communist.

Maya: [surprised] Moscow got an invitation?

Marcus: Everyone did. The offer went out to the Soviets too. Stalin refused, and ordered his satellites — the Eastern European countries locked in Moscow's orbit — to refuse with him. He read the whole thing as a wedge aimed at his empire.

Maya: Your turn. You're Stalin. Washington offers your satellite states free American money, no obvious catch. What's your move?
[10-second pause]

Marcus: You refuse. The money ties a country to American markets and American goodwill. Take it, and your satellites start looking west. The generous offer was also a loyalty test, and Stalin knew it.

Marcus: Then 1948 gives the doctrine its first test. That June, Stalin blocks the roads into West Berlin. Truman answers with the airlift: American and British planes flying food and fuel in, around the clock. The blockade lasts the better part of a year — Stalin backs down in May of '49 — and the airlift keeps flying into the fall, about fifteen months total. The line held.

Maya: So the doctrine works. Now Washington wants the doctrine with an army.

Marcus: April 1949. Twelve nations, the United States, Canada, ten Western Europeans, sign the North Atlantic Treaty. NATO. Article Five: an attack on one is an attack on all.

Maya: Why's that the earthquake?

Marcus: It's America's first peacetime military alliance, ever. Before this, Washington's own farewell warning against permanent alliances had held for a century and a half. NATO ended that tradition. American troops stationed in Europe for decades to come.

Maya: [playful] NATO's first secretary general had the one-liner, right? Lord Ismay: keep the Russians out, the Americans in, and the Germans down.

Marcus: The line he's credited with. Three fears in one sentence: Soviet expansion, American withdrawal, German resurgence. Read it and you've got the whole alliance.

Maya: Read the map from Moscow, though.

Marcus: [serious tone] Honestly? From Moscow, every instrument looked like encirclement. American dollars prying at your satellites, American troops parked on your border, an alliance ring around your western edge. The fear was real. Last episode argued both sides of whose fault it was. Consider this the view from the other side of the line.

Marcus: And Moscow answered in kind. 1955: the Warsaw Pact, the Soviet Union and its satellites in their own military alliance. The mirror image, six years late. NATO admitted West Germany that same year — and from Moscow, that looked like the ring closing.

Marcus: One more instrument: the memo. 1950: a National Security Council paper called NSC-68, drafted by a committee led by Paul Nitze. The argument: containment on the cheap won't hold. America needed a massive military buildup. Nitze pushed further: he wanted to roll back communism, going beyond containment. That memo shaped American policy through the sixties.

Maya: And Kennan, the guy who said patience and diplomacy, watched Washington turn his idea into a military machine instead.

Marcus: He spent years saying they'd missed his point. The doctrine outgrew its author.

Marcus: [firm] NATO forms in '49; the Warsaw Pact answers in '55, six years later. Keep the gap in mind: that's the causation.

Maya: Three boxes on the sheet. Let's see how many survive my memory.

Maya: [conversational] Three boxes, let's land them. One: Kennan's containment idea. February '46, the Long Telegram from Moscow. July '47, the X Article takes it public. Containment meant holding the line: patient pressure until Soviet power collapsed under its own weight.

Maya: Two: the Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan. March '47: four hundred million for Greece and Turkey, and a promise with no borders: support free peoples, anywhere. Then the Marshall Plan: thirteen billion dollars, '48 to '51... to seventeen countries? Or was it seventeen billion to thirteen countries?

Marcus: [playful] Thirteen billion. Seventeen countries.

Maya: Right. Generous and strategic at the same time. The bread was real and so were the strings. Stalin said no for the whole Soviet bloc.

Maya: Three: NATO and the military turn. April '49, twelve founders, an attack on one is an attack on all. America's first peacetime alliance. Moscow's mirror: the Warsaw Pact in '55. And NSC-68, the 1950 memo that turned containment into a military buildup. Checking all three.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One, stimulus. Truman's actual words: it must be American policy "to support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or outside pressures." He names no countries. Why does that wording commit America to more than Greece and Turkey?
[10-second pause]

Maya: Because the wording is the commitment. "Free peoples" turns a funding request into a universal promise, with no borders and no expiration date. Once that sentence exists, any communist threat anywhere fits inside it. The blank check was in the grammar.

Maya: Two. Stalin was offered Marshall Plan aid, for the Soviets and every satellite, and refused it all. What does that refusal tell you about what the plan really was?
[10-second pause]

Maya: It tells you the aid led with strategy, though the bread was real. Stalin read the offer as a wedge: American money pulling his satellites toward the West. He refused because accepting meant loosening Moscow's grip. The refusal proves the plan's double edge — it rebuilt Europe and anchored it to America in the same motion.

Maya: Three, fast. The Long Telegram and the X Article: which came first, and what does the order tell you about how containment became policy?
[10-second pause]

Maya: The telegram: February '46 from Moscow, then the article in July '47. The order matters because the private memo came before the public argument. Containment was Washington's working idea before it was anyone's official policy.

Maya: Check your three boxes. Next time: containment goes to war. Korea: the line gets tested with real guns.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] They drew a line around half the world —
Marcus: [professional broadcast tone] and held it for the rest of the century.
