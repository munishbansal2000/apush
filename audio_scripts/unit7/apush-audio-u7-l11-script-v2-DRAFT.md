# APUSH Unit 7, Lesson 11: Nixon & Watergate
# Script v2 DRAFT — Maya + Marcus interview, directed for Fish Audio
# Read note: Maya (host, student-surrogate) + Marcus (expert). # Pronunciation: Burger (BUR-jer), Kissinger (KISS-in-jer), Brezhnev (BREZH-neff), Mao Zedong (MOW zuh-DONG), Ruckelshaus (RUCK-ulz-house), Jaworski (juh-VOR-skee), Ervin (UR-vin), CREEP (KREEP), Agnew (AG-new), Ellsberg (ELZ-burg)

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: a river caught fire, and twenty million people answered. Now the man who signed those green laws runs a burglary campaign to win an election he was already winning. Four boxes: the paradox president, détente, the break-in and the cover-up, and the fall. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now — and give me about twelve minutes.

Marcus: [conversational] Start with the comeback. Sixty: Nixon loses to Kennedy. Sixty-two: he loses the California governor's race and snaps, quote, "you won't have Nixon to kick around anymore." Six years later he's president: gifted, disciplined, and convinced the press was out to get him — with, the books allow, some justification.

Maya: [incredulous] The most paranoid guy in the room wins the biggest job in the country, and we're surprised when it goes sideways?

Marcus: [dry] Box one: the paradox president. Nixon, the law-and-order Republican, never surrendered a popular cause. Last lesson's story, the EPA and the Clean Air Act, happened on his watch, plus OSHA and a ninety-day wage-price freeze. He wasn't secretly liberal. The logic was pure Nixon: never hand your opponents a popular cause.

Maya: [sheepish] I walked in thinking Nixon was just the Watergate guy. "Signed the Clean Air Act" was not on my card.

Marcus: [conversational] The same cold math drove the darker half: the Southern Strategy. The Solid South had voted Democratic since Reconstruction, and by sixty-eight it was cracking. Nixon set out to move conservative white Southerners into the Republican Party for good. Policy, not speeches: slower desegregation, attacks on court-ordered busing, two Southern Court nominees the Senate rejected. But he still seated four justices, including Chief Justice Warren Burger, and the Court turned more conservative.

Maya: [catching] Wait, no. If Nixon built a conservative Court, how did Roe v. Wade happen in seventy-three? His court, legalizing abortion.

Marcus: [impressed] The right objection, and the paradox inside the paradox. Burger's Court leaned conservative, but justices don't take orders. Roe came down seven to two from the court Nixon built. Presidents appoint; they don't control. The common mistake: writing that Nixon's Court banned abortion or rubber-stamped him. It did neither.

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] Exam tip, and this one fixes a real student error: don't file Nixon under one label. "Simply conservative" or "simply corrupt" flattens him, and flat answers don't score.

Maya: [conversational] Box one. Checking that one.

Maya: [thoughtful tone] Your turn. Nixon was America's loudest anti-communist in the fifties. Why did that make him the perfect president to shake hands with Mao? Say it out loud.

[9-second pause]

Marcus: [confident tone] If you said nobody could call him soft, you've got it. A dove reaching out to China gets eaten alive; a hawk does it and the party trusts him. That's "only Nixon could go to China." Box two: détente, the easing of tensions. With Henry Kissinger running the back channels, Nixon talked to both communist giants at once. February seventy-two: Beijing, Mao, more than two decades of silence broken in a week.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And Moscow just watched?

Marcus: [measured] That was the leverage. Moscow and Beijing were rivals by then, so every handshake with Mao made the Soviets eager to deal. That May, Nixon signs SALT I with Leonid Brezhnev, the first cap on each side's nuclear arsenal. Not friendship. Power politics, no illusions; the books call it realpolitik. And the exam trap: don't confuse détente with containment. Containment stopped communism from spreading. Détente managed the rivalry without ending the world.

Maya: [incredulous] A Republican toasts Mao and his party goes along?

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] It worked. Seventy-two: almost sixty-one percent of the vote, every state but Massachusetts and D.C. Which makes the next part strange: a president cruising to reelection, running a burglary campaign anyway.

Marcus: [serious tone] The paranoia never turned off. Box three: the break-in and the cover-up. Start a year earlier. In seventy-one, an analyst named Daniel Ellsberg leaks the Pentagon Papers, the secret history of Vietnam, full of official lies. Nixon, furious and terrified of more leaks, builds a secret team to plug them: the Plumbers. They break into Ellsberg's psychiatrist's office hunting dirt. Nixon keeps an enemies list and aims the IRS at some of them.

Maya: [incredulous] A president with an enemies list and a burglary crew, and nobody stopped him?

Marcus: [serious tone] His reelection committee, the Committee to Re-Elect the President, goes by CREEP, and runs dirty tricks: one crew ordering two hundred pizzas to a Democratic office to jam the phones. Then the prank war turned felony.

Maya: [conversational] Early June seventeenth, seventy-two. Five men in the Watergate.

Marcus: [serious tone] Five men break into the Democratic National Committee's headquarters in the Watergate complex. Cameras, wiretapping gear. A security guard, Frank Wills, finds tape over a door lock, pulls it off, finds it taped again, calls the police. The five are arrested inside; the money in their pockets traces to CREEP.

Maya: [sheepish] So the scandal is the president ordering a burglary himself.

Marcus: [firm] No. Burn that out of your brain. No evidence ever showed Nixon ordered the break-in or knew about it in advance. The burglary was CREEP's crime. The scandal was what Nixon did next: the cover-up. In public his press secretary dismissed it as a "third-rate burglary," meant to sound small. In private Nixon approved hush money and had the CIA lean on the FBI.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] He was going to win. Why not fire the burglars and move on?

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] Your turn. The break-in failed and he's way ahead in the polls. Say out loud why a cover-up looked safer than coming clean.

[9-second pause]

Marcus: [measured] If you said the trail didn't end with the burglars, you're thinking like an investigator. The money led to his reelection committee, the committee to the Plumbers, the Plumbers to the Ellsberg break-in. Coming clean meant confessing the whole machine. The cover-up kept generating new crimes until it collapsed.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Who cracked it?

Marcus: [conversational] Two young Washington Post reporters, Woodward and Bernstein, follow the money. A source inside the FBI, a man they call Deep Throat, meets Woodward in parking garages at night and points them where to look. The Post keeps publishing, the White House screams witch hunt, and the stories hold because they're built on documents.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Two reporters against the White House? How does that not get crushed?

Marcus: [dramatic] By seventy-three the dam breaks: Senate hearings, chairman Sam Ervin of North Carolina, must-see television. Then the White House counsel, John Dean, the man who ran the cover-up, testifies there was a "cancer on the presidency" and describes Nixon approving the hush money. The ranking Republican, Howard Baker, asks the question the country repeats: what did the president know, and when did he know it?

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And Nixon's answer?

Marcus: [serious tone] Deny, delay, claim executive privilege. Then July sixteenth, seventy-three: aide Alexander Butterfield mentions almost in passing that Nixon tapes everything. Congress subpoenas the tapes. Nixon refuses.

Maya: [incredulous] He taped himself committing crimes?

Maya: [conversational] My grandma covers her phone camera with tape.

Marcus: [conversational] The system was built for history; it became the perfect record of his crimes. One tape had an eighteen-and-a-half-minute gap, erased. His secretary said she did it stretching for a phone, her foot on the pedal. [beat] Almost nobody believed her.

Marcus: [dramatic] Meanwhile the vice presidency blows up too: Spiro Agnew resigns over Maryland corruption charges, and under the Twenty-fifth Amendment, Congress confirms Gerald Ford. Then Nixon tries to kill the investigation. October twentieth, seventy-three: Nixon orders Attorney General Elliot Richardson to fire the special prosecutor, Archibald Cox. Richardson refuses and resigns. The deputy, William Ruckelshaus, refuses and resigns. The third in line, Robert Bork, fires Cox. Two resignations in one night: the Saturday Night Massacre. It backfired. The outrage forces Nixon to appoint Leon Jaworski, who wants the tapes just as badly.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] The final blow?

Marcus: [measured] Summer of seventy-four: the House Judiciary Committee approves three articles of impeachment. July twenty-fourth: the Supreme Court rules eight to zero, and Nixon hands over the tapes. Privilege is real, but it doesn't cover criminal evidence. Not even the president is above the law.

Maya: [speaking slowly] The smoking gun.

Marcus: [serious tone] A tape from June twenty-third, seventy-two, six days after the break-in: Nixon's own voice approving the CIA blocking the FBI. Released August fifth. Within days his last supporters tell him the votes to convict are there. His party gives up on him.

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] Box four: the fall. August eighth, seventy-four, Nixon goes on television. No confession: he's lost the support he needs to govern, so he's resigning. Next day the helicopter lifts off and Gerald Ford is sworn in. The first, and still the only, resignation of an American president.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Then Ford pardons him. That always smelled like a deal.

Marcus: [measured] September eighth, seventy-four: a full pardon for any crimes Nixon may have committed. Ford's reason: the country needs to move on. Your instinct is half the country's: millions called it a corrupt bargain, and plenty of historians say it cost him seventy-six. Mercy or deal, nobody's settled it. The exam rewards holding both readings.

Maya: [thoughtful tone] The verdict?

Marcus: [serious tone] Trust. Faith in the national government collapsed and didn't recover, not through Ford, not through Carter. Congress answered: tighter campaign-finance laws, new ethics rules, stronger checks on the CIA and the FBI.

Maya: [playful] The "-gate" thing! Deflategate, all of it. I know that suffix from sports scandals. [chuckle] It started here?

Marcus: [warm tone] Right here. Every "-gate" since is Watergate's grandchild. But the deeper verdict: the system passed a stress test. No coup, no tanks. Subpoenas, hearings, a unanimous Court, a president leaving by helicopter because the machinery worked. The cover-up is always worse than the crime, because the crime ends and the cover-up keeps generating new crimes until somebody presses play.

Maya: [conversational] Four boxes, let's land them. One: the paradox president. Law-and-order Nixon signed the environmental laws while running the Southern Strategy: slowed desegregation, attacked busing, stacked the Court with conservatives including Chief Justice Burger, whose Court then legalized abortion in Roe.

Marcus: [conversational] Two was yours too.

Maya: [confident tone] Two: détente. Beijing and Mao, then Moscow, SALT I, a ceiling on the arms race. Not friendship, leverage; only the hawk could pull it off. Three: the break-in and the cover-up. June seventeenth, seventy-two, five burglars. Nixon didn't order it; the crime was the cover-up: the tapes, the gap, the Massacre, the smoking-gun tape.

Marcus: [measured] And four?

Maya: [thoughtful tone] Four: the fall. Resignation, August seventy-four, announced on the eighth, effective the ninth. Ford's pardon a month later. The verdict: trust collapsed, Congress reformed the rules, and the system removed a president the tapes had caught obstructing justice — without firing a shot. The Court vote: eight to one, right?

Marcus: [firm] Eight to zero. Unanimous. One justice sat out, so eight votes, all against Nixon. That detail makes it land: not even close.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it.

[5-second pause]

Maya: One. A seventy-two tape catches Nixon telling his staff to have the CIA block the FBI's investigation. A historian calls this tape the reason Nixon resigned. What makes it decisive?

[17-second pause]

Maya: It's the cover-up in his own voice: obstruction of justice, six days after the break-in. Once Congress heard it, the impeachment votes were there and his party walked away.

[5-second pause]

Maya: Two. Why could a famous anti-communist open relations with China when a dovish president probably couldn't?

[15-second pause]

Maya: His hawkish record was armor. Nobody could accuse Nixon of going soft on communism, so his party trusted the move. A dove would have been eaten alive at home.

[5-second pause]

Maya: Three. The Saturday Night Massacre: Nixon guts the Justice Department to fire the special prosecutor. What did that night prove?

[15-second pause]

Maya: Nixon would gut the Justice Department to save himself, and the cover-up was now the visible crime. It backfired: the outrage forced a tougher prosecutor on him.

[5-second pause]

Maya: One more, fast. After Agnew resigned, what made Gerald Ford vice president?

[5-second pause]

Maya: The Twenty-fifth Amendment. Box four, landed.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Check your boxes. No president is above the law, but only because the system refused to look away. Next time: the seventies. Stagflation, gas lines, and a president who says America's crisis is on the inside.

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] The trust broke here —

Maya: [conversational] — and the malaise moved in.
