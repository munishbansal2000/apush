# APUSH Unit 7, Lesson 4: McCarthyism
# Script v2 DRAFT — Interview (Maya hosts, Marcus explains)

# Pronunciation: HUAC (HYOO-ack), Venona (veh-NOH-nuh), McCarthy (muh-KAR-thee), McCarran (muh-KAIR-un), Wheeling, Lavender

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: Korea, the war that ended where it started, and the general Truman fired for wanting a bigger one. This time the Cold War comes home — and a senator from Wisconsin starts naming names. Four boxes on my sheet: the fear had facts, the machinery of the scare, McCarthy himself, and the fall plus the verdict. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. About twelve minutes, pauses included.

Marcus: [measured] The fear came from the front pages. The Soviets test their atomic bomb in nineteen forty-nine, ending America's monopoly. The same year, Mao's communists take China, a quarter of humanity under a red flag. Then Korea, with American soldiers dying in a stalemate. McCarthy walked straight into that weather.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] But weather isn't proof. What made ordinary people believe there were spies inside the government?

Marcus: [serious tone] The spy cases landed first. Alger Hiss, a State Department man, convicted of perjury in January of nineteen fifty after Whittaker Chambers accused him of Soviet espionage. Then Julius and Ethel Rosenberg, convicted of passing atomic secrets to Moscow and executed. Soviet agents really had penetrated the Manhattan Project; that's part of how Moscow built its bomb so early.

Maya: [surprised] Wait, Hiss was convicted in January and the Wheeling speech was February? So McCarthy walked into a room that was already scared.

Marcus: [confident tone] That's the read. The Hiss case was already decided. So the first mistake to dodge: calling the whole scare a fantasy. The fear had receipts. The invented part came later.

Maya: [conversational] The fear had facts. Now the machinery, because McCarthy didn't build this alone, right?

Marcus: [measured] Right. The machine was running before him. In nineteen forty-seven, President Truman signs Executive Order ninety-eight thirty-five, the Federal Employee Loyalty Program. Nearly four million federal workers get screened for loyalty. Not for spying. For beliefs and associations.

Maya: [incredulous] Four million people investigated over what they believed? My school makes us sign an academic-honesty pledge every September, and I signed this year's half-asleep in homeroom. I can't imagine the government doing a background check on my homeroom.

Marcus: [conversational] Different scale, same instinct: make people swear, then watch them. And Congress built its own machine. The House Un-American Activities Committee, HUAC, turns on Hollywood in nineteen forty-seven. The question was never "did you steal secrets." It was whether you'd ever been a communist. Ten writers and directors refused to answer. They went to prison for contempt — not espionage. And the studios blacklisted them anyway.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] The Hollywood Ten. So they served time for refusing to talk, and then couldn't work?

Marcus: [serious tone] One-year sentences, then years of blacklist. Some wrote under fake names; some left the country. The blacklist ran until nineteen sixty. And in nineteen fifty, Congress passed the McCarran Internal Security Act over Truman's veto: communist organizations had to register with the government, and their members were barred from defense work. Truman called it a threat to civil liberties. Congress overrode him.

Maya: [thoughtful tone] So the pattern is: punish the belief, not the crime. And the accusation is the punishment.

Marcus: [firm] That's the machinery. Real error students make: "The Hollywood Ten were spies." They were never charged with espionage. They went to prison for contempt of Congress, for refusing to answer. No spies caught in Hollywood. Just ruined careers.

Marcus: [serious tone] One honest beat before we move on. In nineteen fifty-three, Eisenhower signed an order that added gay and lesbian federal workers to the purge list. The theory was they'd be blackmailed by the Soviets. Thousands lost their jobs. Not communists. Just vulnerable people. The Lavender Scare. A scare keeps finding new people to fear.

Maya: [conversational] Two boxes down, two to go. That Lavender beat belongs in box two as well. The machinery kept finding new targets.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Okay, now the man himself. Wheeling, West Virginia, February nineteen fifty. What did he actually say?

Marcus: [measured] He told a Wheeling audience he held a list of two hundred five communists working in the State Department, made known, he said, to the Secretary of State, and still shaping policy. Then the number kept moving. Two hundred five in Wheeling; eighty-one in a later telling; fifty-seven in another. The names? Nobody ever saw them. The Senate investigated and found no list, no names, no evidence.

Maya: [playful] My sophomore English teacher made us read The Crucible like it was a documentary about this. Girls pointing fingers, no evidence, everybody believes it.

Marcus: [conversational] Miller wrote it in fifty-three, right in the middle of the scare, and HUAC called him in too. Your teacher knew what she was doing.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So why did it work? If the list was fiction, why did millions believe him?

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] Because the headlines did his homework. The bomb, China, Korea, the Rosenbergs: every front page made the unbelievable feel plausible. And he had a trick the cautious politicians didn't: accuse first, and make the accused prove a negative. Try proving you've never been a communist. You can't. So people named names, studios demanded oaths, and the safest career in America was silence.

Maya: [energetic] Your turn. Quick brain check before Marcus answers. A man tells you the number is two hundred five, then eighty-one, then fifty-seven, and never shows a single name. What's your read on the list? [9-second pause]

Marcus: [confident tone] There's no list. There never was. Real evidence gets more specific under questioning, not less. Shrinking numbers with no names is the fingerprint of accusation without evidence. If you write that McCarthy produced the list, you've invented the evidence yourself.

Maya: [conversational] Okay, but come on, two hundred five guesses, you'd hit something. Some of his names had to turn out real.

Marcus: [firm] You'd think. None did. The real spies, Hiss and the Rosenbergs, were handled by the FBI and the courts — not by McCarthy. When the cables finally came out decades later, not one of McCarthy's victims was in them. He caught [emphasis] zero spies. He destroyed reputations.

Maya: [sheepish] Zero. Wow. Okay. So much for the man. Big claims, no list, no spies caught.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] If he never caught anyone, how did he stay powerful for four years? Somebody had to be propping him up.

Marcus: [measured] Fear was useful. Republicans used him as an attack dog. "Who lost China" was the chant, and Truman's party was terrified of looking soft. Even senators who knew his methods were rotten stayed quiet, because the ones who spoke up lost their seats. He was a weapon before he was a liability.

Marcus: [measured] The part people skip: he went too far with the wrong target. In March of nineteen fifty-four he accused the Army of harboring communists, the Army, the most trusted institution in the country. The Senate held hearings, broadcast live on television for thirty-six days. Millions watched him bully witnesses. And he got caught asking the Army for special treatment for an aide of his who'd been drafted.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] The hearings were the beginning of the end?

Marcus: [serious tone] The end had a line in it. The Army's lawyer, Joseph Welch, watched McCarthy smear a young lawyer in his own firm on live television, a kid with no connection to any wrongdoing, and Welch finally said it. [beat] "Have you no sense of decency, sir, at long last? Have you left no sense of decency?" The room broke into applause.

Maya: [surprised] On live TV. Millions of people watching the bully get called a bully.

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] Edward R. Murrow had already done a whole broadcast built from McCarthy's own words and contradictions. Eisenhower worked behind the scenes to line up the Senate. In December of nineteen fifty-four, the Senate censured McCarthy, sixty-seven to twenty-two. He kept his seat, lost his power, and died three years later.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So the verdict: where do historians land? Because the spying was real. Doesn't that give him something?

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] The line your textbook is sitting on is both-sided. In the nineteen nineties, the government released the Venona decrypts, Soviet cables America had been reading for decades. They confirmed the espionage was real, and wider than admitted. The threat was not invented. But the cables also closed the case on McCarthy: the real spies were handled by the FBI and the courts — not by McCarthy — and none of his victims were in them. How far the network reached is something historians still argue about. Confirming a threat is not the same as justifying the hunt.

Maya: [confident tone] The threat was real; the weapons were wrong. And the trap answer is "Venona vindicated McCarthy." It didn't. It confirmed the espionage and buried his methods.

Marcus: [measured] One more piece of the verdict. McCarthy the man fell in fifty-four, but the machinery outlived him. The loyalty boards kept meeting, the laws stayed on the books for years. The scare was bigger than the senator.

Maya: [conversational] Four boxes, let's land them. One: the fear had facts. The Soviet bomb in forty-nine, Mao's China the same year, Korea bleeding, and the Hiss case decided with the atomic-spy hunt already underway.

Maya: [conversational] Two: the machinery. Truman's loyalty program screening nearly four million workers, HUAC and the Hollywood Ten in forty-seven, the McCarran Act over Truman's veto, and the Lavender Scare's purge in fifty-three.

Maya: [conversational] Three: McCarthy himself. The Wheeling speech, two hundred five names that kept shrinking, no list ever produced, zero spies caught.

Maya: [sheepish] Four: the fall and the verdict. Televised hearings in the spring of fifty-four, Welch's line about decency, and the Senate censured him that December. The vote was... sixty-seven to twenty-two?

Marcus: [confident tone] Sixty-seven to twenty-two. And the verdict: the nineties cables proved the spying was real and proved McCarthy caught none of the spies. Real threat, wrong weapons.

Maya: [conversational] If the verdict box is the shaky one, good. Hold both sides of that one — the exam rewards both sides.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One, stimulus. A student reads that Truman's loyalty program screened nearly four million federal workers and says, "See? The government was catching spies." What do you tell the student? [18-second pause]

Maya: The program screened for beliefs and associations, not espionage, and it produced almost no spy cases. What it produced was firings and fear. The student's mistake is confusing the machinery of the scare with actual counterintelligence. The FBI caught the real spies; the loyalty boards caught the unlucky.

Maya: Two. McCarthy's number went from two hundred five to eighty-one to fifty-seven, and no names ever surfaced. Why does the shifting number matter more than the number itself? [16-second pause]

Maya: Because real evidence sharpens under questioning, and his numbers kept shrinking. A shrinking number with zero names is the fingerprint of accusation without evidence. The charge was doing political work all along. No Senate investigation ever found the list, because there wasn't one.

Maya: Three. The Venona decrypts, released in the nineteen nineties, confirmed that Soviet espionage in America was real and wider than admitted. Did that vindicate McCarthy? [15-second pause]

Maya: No. The cables confirmed real, wider espionage. Hiss, the Rosenbergs, caught by the FBI and the courts — not by McCarthy's crusade. None of McCarthy's victims appear in them. Confirming that spies existed never justified the hunt's methods.

Maya: One more, fast. What was the Lavender Scare? [10-second pause]

Maya: Eisenhower's nineteen fifty-three order that purged gay and lesbian federal workers as supposed security risks. The theory was blackmail. Thousands lost their jobs. Same scare, new targets.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Check your boxes. Next time: the suburbs, the boom, the backyard, and the fine print nobody advertised.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Honest fear — (production note: hold the dash beat, do not rush it)

Marcus: [professional broadcast tone] — dishonest hunt.
