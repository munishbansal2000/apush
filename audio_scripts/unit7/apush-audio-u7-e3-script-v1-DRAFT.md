Maya: In 1918, Eugene Debs gave a speech in Canton, Ohio, and went to prison for it. He'd run for president before, and he'd run again from a jail cell. The charge was speaking against the war. Last time: America entered the fight. This time: the fight came home, and it brought handcuffs. Three boxes on your sheet: selling the war and silencing critics, the Great Migration and the vote, and Versailles and the League fight. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes, and you'll check all three off.

Marcus: And the cold open isn't an exception. It's the theme. America went to war to make the world safe for democracy, then cracked down on democracy at home.

Maya: Start with the selling part. How do you get a country that was neutral last week to support a world war?

Marcus: You build a propaganda machine. Wilson created the Committee on Public Information, run by journalist George Creel. Its job was selling the war — posters, pamphlets, films, speakers, everywhere. The famous piece was the Four Minute Men, seventy-five thousand volunteers giving four-minute speeches in movie theaters during the reel change. Four minutes, because that's how long the projectionist needed.

Maya: Seventy-five thousand speeches in theaters. That's a lot of peer pressure.

Marcus: It worked. Enlistment soared, bond drives broke records. But the same government selling the war was also silencing anyone who wouldn't buy it. Box one's second half: the Espionage Act of 1917 and the Sedition Act of 1918. Together they made it a crime to say or print anything disloyal about the government, the flag, or the war effort. Over a thousand people were convicted.

Maya: Debs, for one.

Marcus: Including Debs. His Canton speech urged young men to resist the draft. Ten years in federal prison. A filmmaker named Robert Goldstein got three years for a movie about British atrocities in the Revolution, because in 1917 Britain was our ally. And the Supreme Court signed off on all of it. In Schenck v. United States, 1919, the Court upheld the Espionage Act, with Justice Holmes writing that free speech doesn't protect words creating a "clear and present danger" — falsely shouting fire in a theater.

Maya: So the war that was supposed to defend democracy came with the biggest crackdown on speech in American history to that point.

Marcus: Hold onto that paradox. The government mobilized the economy, the War Industries Board telling factories what to make, but the civil liberties story is the one that echoes. Fear of dissent didn't end with the armistice.

Maya: That was selling the war, and silencing its critics: Creel's committee and the Four Minute Men, the Espionage and Sedition Acts, Debs in prison, Schenck's "clear and present danger."

Marcus: On to box two next, where the war changed America permanently: the Great Migration and the women's vote.

Maya: The war created jobs in northern factories, and Black Southerners moved north to take them.

Marcus: Around half a million during the war years, some counts say six hundred thousand. The push was Jim Crow: segregation, disenfranchisement, lynching, the whole brutal system. The pull was wages, industrial jobs, and the hope of something closer to freedom. The Chicago Defender, a Black newspaper, recruited readers north with job listings and train schedules.

Maya: So the war that segregated the federal workforce, which we talked about last episode, also set off the largest internal migration in American history.

Marcus: History doesn't do simple. The migrants found jobs and built communities, but they found redlining, race riots, and new forms of discrimination. The migration didn't end racism, it relocated the fight. And it changed politics: Black voters concentrated in northern cities became a bloc both parties had to court.

Maya: And the vote part of the box: women's suffrage. How did the war speed that up?

Marcus: Two ways. First, women's war work — factories, nursing, every corner of the mobilization — made the old argument that women didn't deserve the vote look absurd. Second, Alice Paul and the National Woman's Party played hardball: picketing the White House, arrests, hunger strikes, force-feeding. There's an HBO movie about it, Iron Jawed Angels. I watched it in history class and forgot the dates but remembered the force-feeding scenes.

Maya: Horrifying, and effective.

Marcus: It was. Wilson had been lukewarm, but women's war service and Paul's pressure converted him. He backed the Nineteenth Amendment as a war measure, a reward for women's patriotism. It passed Congress in 1919, ratified in 1920.

Maya: The Great Migration and the vote, in one breath: half a million Black Southerners moving north, push and pull, and suffrage accelerated by war work and Alice Paul's pickets.

Marcus: Now box three, the big one: Versailles and the League fight. The war ends in November 1918, and Wilson goes to Paris with a plan.

Maya: The Fourteen Points.

Marcus: January 1918, his blueprint for peace. Open diplomacy, freedom of the seas, free trade, arms reduction, self-determination for oppressed peoples, and the capstone: a League of Nations to keep the peace. The Allies had their own plans, mostly punishing Germany and carving up empires.

Maya: Did America's military contribution give Wilson leverage?

Marcus: Some. The American Expeditionary Forces under General Pershing arrived fresh in 1918 and helped break the stalemate, especially in the Meuse-Argonne offensive. A hundred sixteen thousand Americans died. That sacrifice bought Wilson a hearing, but not obedience. The Treaty of Versailles punished Germany harshly: the war guilt clause, massive reparations, lost territory. Wilson swallowed it to save the League.

Maya: So he brings the treaty home, and the Senate kills it.

Marcus: Not quite kills, strangles. Here's the part people get backwards, and I see it in essays every year. Senator Henry Cabot Lodge, who chaired the Foreign Relations Committee, didn't want to destroy the League. He was a reservationist: accept the treaty, but with reservations protecting American sovereignty, especially the Monroe Doctrine and Congress's sole power to declare war. The twelve "irreconcilables" wanted the treaty dead, no League at all.

Maya: Wait, so Lodge wasn't the villain? My textbook made him sound like the guy who killed the League single-handedly.

Marcus: Common mistake. The real story is a three-way standoff. Lodge would take the League with conditions. Wilson refused any conditions, demanding the treaty pure. The irreconcilables refused everything. Wilson wouldn't compromise with Lodge, Lodge wouldn't bend, and the treaty died between them. Wilson suffered a stroke in 1919 while campaigning for it, and his wife and aides ran a quiet regency while he recovered.

Maya: So nobody got what they wanted, and America never joined the League.

Marcus: Never joined. The Senate rejected the treaty twice, and America retreated into a decade of pretending the world would leave it alone. The League limped on without its strongest member, which is part of why it failed.

Maya: So Versailles and the League fight, in sum: the Fourteen Points, the harsh treaty, Lodge the reservationist versus Wilson the purist, and the Senate saying no.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: selling the war and silencing critics. Creel's Committee on Public Information, the Four Minute Men, the Espionage and Sedition Acts, Debs in prison, and Schenck's "clear and present danger" test.

Marcus: Checked.

Maya: Two: the Great Migration and the vote. Around half a million Black Southerners moving north for war jobs, the Chicago Defender as recruiter, and suffrage won through war work plus Alice Paul's White House pickets.

Marcus: On the sheet.

Maya: Three: Versailles and the League fight. Wilson's Fourteen Points, the punitive treaty, the standoff between Lodge's reservations and Wilson's refusal to bend, and America staying out of the League it invented.

Marcus: All three down.

Maya: Two: why did the Senate reject the Treaty of Versailles even though Wilson had negotiated it?

[5-second pause]

Maya: Three: one push and one pull behind the Great Migration.

[5-second pause]

Maya: Push: Jim Crow, lynching, disenfranchisement. Pull: northern factory wages and steady work.
