Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: the war that ate the dream. Vietnam, the draft, and the marchers who built a counterculture. Now that counterculture turns green. A river catches fire in Ohio, twenty million people walk out of school and work, and the most surprising president of the century signs the most ambitious environmental laws of the era. Four boxes: Silent Spring, Earth Day, Nixon's green laws, and the verdict. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now — and give me about eleven minutes.

Marcus: The movement starts with a book, the way L8 seeded it. Nineteen sixty-two. Rachel Carson, a marine biologist already famous for writing about the sea, publishes Silent Spring. Her subject is DDT, the agricultural pesticide America had been spraying since the war. Carson's argument was simple. The chemical climbs the food chain, getting stronger at every step: plankton to fish to birds. Scientists call that bioaccumulation: the higher you eat, the bigger the dose.

Maya: I grew up two blocks from a creek that ran orange after heavy rain, and I thought that was just what creeks looked like. Nobody told me otherwise. So Carson was saying the same thing about the whole country?

Marcus: [serious tone] She was saying the poisoning is invisible. Her opening chapter is a fable: a town where spring arrives and no birds sing. The eggshells had gone so thin they crushed right in the nest. A silent spring. That fable did something the data couldn't. The book became a bestseller, and suddenly "the environment" was a word people used at the dinner table.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And the chemical companies just let her say that?

Marcus: [firm] They spent a fortune trying to destroy her. Lawsuits were threatened, and the industry dismissed her warnings as emotionalism and distortion. But President Kennedy ordered his science advisors to check her evidence, and the evidence held. Carson testified before Congress. She died in nineteen sixty-four. She never lived to see the movement her book started. Eight years later, the EPA banned DDT outright.

Marcus: Exam tip, and the order is the point: the book proved the poisoning. Twenty million people marched. Then the laws came. Science, then streets, then statute. Keep the order straight.

Marcus: [serious tone] The fuse burned for eight years. Then the disasters started arriving like they'd been scheduled. In nineteen sixty-nine, an oil well blows out off the coast of Santa Barbara, California. Crude coats the beaches, and the cameras are rolling: dead birds, on the evening news.

Maya: [incredulous] On the evening news. In living rooms.

Marcus: [serious tone] And that June, the Cuyahoga River in Cleveland catches fire. The pictures were literal. The river was so choked with oil and industrial waste that it burns. It had burned before, but this time the pictures travel. Time magazine puts the story in front of the whole country. Two disasters months apart, and suddenly pollution was something you could see burn.

Maya: A river on fire. That image does more than a hundred speeches.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Wait, seriously — how does a river even burn? It's water.

Marcus: It's the oil and debris floating on top that burns. The Cuyahoga was carrying so much industrial waste that the surface was basically fuel. That's why the image landed: Carson had warned about invisible poison, and here was the opposite of invisible.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So whose idea was it to put all of that anger in one place on one day?

Marcus: A senator from Wisconsin, Gaylord Nelson. He'd watched the antiwar teach-ins on campuses, classes canceled while a whole school learned one thing for a day, and he borrowed the format for the environment. A national teach-in. A young activist named Denis Hayes took the idea and built it into something enormous.

Maya: [speaking slowly] April twenty-second, nineteen seventy.

Marcus: Twenty million Americans show up. Students, housewives, workers. Republicans march too. The cause hasn't picked a party yet. It's the largest demonstration the country had ever seen, and it's about the air and the water.

Maya: Okay, honest question: isn't this basically the antiwar movement with a new cause? Same campuses, same students.

Marcus: [firm] Easy mix-up to make. Earth Day was its own movement with its own cause: clean air and clean water. The crowds overlapped with the antiwar marches, and plenty of marchers opposed the war. But Republicans marched for clean air too.

Maya: Two down, two to go.

Maya: Okay. Your turn. Twenty million people in the streets, and the president is Richard Nixon, the law-and-order Republican. Ask yourself: why would HE build the environmental state? Say it out loud.

[10-second pause]

Marcus: [confident tone] If you said politics, you're most of the way there. Two things were true at once. The country demanded it. You don't ignore twenty million people. And the Democrats were gaining on the issue, with Senator Edmund Muskie, a likely presidential rival, becoming the face of clean air. Nixon never let an opponent own a popular cause. Plus the timing: in nineteen seventy, clean air belonged to both parties — it was popular with everybody.

Maya: But come on. A guy doesn't sign all of that without caring. Some part of him must have loved nature, right?

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] Nobody's fully sure what he felt in private. The public math is what you can answer: the country demanded it, and the politics rewarded it. The exam asks for the motive.

Maya: [sheepish] I would have bet money the EPA was Kennedy's. Or Johnson's. The New Frontier guys, the Great Society. The environment feels like their thing.

Marcus: This one flips the beat. Nixon signed the National Environmental Policy Act on the first day of nineteen seventy, the law that forces the government to study a project's environmental damage before it builds. Then in December, he creates the EPA, puts a tough lawyer named William Ruckelshaus in charge, and Congress passes the laws that give the agency teeth. [beat]

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] The teeth being what, exactly? What did the laws actually do?

Marcus: Start with the air. The Clean Air Act of nineteen seventy sets national limits on what cars and factories can pump into the sky. Then the water: the Clean Water Act of nineteen seventy-two makes it illegal to dump pollutants into rivers without a permit, and pays for the sewage treatment plants that do the cleaning. And the Endangered Species Act of nineteen seventy-three protects the animals themselves.

Maya: And the mechanism? Who enforces all that, Washington or the states?

Marcus: [confident tone] Both, by design. Washington sets the standards, and the states write the plans to meet them. The EPA enforces. For the first time, Washington guaranteed clean air.

Maya: [playful] Wait — is this the Lorax era? The Seuss book, the Once-ler chopping down the Truffula trees? My little brother made me read that about fifty times. [chuckle]

Marcus: [warm tone] Nineteen seventy-one, the year after the first Earth Day. A kids' book about chopping down the last tree, right in the middle of the green rebellion. The culture was breathing the same air.

Marcus: Your turn. The air's cleaner, the rivers are coming back. So who starts saying these laws cost too much, and why does that fight never end? Say it out loud.

[10-second pause]

Maya: Industry, I'd guess. Somebody's paying for the scrubbers.

Marcus: [serious tone] And they lobbied hard. Through the seventies the bipartisan warmth mostly held. Then the eighties: the Reagan administration tried to roll back the enforcement machine, installing agency chiefs who treated regulation as the enemy. The rollback failed. The laws survived. But the warmth was gone. The new conservative movement started casting environmental rules as federal overreach, the way it cast everything else.

Maya: And the science kept going one direction while the politics went the other.

Marcus: Meanwhile the laws kept working. By the end of the century, the air in most American cities was measurably cleaner than it had been in nineteen seventy. The fight stayed over the price: clean air was the goal everyone shared, and the argument was what it should cost.

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] So the verdict. The environment became a permanent political issue: never again somebody else's problem. And the tension never got resolved. Growth on one side, regulation on the other. Every generation since has fought the same fight with new words. Love Canal would prove the job wasn't finished. Toxic waste buried under a whole neighborhood in New York, and Congress answering with the Superfund cleanup law.

Maya: Four boxes, let's land them. One: Silent Spring. Nineteen sixty-two, Rachel Carson, DDT climbing the food chain. The book that started it.

Marcus: On the sheet.

Maya: Two: Earth Day. April twenty-second, nineteen seventy, twenty million people, the largest demonstration the country had ever seen. Checking that one.

Marcus: On the sheet.

Maya: Three: Nixon's green laws. NEPA in January, the EPA in December of seventy, then the Clean Air Act, the Clean Water Act of seventy-two, and the Endangered Species Act of seventy-three.

Maya: Wait, was the Water one seventy-two or seventy-three?

Marcus: Seventy-two. Seventy-three was the Endangered Species Act.

Maya: [catching] Right — seventy-two. Clean Water seventy-two, Endangered Species seventy-three. And the machinery: Washington sets the standards, and the states write the plans to meet them. The EPA enforces.

Maya: Four: the verdict. The environment becomes a permanent political issue. Bipartisan at first, then the consensus cracks: growth versus regulation, the fight that never ends. Checking that one.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it.

Maya: One: stimulus style. Your textbook prints an excerpt from Carson's opening fable — a spring morning with no birds singing. What's the AP point of that passage? What is it evidence of?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The passage is evidence of the argument: DDT doesn't stay where you spray it. It climbs the food chain until the birds are gone. Read it for the mechanism. Carson's evidence was the thinning shells, and her point was the invisible poisoning.

Maya: Two. Why did Nixon, of all presidents, build the environmental state? Walk the chain.

[10-second pause]

Maya: The country demanded it. Twenty million people don't get ignored. The Democrats were outflanking him on the issue, and Nixon never surrendered a popular cause. And in nineteen seventy, clean air drew support from both parties: public pressure and political calculation, in a bipartisan moment — that's the chain.

Maya: Three. A student writes: "The environmental movement was just a 1960s counterculture fad." Where does that sentence go wrong?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The movement outlasted the decade and outgrew the counterculture. Republicans marched on the first Earth Day. A Republican president signed its laws. And the laws survived the backlash of the eighties. This is what a real movement looks like: it got a federal agency.

Maya: One more, fast. Which agency enforces the Clean Air and Clean Water Acts?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The EPA. Box three, landed.
