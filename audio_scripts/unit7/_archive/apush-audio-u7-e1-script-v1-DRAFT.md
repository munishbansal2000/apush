Maya: Last time: the Gilded Age. Giant fortunes, packed tenements, a government looking the other way. This time, the pushback: the progressives, who decided the government should roll up its sleeves instead. Three boxes on your sheet: the muckrakers, democracy rewired, and three presidents, three progressivisms. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes, and you'll check all three off.

Jay: Progressives wanted progress toward what, exactly? The word sounds good, but what were they actually fixing?

Maya: The mess the Gilded Age left behind. Industrial excess, political corruption, brutal inequality. The trusts crushing competition, city machines buying votes, kids working in factories. The Gilded Age answer was hands off. The progressive answer flipped it: freedom through government. Use the government's hands to fix what the market broke.

Jay: Same problems, opposite prescription.

Maya: Box one: the muckrakers. Investigative journalists, basically, diggers. The name was actually an insult. Theodore Roosevelt said they were like the man in Pilgrim's Progress who raked muck instead of looking up at anything noble. The writers took the name and ran with it.

Jay: Who are the three I have to know?

Maya: Sinclair, Tarbell, Riis. Upton Sinclair wrote The Jungle in 1906, about Chicago's meatpacking plants. There's a passage where the meat gets shoveled into carts, and the packers process whatever comes in. Rat droppings, spoiled meat, all of it ground into the sausage.

Jay: Okay, that's properly disgusting.

Maya: I got food poisoning from a gas station hot dog in Barstow once. Anyway, the public read it and panicked, and Congress passed the Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act, both in 1906. Writing moved law.

Jay: And the other two?

Maya: Ida Tarbell took on Standard Oil, a history of Rockefeller's empire and how it crushed every competitor. And Jacob Riis did How the Other Half Lives in 1890, about New York's tenements. He used flash photography, so people could see the dark, airless rooms families lived in. You couldn't unsee it.

Jay: Did Sinclair write anything I'd know?

Maya: There Will Be Blood is loosely based on his novel Oil!. I watched it for the memes and stayed for the oil derricks.

Jay: A flex I didn't expect from you.

Maya: And the muckrakers, summed up: Sinclair's Jungle and the meat laws, Tarbell's war on Standard Oil, Riis photographing the tenements. Exposure as a weapon.

Jay: Fair enough, but writing about problems still isn't the same as fixing the system.

Maya: In the states, not Washington. Washington was gridlocked, so reformers ran experiments closer to home. Wisconsin, under Governor Robert La Follette. People called it the laboratory of democracy. Try the reform in one state, and if it works, it spreads.

Jay: What did Wisconsin actually do?

Maya: Gave voters new tools. The initiative: voters can propose laws by petition instead of waiting for the legislature. The referendum: voters get the final say on a proposed law, a direct yes-or-no vote. The recall: voters can fire an elected official mid-term. Plus direct primaries, where party members pick the candidates instead of party bosses in a back room.

Jay: So power moved from the machines to the voters.

Maya: Right. Then the same energy went national: four constitutional amendments. Rapid-fire. Sixteenth, 1913: the income tax, which gave the federal government its own steady revenue. Seventeenth, 1913: direct election of senators. Before that, state legislatures picked them, which is how corporations bought Senate seats.

Jay: The income tax one feels like the quiet big deal. No income tax, no money for any of the rest.

Maya: Exactly. You can't have an active federal government on tariffs alone. Then eighteenth, 1919: Prohibition, the ban on alcohol. Nineteenth, 1920: women's suffrage.

Jay: Wait, so the Eighteenth gave women the vote, right?

Maya: Nope, classic trap. Eighteenth is Prohibition, nineteenth is suffrage. Eighteen is drink, nineteen is vote. Say it with me.

Jay: Eighteen means drink, nineteen means vote. But hold on, did the Nineteenth actually give every woman the vote?

Maya: On paper, yes. In practice, progressivism left people out. A lot of white progressives just ignored segregation, and some actively supported it. Woodrow Wilson segregated the federal workforce. He even screened Birth of a Nation, this glowing portrait of the Ku Klux Klan, at the White House in 1915. And in the South, Black women faced the same Jim Crow barriers as Black men: poll taxes, literacy tests, violence. The amendment said one thing, the South did another.

Jay: So the reformers who trusted the government to fix everything couldn't see the government's own racism.

Maya: The sharpest version of the critique. And it wasn't only race. A lot of progressive reform had a moralizing streak that treated immigrants and the poor as problems to be managed, settlement houses and Americanization programs. Help with strings attached. Historians still argue about whether to call the movement a success with blind spots or a movement built on exclusions.

Jay: So the people who needed reform most got the least of it.

Maya: Honest version, yeah. So democracy rewired, in short: the Wisconsin Idea, initiative, referendum, recall, direct primaries, and the four amendments: sixteen, seventeen, eighteen, nineteen. Income tax, senators, Prohibition, suffrage.

Jay: Two down. Now the presidents. TR, Taft, Wilson. How were they different kinds of progressive?

Maya: Box three, the classic comparison. Start with Roosevelt. His 1912 platform was the New Nationalism: the federal government should get bigger and stronger, then use that strength to regulate the trusts. Don't break up bigness, tame it.

Jay: And Taft? He was TR's guy, right? Handpicked successor.

Maya: Handpicked, then the friendship exploded. Here's the thing everyone gets backwards, and I had it backwards too. Roosevelt has the trust-buster reputation, but Taft was actually more aggressive. In four years, Taft's administration brought suit against ninety-five corporations. Ninety-five.

Jay: Wait, really? My flashcards say TR was the trust-buster.

Maya: Mine did too, until I checked. Roosevelt talked louder; Taft filed more suits. What broke them apart was the Ballinger-Pinchot affair. Taft's interior secretary opened public land to business, Pinchot, the head of the Forest Service, cried corruption, Taft fired Pinchot. Roosevelt took it personally, ran against Taft in 1912 on the Bull Moose ticket, split the Republicans, and Woodrow Wilson won through the middle.

Jay: So Wilson's the third kind. What was his version?

Maya: The New Freedom. Wilson was actually more hostile to bigness than Roosevelt. Where TR said regulate the trusts, Wilson said break them up, restore competition instead of managing monopoly. Big government versus broken-up business.

Jay: And conservation, where does that fit?

Maya: Under Roosevelt, mostly, and it's progressivism's outdoors chapter. TR set aside something like two hundred thirty million acres of public land: forests, parks, monuments. But even the conservationists fought about what saving nature meant. Gifford Pinchot said use the forests wisely, the greatest good for the greatest number. John Muir said leave wilderness untouched. He'd founded the Sierra Club to protect places like Yosemite.

Jay: So use it versus don't touch it.

Maya: And they collided at Hetch Hetchy, a valley in Yosemite. San Francisco wanted a dam there for water. Muir fought it as a desecration; Pinchot backed the dam as the rational use. The dam got built. Muir lost that one.

Jay: The last box: three presidents, three progressivisms. TR's New Nationalism, Taft the forgotten trust-buster, Wilson's New Freedom. Plus conservation, Pinchot versus Muir.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: the muckrakers. Sinclair's Jungle led to the meat inspection laws, Tarbell's war on Standard Oil, Riis photographing the tenements. Writing as a weapon.

Jay: Two: democracy, rewired. Wisconsin's laboratory, initiative, referendum, recall, direct primaries, and the four amendments. Sixteenth, income tax; seventeenth, elected senators…

Maya: Right, and eighteenth is drink, nineteenth is vote. Plus the shadow: who progressivism left out.

Jay: Three: three presidents, three progressivisms. TR's New Nationalism, Taft's trust suits, Wilson's New Freedom. And conservation, Pinchot's use, Muir's preserve.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One: a 1906 newspaper prints an excerpt from The Jungle. The question asks what federal response writing like this helped bring about, and what that tells you about how progressives worked.

[5-second pause]

Maya: The Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act, both 1906. Progressives used exposure to force government action: investigate, outrage, legislate.

Maya: Two: name the four Progressive amendments and one thing each changed.

[5-second pause]

Maya: Sixteenth: income tax. Seventeenth: direct election of senators. Eighteenth: Prohibition. Nineteenth: women's suffrage.

Maya: Three: Roosevelt and Wilson both called themselves progressives. One difference in how they'd handle a trust?

[5-second pause]

Maya: Roosevelt would regulate it — tame bigness. Wilson would break it up — restore competition.

Maya: Check your three boxes. Next time: 1914, the lights go out in Europe, and America spends three years insisting it's not our fight.

Jay: The government finally rolls up its sleeves —

Maya: and finds out the sleeves were covering someone else's arms too.
