# Pronunciation: SKOHPS (Scopes), DA-roh (Darrow), BRY-un (Bryan), fits-JEH-rald (Fitzgerald), HEM-ing-way (Hemingway), LANG-stun HYOOZ (Langston Hughes), gahr-VEE (Garvey), SAH-koh (Sacco), vahn-ZEH-tee (Vanzetti), LIN-berg (Lindbergh)

Maya: Last time: Versailles: the treaty landed, the League died in the Senate, and America walked back toward isolation. Now it's 1925. Behind one door in Chicago: jazz, bathtub gin, and a cop looking the other way. Behind another in Tennessee: a teacher on trial for teaching evolution. Three boxes on your sheet: the roaring engine, the modern moment, the backlash. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes, and you'll check all three off.

Marcus: Start with the engine, because the roar had a machine under it. Henry Ford didn't invent the assembly line, but he perfected it: the car moved, the worker stayed put. Boring work, cheap cars. And Ford borrowed the logic from Frederick Taylor's scientific management: time every motion, waste nothing.

Maya: So the car got cheap because the job got boring. The trade, right?

Marcus: Exactly the trade. Then add credit. Installment buying, buy now and pay later, putting cars and radios and refrigerators in houses that couldn't pay cash. Then add electricity: by the late twenties, wired homes ran vacuum cleaners, washing machines, electric refrigerators. And advertisers, reading Freud, stopped selling the toaster and started selling the woman you'd be without one.

Maya: Okay, that's diabolical. But the tax stuff wasn't an accident either, right? Somebody decided the rich should keep more.

Marcus: Policy, yes. During the war the top income tax rate hit seventy-seven percent. Under Harding the cutting started; by 1926, under Coolidge, it had fallen to about twenty-five. The theory: the wealthy invest, businesses grow, jobs follow — wealth trickles down. Harding's Commerce Secretary, future President Hoover, pushed the American Plan: unions were called un-American, so companies offered their own health insurance and pensions to keep unions out.

Maya: Welfare capitalism as union repellent. So the government swore it was hands-off while its hands were all over business?

Marcus: The 1920s bargain in one line: laissez-faire for workers, a helping hand for owners.

Maya: Sweet averages, huh? Who's missing from them?

Marcus: Plenty. National income rose nearly forty percent, buying power up about twenty percent, unemployment around three percent. Glowing, on average. But farmers never got the memo. They'd overproduced for the war, prices collapsed, and debt ate the decade. Black workers and recent immigrants mostly watched the roar from outside.

Maya: So box one: the roaring engine: Ford's line, buy-now-pay-later, electricity in the walls, a tax cut up top. And an engine that left the farm behind.

Marcus: Now the moment: what the prosperity felt like. The 1920 census found more Americans in cities than on farms. Cars made it stick: by decade's end Americans owned something like eighty percent of the world's automobiles, and new federal roads carried the middle class to the suburbs.

Maya: My Halloween costume junior year was a flapper: bobbed wig, knee-length skirt, a headband that wouldn't stay on. I felt very modern until it slid into my punch cup.

Marcus: You were wearing the decade's argument. After the 19th Amendment, some women wanted more than the vote — the social freedom to match. Short hair, cigarettes in public, skirts that showed the shin. And the soundtrack: radio. NBC and CBS wired the country together: same programs, same jokes, coast to coast. Movies did the rest; by 1929 nearly three in four Americans went weekly.

Maya: We read Gatsby in English: the parties, the green light, all that ash in the valley. Fitzgerald was writing about this decade, right?

Marcus: He named it: "The Jazz Age" is Fitzgerald's phrase, your bridge to the Lost Generation: Hemingway, Gertrude Stein, writers who came home from the war doubting the old pieties of progress and patriotism. And the decade's Black modernity had its own capital: Harlem. The Great Migration carried over a million Black southerners north, and in Harlem they built a renaissance — Langston Hughes writing "I, Too," jazz pouring out of the clubs, Louis Armstrong and Duke Ellington carrying the idiom nationwide. Art as identity, art as argument.

Maya: And Marcus Garvey? Harlem too, but he didn't want in — he wanted out?

Marcus: Exactly the split. Garvey's Universal Negro Improvement Association preached Black separatism and a back-to-Africa movement. Du Bois and the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People fought for inclusion and equality. Same neighborhood, opposite answers.

Maya: And Lindbergh? He feels like the decade's mascot: 1927, the Atlantic, solo.

Marcus: The hero the radio age needed: one man, one plane, New York to Paris. Modernity you could cheer for.

Maya: My grandpa has a model of the Spirit of St. Louis on his desk. I always thought it was just a plane.

Marcus: It was never just a plane. Thirty-three hours alone, no radio, and when he landed in Paris a hundred thousand people were waiting. The whole country followed it on the radio he'd helped make essential.

Maya: Okay. Engine, moment — now the backlash. A decade this modern had to scare somebody.

Marcus: It scared plenty. Start with Prohibition. The 18th Amendment didn't ban drinking — it banned making, selling, transporting alcohol.

Maya: Wait, so my great-grandpa having a beer at home wasn't breaking the law?

Marcus: Common mix-up, and a good one. Drinking wasn't the crime; the supply was, so it went underground: speakeasies, bootleggers, organized crime scaling up to meet demand. Chicago's Al Capone built an empire on it.

Maya: The law created the criminals. Then the Klan?

Marcus: The Klan rebooted, and this version wasn't just Southern or only anti-Black. By 1925 it claimed five million members, selling "one hundred percent Americanism" against Catholics, Jews, immigrants, anyone outside the white Protestant norm. For a while it ran Indiana politics.

Maya: Five million. That's not a fringe, that's a constituency.

Marcus: And a business. The 1920s Klan sold memberships like magazine subscriptions — initiation fees, robes by mail. Modern marketing for an old hatred.

Marcus: Then Washington wrote the fear into law. The Emergency Quota Act of 1921, then the National Origins Act of 1924: immigration capped at two percent of each group's 1890 population, a formula aimed at Southern and Eastern Europe, and Asian immigration banned outright. The Sacco and Vanzetti case lit the fuse: two Italian anarchists, a shaky murder trial, executed in 1927, and half the country convinced the verdict was about who they were, not what they did.

Maya: And Scopes — the monkey trial. That's the backlash with cameras.

Marcus: Dayton, Tennessee, 1925. John Scopes taught evolution, violating state law, on purpose. The American Civil Liberties Union wanted a test case. Clarence Darrow for the defense, William Jennings Bryan for the prosecution, and the courtroom became a national stage: modernism versus fundamentalism, city versus country. Scopes was found guilty, though the conviction was later tossed out on a technicality, and Bryan died days after the trial. Nobody's mind changed, which was the point. The divide was the story.

Maya: So the decade roared and recoiled in the same breath.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: the roaring engine: Ford's line, installment credit, wired homes, a top tax rate cut from seventy-seven percent to about twenty-five. And the engine skipped the farm entirely.

Marcus: Skipped it and then some.

Maya: Two: the modern moment: cities tipping past farms, the flapper, radio and the movies wiring one mass culture, Harlem and Hughes and the jazz clubs, Garvey versus Du Bois, Lindbergh crossing the Atlantic.

Marcus: Landed.

Maya: The cut to twenty-five: Coolidge's doing, not Harding's?

Marcus: Coolidge signed it in 1926. Harding and Mellon had started the cutting.

Maya: Three: the backlash: Prohibition and the speakeasies, the Klan claiming five million, the 1924 quotas, Sacco and Vanzetti, and Scopes: Tennessee versus Darwin, Darrow and Bryan playing to the whole country.

Marcus: All three, checked.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One: a textbook prints a 1925 newspaper cartoon: the Scopes courtroom, Darrow and Bryan, a monkey doodled in the corner. The prompt asks: what larger 1920s conflict does this image capture?

[5-second pause]

Maya: Not the trial's verdict. The decade's argument: modern, urban America versus traditional, rural America, science against fundamentalism, new mass culture against old norms. The cartoon's point: the courtroom was a stage, and the whole country was the audience.

Maya: Two: name one engine of the roar, and one group it left out.

[5-second pause]

Maya: Engine: the assembly line, installment credit, electrification, the tax cuts — any one earns it. Left out: farmers, drowning in wartime overproduction; or Black workers and immigrants, watching from outside the averages.

Maya: Three: the 1924 immigration law capped newcomers at two percent of each group's 1890 population. Which newcomers was that formula aimed at?

[5-second pause]

Maya: Southern and Eastern Europeans, and Asian immigrants were banned outright.

Maya: Check your three boxes. Episode four in the books. Next time: October 1929, when the ticker can't keep up and the roar stops cold.

Marcus: Jazz on the radio —

Maya: and a trial in Tennessee.
