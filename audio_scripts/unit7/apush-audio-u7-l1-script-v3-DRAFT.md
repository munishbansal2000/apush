# Pronunciation: Yalta (YAWL-tuh), Potsdam (POTS-dam), Kennan (KEN-nen), Gaddis (GAD-iss), Bandung (bahn-DOONG), Volkov (VAWL-kov), Fulton (FOOL-tuhn)

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: the war ended, and the alliance that won it started coming apart. This time: two superpowers, one broken promise, and a fight over who broke it first that outlived them both. Three boxes on your sheet. One: Soviet buffer states, why Moscow wanted Eastern Europe. Two: the Cold War going global, how collapsing empires turned a European split into a world fight. Three: who started it, the three schools of historians still arguing about it. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Twelve minutes, and you'll land all three.

Maya: The setup. Marcus argues the orthodox case: the Soviet Union broke the peace. Dr. Volkov, a historian of Soviet foreign policy, argues Moscow's case: the Soviet Union was defending itself. I moderate, I push, I keep score. Marcus, open.

Marcus: The orthodox case starts with a signature. February 1945, Yalta: Roosevelt, Churchill, Stalin. Stalin signs onto free elections across Eastern Europe, Poland first. Then the Red Army, already sitting in those countries, stays, and the elections never come. A promise on paper, a continent under new management. The alliance never recovers.

Maya: Your turn. Stalin's army is sitting in Poland, and he's just signed that paper. You're Stalin. What does a safe Poland look like to you: a free vote, or a friendly government?

[10-second pause]

Volkov: [passionate] A friendly government, and he wouldn't have hesitated. Think about what Poland looked like to Moscow instead: the corridor every invader used. Napoleon marched through it. The Kaiser's armies marched through it. Hitler marched through it. Three invasions in a hundred and thirty years, the last one costing the Soviet Union roughly twenty-seven million dead. No Soviet leader survives a fourth march from the west. The buffer was arithmetic. You don't leave the door open after the third break-in.

Marcus: [intense] Even real fear built police states. By 1946 and 1947 the pattern is set: Romania, Hungary, Bulgaria, Poland, Czechoslovakia: non-communists pushed out of every government by communists loyal to Stalin, regimes that ruled through repression and terror. Moscow wanted a Poland that was both friendly and free, and those two pulled apart. The body count doesn't explain that part.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Hold on — Roosevelt really thought he could work with Stalin?

Marcus: He thought he could handle him. Then Roosevelt died that spring, and Harry Truman walked into the job, a man Roosevelt had barely briefed on any of it. July 1945, Potsdam: Truman's first meeting with Stalin. Colder room, colder sequel. And here's where the bomb walks in.

Maya: Okay, film-class corner, because Oppenheimer wrecked me. [chuckle] That "now I am become death" scene. My teacher said Truman barely told Stalin about the bomb. True?

Marcus: At Potsdam he mentioned it in passing, like weather. Small talk about a successful test. He didn't know Soviet spies had already been keeping Stalin up to date on atomic developments. But the casual way he dropped it told Moscow plenty: the new president had a superweapon and wasn't sharing it with his allies.

Volkov: [intense] Exactly. Now flip the camera. Your ally builds the most terrible weapon in history in secret, tests it, then mentions it the way you'd mention rain. From Moscow's chair, that looked like a message. And four years later, when the Soviets test their own bomb, every crisis after has an existential edge. The secrecy poisoned the peace before it started — if you were sitting in Moscow.

Maya: Quick exam tip, because this one burns people. When a prompt says Yalta, reach for the promise: free elections, February of '45. When it says Potsdam, reach for the colder room: Truman, the bomb aside, July of '45. Students mash them into one meeting. They're five months and one dead president apart.

Maya: [sheepish] So when the question says Potsdam, the free-elections promise is my answer?

Marcus: [firm] The promise is Yalta's: February of '45, on paper. Potsdam watched it rot.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Poland's vote finally happens in January 1947. The communists announce about eighty percent. Am I supposed to believe that number?

Volkov: Nobody outside Moscow believed that number. But Washington read it as proof of a broken promise, and Moscow read Washington's outrage as proof America would never accept a friendly Poland. Same election, two verdicts. The whole Cold War in miniature.

Marcus: March 1946, in between those two moments, Churchill, out of office, delivers a speech at a small college in Fulton, Missouri, and gives the split its famous image: an iron curtain across Europe. A year after Yalta's handshake, a former prime minister is telling the world the continent is divided. The mood had curdled that fast.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So where does containment come from? I always figured it was the plan from the start.

Marcus: [measured] It starts as one diplomat's warning. February 1946: George Kennan, at the American embassy in Moscow, sends the Long Telegram, arguing Moscow is driven by ideology and old Russian insecurity to push outward, and the answer is to hold the line, long-term, until the pressure cracks it from inside. One memo, thousands of words. The doctrine comes later.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Kennan writes the warning. Who actually reads it?

Maya: Box one down: Soviet buffer states. Moscow's fear was real, Washington's fear was real, and they read the same map in opposite directions. Checking that one.

Maya: Round two: the fight leaves Europe. Marcus, the flashpoints fast. The details are next lesson's story.

Marcus: Two quick setups, thirty seconds each. 1946: Soviet troops drag their feet leaving Iran, backing breakaway regions in the north, and only pull out under pressure. 1947: Greece is sliding into a civil war, Turkey is staring down Soviet pressure, and Washington starts writing checks. The pattern: every local crisis starts looking like Moscow versus freedom.

Volkov: And notice what that pattern does. Define the threat as global, and the response becomes global. But the bigger story lives outside Europe: the empires collapsing. India breaks free in 1947. Over the next decades, roughly eighty new states appear on the world map. Both superpowers show up with aid and arms, always with strings attached. The Cold War stops being a European argument and becomes a global auction.

Maya: Your turn. You're one of the new nations. Washington's offering aid, Moscow's offering aid, both with strings. What's your play?

[10-second pause]

Volkov: You refuse the auction. April 1955, Bandung, Indonesia: twenty-nine Asian and African delegations in one room, and the final statement condemns colonialism in every form, including, pointedly, the Soviet kind. They called it non-alignment: neither superpower owned them. That sentence, in that room, was the whole point.

Maya: [sheepish] And Bandung 1955 — that's the Non-Aligned Movement's birthday, right?

Marcus: [firm] Bandung is the meeting: twenty-nine delegations, April 1955, one loud statement. The movement gets its formal start six years later, in Belgrade.

Maya: Exam tip for the box: if a stimulus shows new nations in the fifties, a photo, a speech, a vote count, the answer is the Cold War going global. Bandung is the one the exam hands you when it wants Berlin.

Maya: Round three: who started it. Marcus, the schools.

Marcus: [measured] Three of them. The orthodox school, the traditional American case: Soviet expansionism broke the Yalta promises, and everything after is the response. Then the revisionists, led by William Appleman Williams in the sixties, reverse the story: America came out of the war with its factories intact, the only bomb on earth, and an economy hungry for open markets everywhere. By that reading, Moscow was reacting to an American economic empire rather than building its own. How much of it was fear and how much was appetite, I don't think that's settled.

Volkov: Then the post-revisionists say both stories are half true. John Lewis Gaddis is the name you'll see. The Soviets wanted security and chased it in ways that looked like aggression. The Americans wanted open markets and chased them in ways that looked like encirclement. Both capitals misread each other, and neither side's fear was imaginary. On this one, I'd say nobody's fully sure, because both sides were answering threats the other never quite understood.

Maya: So the exam tip writes itself: for a "who started it" prompt, name two schools and give each one fact. The rubric has room for both sides. The students who lose points are the ones who pick a team and preach. The exam rewards you for arguing both.

Marcus: [intense] My closing. Volkov says security. Fine. But real fear still built police states: rigged elections, purges from Warsaw to Prague. Stalin faced a real choice in 1945, a buffer or a prison. He chose the prison. The broken promise is on the record, and no body count erases a signature.

Volkov: [intense] My closing: Marcus calls it choice, and choice was real — but a country that buried twenty-seven million people doesn't gamble its border on trust. Washington built its bomb in secret, grew its markets into an empire, and built alliances that looked, from Moscow, like encirclement — then called the Soviet reaction aggression. Read the map from Moscow once, honestly, and the buffer stops looking like conquest.

Maya: [thoughtful tone] I'm not handing anybody the trophy. Marcus has the paper trail: the promise is on the record. Volkov has the arithmetic: millions of dead behind a corridor nobody in Moscow would leave open, and a bomb Washington built in secret. My score: the argument never dies because every school finds something real in the evidence. The exam wants both sides on the page.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: Soviet buffer states. After Yalta's promise died in Poland's 1947 vote, Moscow built its wall of friendly governments, and Washington read conquest in it. Two: the Cold War going global. Roughly eighty new nations — wait, Bandung was '55, and the movement itself was... '61? Belgrade?

Marcus: Belgrade, 1961. The meeting's '55, the movement's '61.

Maya: Right. Twenty-nine delegations at Bandung saying not your pawns, the movement formalized in Belgrade six years later. Three: who started it. Orthodox says Moscow expanded, revisionists say Washington built an economic empire, post-revisionists say both misread each other. Checking all three.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One: a historian writes that the Cold War came from Soviet expansionism in violation of the Yalta agreements. Which school is that, and what would a revisionist say back?

[10-second pause]

Maya: That's the orthodox school. The revisionist pushback: America came out of the war with intact factories, the only bomb, and an open-markets agenda. By that reading, Moscow was answering an American empire. Two schools, one fact each.

Maya: Two, stimulus-style. A 1955 photograph shows twenty-nine Asian and African delegations meeting in Bandung, Indonesia, and the final statement condemns colonialism in all its forms. A historian cites this photo as proof the Cold War had gone global. What's the strongest reading of this source, and its limit?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Strongest reading: the new nations saw the superpower rivalry and publicly refused to be its pawns. The fight had outgrown Europe. The limit: the photo shows the refusal. It captures where the new nations stood.

Maya: Three, chronology trap, fast. The Long Telegram and the Iron Curtain speech: which came first, and why does the order matter?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The telegram. February of '46, then the speech in March. The order matters because students flip them: the memo is Washington thinking in private, the speech is the alarm going public, and both land before containment is anyone's official policy.

Maya: One more, fast. A student writes that America started containing the Soviet Union at Yalta. What's the flaw?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Yalta made a promise about elections. Kennan's warning was one diplomat's memo in February of '46, and the doctrine came later. In '45, containment didn't exist yet.

Maya: Check your three boxes. Next time: containment gets its instruments. The doctrine, the dollars, the alliance, and the memo that militarized the whole thing.

Marcus: [professional broadcast tone] The alliance won the war —
Maya: [professional broadcast tone] — then lost the peace, and never agreed on whose fault it was.
