# APUSH Unit 7, Lesson 6: The Civil Rights Movement
# Script v2 DRAFT — Interview (Maya + Marcus)

# Pronunciation: Thurgood (THUR-good), Faubus (FAW-bus), Selma (SEL-muh), Pettus (PET-us), SNCC (snick), SCLC (ess-see-ell-see)

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Nine teenagers on the steps of a high school in Little Rock, Arkansas. An angry mob. A governor's soldiers blocking the door. Last time: the boom built the houses, and the fine print decided who got the keys. This time: the people who refused to be left out. Montgomery, Birmingham, Selma: the decade that forced America to keep its promises. Four boxes: Brown v. Board, the Montgomery bus boycott, the movement's toolkit, and the legislative harvest. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. About eleven minutes, and you'll land all four.

Marcus: [measured] Box one starts in a courtroom, not a street. For years the NAACP had been chipping at segregation through lawsuits, the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People, for anyone hearing the letters for the first time. Their lead lawyer: Thurgood Marshall. Their target: the "separate but equal" rule from eighteen ninety-six that let the South run two school systems and pretend they were equal.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And the test case is Brown? The dad from Kansas?

Marcus: [measured] The Reverend Oliver Brown, Topeka, Kansas. His daughter Linda had to cross town to a Black school when a white school sat nearby. The Court bundled five cases like hers together, and in nineteen fifty-four, with Earl Warren, the new chief justice, holding all nine votes, the ruling came down unanimous. "Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal." The eighteen ninety-six rule was dead.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So that's it, then? Segregation's illegal, so the schools just integrate and everybody goes home?

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] The ruling killed the doctrine. Then came the implementation order, and it carried a loophole the whole South drove straight through: desegregate "with all deliberate speed."

Marcus: [measured] Your turn. You're a Southern governor in nineteen fifty-five, and the Court just told you to move "with all deliberate speed." What do you hear in that sentence? Think.

[8-second pause]

Maya: [conversational] I hear a suggestion. "Deliberate" sounds like... take your time. No rush.

Marcus: [firm] And that's exactly what the South heard. They called it "massive resistance": the plan to stall, dodge, and outwait the Court. One exam mistake to kill: don't write that Brown integrated the schools. Brown killed the legal rule. Getting Black children into white classrooms took another decade of fights.

Marcus: [measured] A court order needs a movement behind it. That movement starts on a bus.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Montgomery, Alabama. December, nineteen fifty-five.

Marcus: [measured] Rosa Parks refuses to give up her seat to a white passenger, and she's arrested. Now here's what the legend skips: Parks wasn't some tired seamstress who'd had enough by accident. She was the secretary of the Montgomery NAACP, a trained organizer making a deliberate stand. She wasn't even the first to defy the bus rules. A teenager named Claudette Colvin had done it months earlier, but the local leaders decided Parks had the right public persona for the fight. And organizers like Jo Ann Robinson already had the networks in place, ready to move.

Maya: [sheepish] So my whole "one tired woman started a movement" story is the myth?

Marcus: [firm] Not quite. That's the legend, and it's a flattering one. But the truth is better, because the truth was organized. The Montgomery Improvement Association formed to run the boycott, and they picked a young new pastor to lead it. Martin Luther King Jr.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] But how do you boycott the bus when the bus is how you get to work?

Marcus: [measured] You walk. You carpool. The boycott's organizers ran whole carpool networks to get people to work. For more than a year, Montgomery's Black community simply didn't ride. And it worked: in nineteen fifty-six, the Supreme Court ruled segregation on public transportation unconstitutional, and the boycotters took their seats. King walked out of Montgomery a national figure, preaching nonviolent civil disobedience: break the unjust law openly, take the blow without throwing one back. He'd studied Gandhi and Thoreau. Montgomery was the proof it worked in America.

Maya: [conversational] Two boxes down. Brown, then Montgomery: the ruling, then the movement.

Marcus: [serious tone] Box three is the toolkit, and it opens with the federal government finally showing up. Nineteen fifty-seven, Little Rock, Arkansas. Nine Black students enroll at Central High School. Governor Faubus sends the Arkansas National Guard to block the door. Not to keep order; to keep them out.

Maya: [incredulous] A governor uses his own soldiers against nine kids trying to go to school?

Marcus: [firm] And the president answers with soldiers of his own. Eisenhower nationalizes the Arkansas Guard and sends federal troops to walk those students into class. Faubus called it an occupation: "Yankee troops," "occupied territory." That was the point. A court order, backed by rifles. One mistake to kill: Little Rock is federal power enforcing a court order. Birmingham is something else entirely. Keep the two cities straight.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Then the students take over? Nineteen sixty — the sit-ins?

Marcus: [measured] Greensboro, North Carolina. Four Black college students sit down at a Woolworth's lunch counter marked "whites only" and refuse to leave. The sit-ins spread, Nashville then across the South, and the students build their own organization: the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee. SNCC. The movement's youth wing, and they don't wait for permission.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] SNCC — wait, is that John Lewis's group? I read his graphic novel in eighth grade. March.

Marcus: [impressed] March, yes. Lewis led SNCC, the movement's youth wing. And the movement's next big stage was Birmingham, Alabama, spring of nineteen sixty-three. King and the Southern Christian Leadership Conference launch a major campaign there — a deliberate choice, in a city famous for how rigidly it held the color line. The city's public safety commissioner, Eugene "Bull" Connor, answers marchers with fire hoses and police dogs. In May, the children's march, schoolchildren filling the streets, and Connor turns the hoses on them. On television. The whole country watches.

Maya: [serious tone] Kids getting knocked down the street by water cannons. Was that the image that broke it open?

Marcus: [serious tone] That image did it. King is jailed in Birmingham and writes the Letter from a Birmingham Jail: "Wait has almost always meant Never." The campaign never persuades Connor. It persuades the nation. And that pressure is what makes federal legislation possible. The Birmingham campaign paved the way for the Civil Rights Act, a year later.

Marcus: [measured] That August, the movement shows its size. The March on Washington: more than two hundred thousand people, and King delivers "I Have a Dream." And here's the tell about where the movement was heading: the older organizers made Lewis tone down his speech. His first draft wanted to march "through the heart of Dixie the way Sherman did... and burn Jim Crow to the ground."

Maya: [incredulous] They made John Lewis water down his own speech?

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] They were protecting the coalition, and it worked. But that draft was the future knocking. The youth wing wasn't going to wait forever.

Marcus: [confident tone] Box four: the harvest. All that pressure becomes law. The Civil Rights Act of nineteen sixty-four: segregation in public places, illegal; discrimination in employment, illegal. The government even builds an enforcement arm, the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, to police the jobs clause. To this day, it's the basis of nearly every discrimination lawsuit in the country.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So sixty-four ends it? What was left to fight for?

Marcus: [measured] The vote. The Fifteenth Amendment had promised it since eighteen seventy, but across the South, Black citizens still couldn't register. The poll tax fell first: the Twenty-fourth Amendment bans it. Then the movement goes back to Alabama for the hardest fight of all.

Marcus: [serious tone] Selma, March nineteen sixty-five. The movement demands the ballot, and Governor Wallace orders the march from Selma to Montgomery stopped. The marchers cross the Edmund Pettus Bridge — [beat] and the police are waiting with clubs and tear gas. Bloody Sunday, on the national news that night. The march finishes later that month, and months later President Johnson signs the Voting Rights Act. It cracked down on the states that denied Black people the vote, Fifteenth Amendment or not.

Maya: [confident tone] So the exam split is: sixty-four kills segregation in public places and jobs, sixty-five protects the vote. Don't swap them.

Marcus: [confident tone] Exactly. Don't swap them: public places and jobs in sixty-four, the vote in sixty-five.

Maya: [conversational] Four boxes, let's land them. One: Brown v. Board, nineteen fifty-four. Unanimous, Warren holding all nine votes, and "separate educational facilities are inherently unequal." The wedge. Checking that one.

Marcus: [confident tone] On the sheet.

Maya: [conversational] Two: Montgomery, fifty-five to fifty-six. Parks, not a tired accident but a trained organizer: the boycott, the carpools, more than a year, and the Court desegregates the buses. King goes national. Checking that one.

Maya: [conversational] Three: the toolkit. Little Rock: Faubus, the Guard, Eisenhower's federal troops. The sit-ins: Greensboro, Woolworth's, SNCC. Birmingham: Bull Connor, the hoses, the kids, the letter. The March on Washington: more than two hundred thousand, "I Have a Dream." Wait, Little Rock was fifty-seven and Birmingham was sixty-three, right? I always want to flip them.

Marcus: [firm] You've got them right. Federal troops in fifty-seven, fire hoses in sixty-three. Three on the sheet.

Maya: [conversational] And four: the harvest. The Civil Rights Act of sixty-four: public places, jobs, the EEOC. The Twenty-fourth Amendment kills the poll tax. Then Selma, Bloody Sunday, and the Voting Rights Act of sixty-five. And four.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it.

Maya: One: Brown killed "separate but equal" in nineteen fifty-four. So why did it take a boycott, soldiers, and a decade to get Black students into white schools?

[15-second pause]

Maya: Because a ruling isn't enforcement. Brown removed the legal excuse, but "all deliberate speed" let the South stall — so Montgomery proved boycotts could win, Little Rock proved the feds would back a court order, and Birmingham proved the cameras could force Congress. The ruling opened the door; the movement walked through it.

Maya: Two, a source. A newspaper photo from May nineteen sixty-three shows fire hoses knocking teenage marchers down a Birmingham street. The exam asks: what's the point of this source?

[15-second pause]

Maya: The point is the strategy, made visible. Nonviolent marchers met a violent response with the whole country watching, and that sympathy became the pressure behind the Civil Rights Act a year later. The photo isn't about hoses. It's about leverage.

Maya: Three: an exam hands you two laws — one banning segregation in public places and job discrimination, one protecting Black voting rights. Which is which, and why does the order matter?

[15-second pause]

Maya: The first is the Civil Rights Act of sixty-four; the second is the Voting Rights Act of sixty-five. The order matters because the movement sequenced its wins — public segregation fell first, then the movement aimed its full weight at the ballot, the right that protects every other right.

Maya: One more, fast. Name the bridge at Selma where Bloody Sunday happened.

[8-second pause]

Maya: The Edmund Pettus Bridge.

Marcus: [professional broadcast tone] A courtroom opened the door —

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] and the marchers walked through it.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Check your boxes. Next time: Cuba, Soviet missiles, and the closest the Cold War ever came to going hot.
