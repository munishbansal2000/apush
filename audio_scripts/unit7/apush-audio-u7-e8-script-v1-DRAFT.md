# Pronunciation: koh-reh-MAHT-soo (Korematsu), ees-SAY (Issei), nee-SAY (Nisei), OP-en-hy-mer (Oppenheimer), los AL-uh-mohs (Los Alamos), brah-SEH-roh (bracero)

Maya: Last time: one morning in December ended isolationism forever. This time: what the war did to America itself. Spring 1942. A family on the West Coast gets 48 hours to pack. Two suitcases each, tags on their coats like luggage. Three boxes: the mobilization miracle, internment and Korematsu, and the atomic bomb decision. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes, and you'll check all three off.

Marcus: Box one starts with a strange fact. The Depression survived everything Roosevelt threw at it. Then the war did in four years what the New Deal couldn't do in eight. Unemployment fell from around 15 percent to about 1 percent. GDP grew by half. Industrial output nearly doubled. The government hired millions and spent like the future depended on it.

Maya: So the war ended the Depression. Does that mean the New Deal didn't work?

Marcus: It means scale matters. The New Deal built the machinery: the idea that Washington could spend to prime the economy, the agencies, the precedent. But Roosevelt never spent at wartime levels during the thirties because Congress wouldn't let him. The war removed the political ceiling.

Maya: The pump got primed. Who did the work, with the men shipping out?

Marcus: Women, in numbers nobody had seen. The iconic image is Rosie the Riveter, sleeves rolled, "We Can Do It." Behind the poster: women in manufacturing employment rose about 140 percent by 1944. Six million women took war jobs. Black Southerners headed west and north for defense jobs, the second wave of the Great Migration. The government brought in Mexican farmworkers under the bracero program. The Pittsburgh Courier ran the Double V campaign: victory over fascism abroad, victory over racism at home.

Maya: Double V. That's a great slogan. Did it work?

Marcus: As a slogan, brilliantly. As a promise, partially. Black workers got jobs they'd been locked out of, and the migration reshaped Northern and Western cities for good. But the jobs were often the worst ones, housing was segregated, and the military stayed segregated through the whole war. The war opened doors and showed who was still standing in the doorways.

Maya: Checking box one. The mobilization miracle: the war ends the Depression, unemployment collapses, women flood the factories, the Great Migration's second wave, braceros, Double V.

Marcus: On the sheet. Box two stains the whole story. February 1942: Roosevelt signs Executive Order 9066. Everyone of Japanese ancestry on the West Coast, citizen or not, gets rounded up. Around 120,000 people. Two-thirds of them were Nisei, American-born citizens. They got days to sell everything, then barbed wire in the desert.

Maya: For what reason? Was there any evidence of sabotage?

Marcus: None that held up. Military leaders claimed necessity, politicians wanted it, fear did the rest. The order never named the Japanese, it just gave the military power to clear "sensitive" areas. But only Japanese Americans were ever mass-removed. Not German Americans. Not Italian Americans.

Maya: The Supreme Court let it happen?

Marcus: In the Korematsu decision of 1944, the Court upheld it, six to three. National security, the majority said, with almost total deference to the military. Compare Schenck from the First World War, where the Court let the government jail war dissenters. Wartime, the Court tends to step aside. Decades later the verdict flipped: in 1988 Congress apologized and paid $20,000 to each surviving internee, admitting the whole thing rested on race prejudice and war hysteria, not security.

Maya: The 442nd — the Japanese American unit? Weren't they the most decorated in the whole war?

Marcus: The 442nd Regimental Combat Team: Nisei volunteers, many enlisting straight out of the camps. Most decorated unit of its size in American history, fighting for a country that held their families behind barbed wire. One image of the whole contradiction.

Maya: Checking box two. Internment and Korematsu: Executive Order 9066, around 120,000 removed, the Court upholds it in 1944, the 442nd fights anyway, and 1988 brings an apology and reparations.

Marcus: Box two's done. Now the third one, and the hardest. Summer 1945. Germany's beaten — Midway turned the Pacific, D-Day cracked France. The Pacific war has become a meat grinder of islands. Okinawa just cost something like 200,000 lives, soldiers and civilians together. Truman, four months into the job after Roosevelt's death, faces a choice: invade Japan, or use the new weapon.

Maya: Okay, I saw Oppenheimer, so I have to ask. Is it true the scientists weren't sure the first test wouldn't set the atmosphere on fire?

Marcus: They ran the numbers and decided it wouldn't, but yes, the question was asked out loud at Los Alamos. That shows how new this was. The Manhattan Project had been running in secret since 1942, and by July 1945 the test in New Mexico worked. Truman now held a weapon nobody had ever used, and his military advisers were planning an invasion of Japan with casualty estimates that ran into the hundreds of thousands of American lives.

Maya: So the pro-bomb case: invasion costs more lives than the bomb.

Marcus: The heart of it. The invasion, planned in two waves, was expected to be Okinawa multiplied. Japan's military was preparing civilians, including children, to resist. The Potsdam Declaration in July demanded unconditional surrender and warned of "prompt and utter destruction." Japan's leadership deadlocked. Then August 6th: Hiroshima. August 9th: Nagasaki. As many as 226,000 dead. And on August 8th, between the two bombs, the Soviet Union invaded Manchuria, crushing Japan's last hope of Soviet-mediated peace terms.

Maya: The Soviets invaded too? Then how do we know it was the bombs, not that?

Marcus: Now you're asking the historian's question. It gets genuinely contested. One school says the bombs shocked Japan's leadership into surrender and saved lives on both sides. Another, associated with the historian Gar Alperovitz, argues Japan was already beaten, and the real audience for the bomb was Stalin: a warning that America owned the postwar world. The evidence is mixed. Some Japanese leaders cited the bombs. Others pointed to the Soviet invasion. Truman's own diary mentions both.

Marcus: There's no settled answer, and be suspicious of anyone who tells you otherwise. What we can say: the decision ended the war within days, it opened the nuclear age, and every president since has lived with the precedent. The "Good War" story says the bomb saved lives. Critics say it targeted civilians and wasn't necessary. Both sides have real evidence. That's why historians still fight about it.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: the mobilization miracle. The war ends the Depression, unemployment collapses, women in manufacturing up 140 percent, the Second Great Migration, braceros, Double V.

Marcus: Checked.

Maya: Two: internment and Korematsu. Executive Order 9066 (February 1942, right?), around 120,000 Japanese Americans removed, the Court upholds it in 1944, the 442nd becomes the most decorated unit, and 1988 brings the apology and the $20,000 payments.

Marcus: February 1942. On the sheet.

Maya: Three: the atomic bomb decision. Manhattan Project since 1942, Okinawa's cost, the invasion estimates, Potsdam in July, Hiroshima August 6th, Nagasaki August 9th, the Soviets invade Manchuria in between, and the historians split: necessity or the Alperovitz argument about Stalin. No settled answer.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One: a textbook prints this line from the Korematsu majority opinion: the Court defers to military judgment in wartime. The prompt asks: how does this reasoning compare to Schenck, and what does the comparison tell you about the Court in wartime?

[5-second pause]

Maya: Both cases show the same pattern: in wartime, the Court steps aside for the government. Schenck let Washington jail antiwar dissenters in 1919; Korematsu let it intern citizens in 1944. The comparison tells you civil liberties shrink when the Court defers, and the 1988 redress shows the country judged Korematsu a failure.

Maya: Two: the strongest one-sentence argument for each side of the bomb decision.

[5-second pause]

Maya: For: the invasion would have cost hundreds of thousands of lives, and the bombs ended the war in days. Against: Japan was already defeated, the bombs targeted civilians, and the Soviet invasion may have been the real trigger, which makes the decision look like a warning to Stalin.

Maya: Three: the war ended a Depression the New Deal couldn't fix in eight years. What did that prove, and what did the miracle cost at home?

[5-second pause]

Maya: It proved spending at wartime scale could end a depression: unemployment fell from fifteen percent to about one. The cost was rationing and a mountain of debt — and a miracle that still handed Black workers the worst jobs. Scale won. Justice lagged.

Maya: Check your three boxes. Next time: the whole unit, rapid-fire. Progressivism to the bomb in eight minutes.

Marcus: The war America won abroad —

Maya: and the promises it still owed at home.
