Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: the workers fought the bosses — and immigrants stood on both sides of the picket line: strikebreakers AND union members. That tension is this whole episode. The mills were hungry for hands, and the hands came: from Italy, from Russia, from China. The welcome they got was complicated. Four boxes: one, the new immigration wave. Two, the immigrant city: tenements, neighborhoods, chain immigration. Three, the Chinese Exclusion Act. Four, the nativists, plus the honest case for closing the door. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. About thirteen minutes.

Marcus: [conversational] Start with who changed. Before the Civil War, most immigrants came from northern and western Europe: the British Isles, Germany, Ireland. They spoke the language, or something close to it. Then the map flips. From the 1880s, the new wave comes from southern and eastern Europe: Italy, Russia, Poland, the Balkans. Mostly Catholic and Jewish, in a country that was still mostly Protestant. The scale: between 1870 and 1920, something like 28 million people crossed the ocean into the United States.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And the mills were pulling them in. Last episode: cheap hands, de-skilled work, the whole engine. Somebody had to feed it.

Marcus: [measured] That was the pull: industrialization ran on cheap labor, and the factories needed bodies faster than the country could grow them. Steamships made the crossing cheaper, and word went home that there was work. One cousin lands a job and sends for the next. Chain immigration: links forged one family letter at a time.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So the 1880s wave — Irish and German, mostly?

Marcus: [firm] Those are the older wave: Irish and German. By the 1880s the faces are Italian, Russian, Polish. The switch is what the exam tests.

Maya: [warm tone] My class did the Tenement Museum virtual tour in eighth grade. One family, four rooms, and the guide said six families shared a hallway toilet. I still think about that hallway.

Marcus: [warm tone] Box two, and the museum got it right. Unlike the old wave, which scattered to farms, the new immigrants piled into the industrial cities, New York, Chicago, Pittsburgh, and into tenements where the air never moved and the rooms never emptied. Jacob Riis photographed it in 1890, How the Other Half Lives, and the contrast did the arguing for him: Carnegie and Frick's mansions on Fifth Avenue, tenements a few avenues east.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And they kept their own worlds, Little Italy, the Lower East Side. Was that choice, or did the city push them there?

Marcus: [conversational] Both. The enclaves had the food, the churches and synagogues, the foreign-language papers: the Jewish Daily Forward, Il Progresso. Savings clubs and burial societies: the whole scaffolding of a neighborhood. And the city pushed too: cheap rents near the factories. Millions of young men never planned to stay at all. They'd work a season or two and sail home. The books call them birds of passage.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Ellis Island, 1890, the front door of America?

Marcus: [conversational] January 1st, 1892. That 1890 date is a myth. The federal front door: health checks and paperwork, mostly Europeans walking through. The other door sat in San Francisco Bay: Angel Island, opened 1910.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So the West Coast Ellis Island, same idea, other ocean?

Marcus: [conversational] Angel Island was built to detain and interrogate, and it sat on an island on purpose, fenced off with a guard tower, where questioning could drag on for weeks.

Maya: [thoughtful tone] I saw a photo once of the walls inside those barracks, poems carved into the wood. People stuck there for months, writing it on the walls.

Marcus: [serious tone] They're real. Chinese detainees carved their anger and homesickness into the barracks walls. Because Angel Island was aimed at one group above all. And that brings box three, and the law behind it: the Chinese Exclusion Act.

Maya: [sheepish] The law that banned Chinese immigration. 1882.

Marcus: [firm] Not quite: it's wrong in the way the exam tests. The 1882 Act suspended Chinese LABOR immigration, laborers only, for ten years. Merchants, students, teachers, diplomats: all exempt. Ten years, and then Congress renewed it, the Geary Act of 1892, and later made it permanent. The first time Washington ever shut the door on a whole nationality. Race became a federal border criterion.

Maya: [sheepish] It was laborers only. I've got that filed. The door stayed open for merchants, students, teachers, diplomats.

Marcus: [conversational] Most people say it that way. It was a labor ban with carved-out exceptions, which is why Angel Island later spent so much effort interrogating who was really exempt.

Marcus: [serious tone] Now the why. The Chinese had come for the railroads and the gold country, thousands of workers. But the railroads got finished, the jobs got scarcer, and in the hard years white workers blamed Chinese laborers for working cheaper and dragging wages down. Chinatowns grew partly as shelter from that hostility. And Congress listened. 1882 gave America two immigration laws in one year. The famous one sorted people by race. The other one, the Immigration Act, put [emphasis]a fifty-cent head tax on every arriving non-citizen, and barred convicts, the mentally ill, and anyone likely to become a public charge.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Two laws, one year, and everybody remembers only one of them. The head-tax one doesn't even have "Chinese" in it.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Wait, so the head tax was part of the Exclusion Act?

Marcus: [firm] The head tax was in the other law. It's an easy mix-up: the Exclusion Act sorted people by race, and the Immigration Act carried the fifty-cent head tax and the excluded categories. Two laws, one year. Keep them on separate cards.

Marcus: [conversational] One footnote before we land the boxes. Same decade, different story. In 1890, the census office reported that the frontier LINE had disappeared. File it away. It matters in two episodes.

Maya: [conversational] I'll file that, though the prep books flatten this one into a single word: "closed."

Marcus: [firm] The bulletin said the frontier line had disappeared. "Closed" was the gloss a historian put on it in an 1893 essay.

Maya: [conversational] Two boxes down.

Marcus: [measured] Now the backlash, in full: box four. The new immigrants frightened a lot of native-born Americans, and the fright got organized. The American Protective Association, founded 1887: anti-immigrant, anti-Catholic, carrying on the Know-Nothings' tradition. And keep the dates apart. The Know-Nothings were the 1850s. The APA was the 1880s.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] The APA is the loud version of the backlash. But there's a cleverer one coming in 1894. Your turn: you're a Boston nativist. You want fewer Italians and Poles, but you can't write a nationality into federal law. What test do you invent instead? Think it through.

[10-second pause]

Marcus: [conversational] You invent a literacy test: read and write, which sounds neutral. The Immigration Restriction League, founded 1894, ran that play, with Senator Henry Cabot Lodge carrying it in Congress. And the disguise didn't fool anyone at the time. The nativist talk was getting blunt: fears that Anglo-Saxon America was committing "race suicide" by admitting what they called "inferior" races. That was the movement's own vocabulary. Lodge and Grant used it in the open.

Maya: [passionate] Okay, I'll make their case, because somebody has to. It's 1894, you're a working man in Chicago, and the boss just hired thirty Italians at half your wage. You watched the strikebreakers roll in. The fear isn't crazy: every panic, wages fall, and the line of desperate men at the gate gets longer. Why wouldn't you want the door shut?

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] The boss set the wage. He cut your pay in the panics whether the Italians existed or not; you heard that in the labor episode. And the same cheap-labor machine that hired the strikebreakers built the mills and the rails. Blaming the newcomers let the owners off the hook for the system they designed.

Maya: [passionate] But the pace! Twenty-eight million people in fifty years, most of them landing in a handful of cities, keeping their own papers and churches. A country gets to ask whether it can absorb that. The question is arithmetic.

Marcus: [reasonable] The point was the contradiction. The economy was demanding those hands; the factories couldn't staff themselves without immigrants. Shut the door in 1894 and the mills starve, the same mills the whole industrial story depends on. The country wanted the labor and hated the laborers.

Maya: [intense] Then what about the literacy test? Read and write. That's a filter any honest worker can pass. If the new wave is really the future, let it prove it.

Marcus: [firm] It was a wall wearing a filter's clothes. The League's own design showed who it would screen out: southern and eastern Europeans, almost no one else. And here's the check to run on it. If the test were really about reading, why did its biggest champions keep talking about inferior races? The neutral language was the disguise.

Maya: [intense] I'll give you the disguise. Lodge's crowd meant what they meant. The wage fear was honest. When the boss can always find a hungrier man, the worker's only leverage dies. The Knights knew it, the AFL knew it; that's why the AFL largely shut out the unskilled. Everybody in 1894 was drawing lines. The nativists just drew theirs at the water's edge.

Maya: [passionate] One more case for them, and it's the one the APA was really about. Forget wages. This was a Protestant country watching Catholic churches rise on its corners, parishes running their own schools, priests telling the flock how to vote. The APA said Rome wanted the republic. The real question was whether the country stays itself.

Marcus: [reasonable] That fear was real, and it ran elections; the APA's whole engine was anti-Catholic. But the cities gave their own answer: the enclave: foreign-language papers and mutual-aid societies, the scaffolding from box two. The neighborhoods did the absorbing the nativists said was impossible — on the immigrants' own schedule.

Marcus: [measured] And the AFL drew theirs at the union hall, the bosses drew theirs around the plant. But only one of those lines became federal law aimed at a race. The Chinese Exclusion Act turned private prejudice into public policy, the first time America sorted human beings by nationality at the border. The anxiety was real. The law it built was something new, and something worse.

Maya: [conversational] Four boxes, let's land them. One: the new wave. From the 1880s, southern and eastern Europe, Italians, Russians, Poles, poor, Catholic or Jewish, and about 28 million crossed between 1870 and 1920 because the industrial machine was hungry for hands.

Marcus: [conversational] Two: the immigrant city. Tenements, overcrowded and airless; Riis photographed them in 1890. Ethnic neighborhoods kept the home culture alive: the foreign-language papers, the parish churches. The savings clubs and burial societies did the rest. And the two doors. Ellis Island, 1892, mostly Europeans walking through; Angel Island, 1910, built to detain, aimed at the Chinese.

Maya: [conversational] Three: the Exclusion Act. 1882: Chinese labor immigration suspended for ten years, with exemptions for merchants, students, teachers, and diplomats, and Congress kept renewing it. The first federal law aimed at a whole nationality.

Maya: [conversational] Four: the nativists. The APA, 1887, anti-immigrant, anti-Catholic, the Know-Nothings'... grandchild? And the literacy-test crowd, 1894. The test that was really a race filter.

Marcus: [conversational] Grandchild works. Different decade, same family.

Maya: [conversational] All four checked.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it.

Maya: Question one, and it's a stimulus. A student writes: "The 1880s immigration wave was mostly Irish and German Catholics fleeing the famine." Name the two traps in that one sentence.

[10-second pause]

Maya: Two traps. Irish and German is the old wave. What the record shows is the post-1880 wave was Italian, Russian, Polish, mostly Catholic or Jewish, and the famine was the 1840s, decades before the 1880s. Which means the exam tests the wave switch. Keep the two waves on separate cards.

Maya: Question two: why was the Chinese Exclusion Act a first in American law?

[10-second pause]

Maya: It was the first federal immigration law aimed at a whole nationality. What the record shows is that Congress suspended Chinese labor immigration for ten years in 1882, laborers only, with exceptions for merchants, students, teachers, diplomats, and then renewed it. Which means Washington had turned race into a federal border criterion for the first time.

Maya: Question three: a nativist in 1894 calls the literacy test "neutral." What's the strongest evidence he's not telling the truth?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The test's own champions named the targets. What the record shows is the movement's own language gave it away: "race suicide" warnings, "inferior" races, from the same crowd pushing a test designed to screen out southern and eastern Europeans. Which means the neutrality was costume, and the design underneath was a race filter.

Maya: One more, fast. 1882 gave America two immigration laws. Name the pair.

[10-second pause]

Maya: The Chinese Exclusion Act, laborers, ten years; and the Immigration Act, the fifty-cent head tax and the excluded categories.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] The lock got invented — and the fight over the key never ended. Next time: the farmers' revolt. Populism, the People's Party, and the election of 1896. Check your boxes.
