# APUSH Unit 9, Episode 2: End of the Cold War
# Script v2 DRAFT
# Format: Interview (Maya + Marcus)
# Read note: Maya tracks three boxes and checks each off as earned. [5-second pause]
# [5-second pause] and [2-second pause] marks are production notes for real silence in the self-test.
# Pronunciation: Gorbachev (GOR-buh-choff), glasnost (GLAHZ-nost), perestroika (peh-reh-STROY-kuh), Wałęsa (vah-WEN-sah)

Maya: Last time: Reagan, the debt, the coalition, the argument that never ended. This time: the Cold War ends. Not with a bang, but with a border guard who didn't get the memo. Three boxes. Circle the ones you couldn't explain. One: why the Soviet Union rotted from inside. Two: what Gorbachev tried to do, and why it backfired. Three: did Reagan win it, or was it already falling? Eight minutes. Starting November 9, 1989.

Marcus: Bornholmer Straße, East Berlin. It's evening. A border guard named Harald Jäger is on duty. Earlier that day, the East German government announced new travel rules, but nobody told the guards what they meant. A crowd gathers. Thousands of East Berliners, they've heard the border's open. Jäger calls his superiors. No clear orders. The crowd keeps growing. Finally, he just opens the gate. People flood through. The Wall, symbol of the Cold War, falls because one tired guard made a decision.

Maya: No way. It wasn't planned?

Marcus: Not that night. The Wall was supposed to open eventually, but the bureaucracy messed up the announcement. A spokesman said the rules took effect "immediately" when they weren't supposed to. The crowd took him literally. History sometimes turns on confusion.

Maya: Box one: why was the Soviet Union rotting? What did "stagnation" actually look like?

Marcus: By the 1980s, the Soviet economy had stopped growing. The central planning system couldn't handle a modern economy. They could build tanks and rockets, but they couldn't make decent jeans or computers. The technology gap with the West kept widening. By the mid-eighties the Soviets had maybe ten thousand computers in the whole country. The US had over a million. And Afghanistan: the Soviets invaded in 1979, got stuck in a guerrilla war, and bled for a decade. Roughly fifteen thousand Soviet soldiers died there. It was their Vietnam.

Maya: What did "stagnation" feel like, day to day?

Marcus: Empty shelves and full cynicism. The joke going around Moscow was "we pretend to work, and they pretend to pay us." Then Chernobyl blew in 1986 — a reactor exploding because the system rewarded cover-ups over safety. Gorbachev let the news out, and the whole country saw the rot at once.

Marcus: Three things, feeding each other. Economic stagnation meant they couldn't keep up militarily or technologically. Afghanistan drained what little they had. And the nationalities, Ukrainians, Balts, Central Asians, never wanted to be in the Soviet Union. When Moscow looked weak, they started pulling away.

Maya: Checking box one. The rot: a planned economy that couldn't innovate, a losing war in Afghanistan, captive nations waiting for weakness.

Marcus: Box two: Gorbachev. He comes to power in 1985, young by Soviet standards, 54. He sees the rot. He tries two reforms. Glasnost, which means openness, let people criticize the system, talk freely, read uncensored news. Perestroika, restructuring, tried to introduce market elements into the planned economy.

Maya: That sounds reasonable. So he was trying to take the whole thing apart?

Marcus: The opposite. He was trying to save it. That's the tragedy. Because once you let people tell the truth, they tell the truth about everything. Glasnost unleashed criticism not just of the economy but of the whole Soviet project. The Ukrainians said, why are we in this union? The Balts said, we were independent before 1940. Perestroika didn't work because you can't half-reform a planned economy. It created chaos without creating growth. Gorbachev tried to save the system by loosening it, and the loosening destroyed it.

Maya: Like trying to fix a dam by poking holes?

Marcus: He thought controlled reform would strengthen the USSR. Instead, each reform demanded the next, until the whole thing burst.

Maya: Box two checked. Glasnost meant openness, perestroika meant restructuring. Both were rescue attempts. Both unleashed the forces that killed the patient.

Marcus: Then the fall, fast. 1989: Poland holds free elections. Solidarity wins.

Maya: An electrician beat the Communist Party?

Marcus: Lech Wałęsa, Gdansk shipyards. Solidarity started as a strike and became a movement — ten million members. When they won, every other captive nation got the message: it can be done. Hungary opens its border next. That August, about two million people formed a human chain across all three Baltic republics, the Baltic Way. You can't arrest two million people. The Berlin Wall falls in November. 1990: the Baltic republics declare independence. Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania sang their way out — the Singing Revolution. Hundreds of thousands of people holding hands across three countries, singing the songs Moscow had banned. Hard to shoot a choir. 1991: hardliners try a coup in August, it collapses in three days because nobody follows orders, Boris Yeltsin stands on a tank. By December, the Soviet flag comes down over the Kremlin. Two years, start to finish.

Maya: Two years? After forty-five years of Cold War?

Marcus: That's how brittle it was. Once the fear went away, there was nothing holding it together. No one was willing to shoot to save it.

Maya: If it was that weak, why was everyone so terrified of it?

Marcus: Because a weak system with tens of thousands of nuclear weapons is the scariest kind. The fear was real where the strength was hollow. That paradox is what the whole Cold War ran on.

Maya: And it ended without a war. Lucky?

Marcus: Partly. December 1989, Bush and Gorbachev meet at Malta and basically declare the Cold War over. The next year Gorbachev wins the Nobel Peace Prize. For forty years, everyone assumed two endings: the Soviets go down and take the world with them, or it never ends. It ended a third way — and the people steering it deserve credit.

Maya: Box three: did Reagan win it? I know this one's the big debate.

Marcus: The case for Reagan: he called it an evil empire, he built up the military, he proposed missile defense, he said "tear down this wall" in 1987. Supporters argue the pressure forced Moscow to spend itself into collapse, and Reagan's willingness to negotiate gave Gorbachev room to reform.

Maya: And the case against?

Marcus: The rot was structural. No American president could have fixed the Soviet economy or made Afghanistan winnable or kept the Balts happy. Gorbachev's reforms, not Reagan's speeches, triggered the collapse. And plenty of historians point out that Reagan's first term was confrontational, his second term was cooperative. The cooperation mattered more than the confrontation.

Maya: Cooperative how? The "tear down this wall" guy?

Marcus: Reykjavik, 1986. Reagan and Gorbachev nearly agreed to eliminate nuclear weapons entirely. The deal died over missile defense, but the two men came out trusting each other. That trust is what made the peaceful ending possible.

Maya: But they did sign something real, right? Not just talk.

Marcus: The INF Treaty, 1987. Reagan and Gorbachev agreed to eliminate every short and medium-range nuclear missile. It was the first treaty that actually shrank the arsenals instead of capping them. Real weapons, destroyed, on camera.

Maya: I need your landing spot.

Marcus: I think it's both, but the structure mattered more than the person. The Soviet Union was going to fall. Reagan shaped how it fell, peacefully instead of violently, and he deserves credit for working with Gorbachev when it counted. But the idea that one president's toughness caused the collapse? Too neat, really. It erases the Poles who struck, the Afghans who fought, the Soviet citizens who stopped believing.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: the rot — economic stagnation, Afghanistan, nationalism. Two: Gorbachev's glasnost and perestroika — glasnost was the openness one, right? Both meant as rescue, acted as poison.

Marcus: Right. Glasnost, openness. Perestroika, restructuring. Three: the Reagan debate, pressure plus negotiation versus structural collapse. The honest answer is both, with structure doing most of the work.

Maya: Checking all three.

Marcus: The aftermath question: did America win? We got a unipolar moment, the 1990s as the sole superpower. But the victory had costs. We expanded NATO, which Russia saw as betrayal. We assumed liberal democracy would spread everywhere. Francis Fukuyama wrote about "the end of history." That didn't age well.

Maya: The end of history? Like, no more big conflicts?

Marcus: The idea that Western liberal democracy was the final form of government — everything else would converge. Then came 9/11, Iraq, the financial crisis, China's rise, Russia's revanchism. History came back with a vengeance.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer out loud before I give it. One: glasnost versus perestroika — what was each, and why did both backfire? [2-second pause] Glasnost was political openness — once people could criticize the system, they delegitimized all of it. Perestroika was economic restructuring — it broke central planning without building a working market, so chaos instead of real growth.

Two: a historian says Reagan's buildup caused the collapse. Best counterargument? [2-second pause] The rot was internal — economic stagnation, Afghanistan, nationalism. Gorbachev's reforms triggered the fall, not outside pressure alone.

Three: 'the Wall fell because a border guard didn't get the memo.' What does that caption get right? [2-second pause] The ending came from confusion and ordinary people — not a treaty, not a battle. Far too brittle to survive its own mistake.

Maya: The Wall fell because a guard didn't get the memo. Sometimes that's how history works. Check your three boxes.
