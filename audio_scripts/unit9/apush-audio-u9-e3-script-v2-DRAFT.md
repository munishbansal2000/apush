# Pronunciation: NAFTA (NAF-tuh)

Maya: Last time: the Wall fell, the Soviet Union collapsed, America stood alone as the superpower. This time: the nineties. The modem screeches, the economy booms, and everyone wonders if history just ended. Three boxes. Circle the ones you couldn't explain. One: globalization, who won and who lost. Two: the internet, how it changed everything before we noticed. Three: the holiday question, was the peace real? Eight minutes. Starting with a sound you might not recognize.

Marcus: It's 1995. You pick up the phone, you hear a dial tone, you type a number into your computer, and then: screeching, static, beeping. The modem handshake. You're connecting to the internet at 28.8 kilobits per second. Slower than a single photo loads today. But in 1995, that sound meant the future was arriving through your phone line.

Maya: I know that sound from movies! Five minutes just to load one page?

Marcus: And if someone picked up the phone, you got disconnected. But here's the thing: while we were waiting for pages to load, the economy was being rewired. Box one: globalization. NAFTA, the North American Free Trade Agreement, took effect in 1994. It eliminated most tariffs between the US, Canada, and Mexico. The idea: free trade would make everyone richer.

Maya: So NAFTA was basically America handing jobs to Mexico?

Marcus: The bumper-sticker version. The reality's messier. Consumers got cheaper goods. American farmers got new markets in Mexico. But manufacturing workers got hammered. Factories moved to Mexico where labor was cheaper. Ross Perot, running for president in 1992, warned about a "giant sucking sound" of jobs leaving. He wasn't entirely wrong. The Rust Belt kept rusting.

Maya: And the WTO?

Marcus: The World Trade Organization, 1995. It set global trade rules. China wasn't in yet, that came in 2001, but the framework was being built.

Maya: People noticed the framework, right? Weren't there really huge protests?

Marcus: Seattle, 1999. Tens of thousands shut down the WTO meeting. Labor unions, environmentalists, students, the whole coalition. The Battle of Seattle. The losers of globalization made themselves impossible to ignore.

Maya: Was letting China into that system controversial?

Marcus: Hugely. Congress granted China permanent normal trade relations in 2000 after a brutal fight. Supporters said trade would open China up. Critics said it would hollow out American factories. Decades later historians are still arguing about who was right — which tells you the nineties opened globalization's questions without settling them. The nineties were when "globalization" stopped being an academic word and became the air the economy breathed.

Maya: Checking box one. Globalization: NAFTA and the WTO opened trade. Consumers and exporters won. Manufacturing towns lost. The economy grew, but the gains weren't shared.

Marcus: The numbers: the nineties saw the longest economic expansion in American history to that point, 120 months. Unemployment fell below 4 percent. The federal budget actually ran a surplus from 1998 to 2001, the first time since the sixties.

Maya: Wait — the government had extra money?

Marcus: Four straight years of it. A booming economy, higher taxes on top earners, and a smaller military after the Cold War — the peace dividend was real. Both parties instantly started fighting over the extra cash: tax cuts or paying down the debt. The argument never got settled — the surplus didn't survive 2001. But here's the catch the boom hid: real wages for most workers barely moved. The economy grew, but paychecks didn't. Union membership kept falling, from about 20 percent in 1980 to around 13 percent by 2000.

Maya: So the boom was real, but narrow? Like the eighties?

Marcus: Similar pattern, different decade. The stock market soared, especially tech stocks. If you owned stock, you got rich. If you worked for wages, you treaded water. That's the nineties in one sentence.

Maya: Then the tech stocks crashed, right?

Marcus: March 2000. The NASDAQ tripled in two years on companies with no profits — pets.com, Webvan, the lot. Then it lost nearly four-fifths of its value. Trillions gone. Funny part: the internet kept growing anyway. The bubble burst. The revolution didn't.

Maya: Box two: the internet. How did it change everything before we had a name for it?

Marcus: In 1990, almost nobody had email. By 2000, it was essential. Amazon started in 1994 selling books. eBay in 1995. Google in 1998. But the deeper change was how it rewired daily life. News stopped being something you got at 6pm. Shopping stopped requiring stores. The line between work and home started blurring because you could always be reached.

Maya: My mom says the internet was fun, way before it was everything.

Marcus: It really was. In the nineties, the internet was a place you visited. You "went online." By the 2000s, you lived there. The nineties were the transition, and nobody quite realized how big it was. We thought faster communication. It was restructuring commerce, media, and social life.

Maya: AOL CDs in the mail. My mom said they came with everything.

Marcus: Millions of them. Behind "you've got mail" were the browser wars, online shopping, and a kid in Ohio arguing with a stranger in Oslo at midnight.

Maya: And who America became? The demographics?

Marcus: The 1990s saw the biggest immigration wave since the early 1900s. Mostly from Latin America and Asia. By 2000, roughly one in ten Americans was foreign-born. California, Texas, Florida transformed.

Maya: So the map itself was changing?

Marcus: Literally. The old industrial states lost House seats every census while Texas and Florida gained them. Jobs, air conditioning, cheaper land, all of it kept pulling Americans south and west. Political power moved with them. The Sunbelt kept rising — decade by decade, the House map tilted steadily toward the new arrivals. And the culture wars heated up: fights over multiculturalism, bilingual education, gay rights. The nineties felt prosperous, but the country was arguing about what it meant to be American. The culture wars weren't abstract. They were laws. In 1993, "Don't Ask, Don't Tell" let gay Americans serve only if they hid who they were. In 1996, the Defense of Marriage Act wrote that exclusion into federal law. Prosperity didn't settle the argument about who counted as fully American. It just funded both sides of it.

Maya: Checking box two. The internet went from novelty to necessity, rewiring commerce and daily life. Immigration reshaped the demographics. The culture wars started.

Marcus: Box three: the holiday question. After the Cold War, a lot of people thought great-power conflict was over. Fukuyama's "end of history." America intervened in the Balkans — Bosnia, then Kosovo in 1999 — but stood by during Rwanda. Eight hundred thousand people killed in about a hundred days in 1994, and Washington avoided even saying the word "genocide," because the word would have demanded action. The holiday had limits, and they were moral ones. But was the peace real?

Maya: 9/11 says no?

Marcus: 9/11 is the answer, but the warnings were there in the nineties. The 1993 World Trade Center bombing. The embassy bombings in Africa in 1998. Al-Qaeda was already operating. The US just didn't treat it as a top priority. We were focused on the economy.

Maya: And the environment? Where do fossil fuels and Kyoto fit?

Marcus: The Gulf War in 1991: Iraq invaded Kuwait, America led a coalition to push them out. When retreating, Iraqi forces set Kuwait's oil fields on fire. Hundreds of wells burned for months. It was an ecological catastrophe on live TV. Then the Kyoto Protocol in 1997 tried to set global carbon limits. Before the ink was dry, the Senate went 95 to 0 on the Byrd-Hagel resolution, warning Washington not to sign any treaty that let developing countries off the hook. Clinton signed Kyoto in 1998 but never even sent it to the Senate. Middle East conflicts and climate science were forcing a debate about fossil-fuel dependence, but America wasn't ready to change.

Maya: So the holiday was American inattention, not actual peace.

Marcus: That's the historians' verdict. The world kept being dangerous. America just looked away because the economy was good and the Cold War was over.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: globalization via NAFTA and the WTO, the longest expansion on record, but wages flat and unions shrinking. Two: the internet's rise from novelty to — necessity, right? — plus the immigration wave reshaping America.

Marcus: Novelty to necessity. There's the arc. Three: the holiday was an illusion, the Balkans, Rwanda, early terrorism warnings, and the unaddressed climate question prove it.

Maya: Checking all three.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer out loud before I give it. One: who won and who lost under NAFTA? [2-second pause] Consumers got cheaper goods, American farmers got new markets. Manufacturing towns lost factories to Mexico.

[2-second pause] Two: the 'holiday from history' — real? One example. [2-second pause] Not real. The 1998 embassy bombings proved terrorism was growing while America watched the economy.

[2-second pause] Three: a 1999 photo shows protesters shutting down Seattle during the WTO meeting. Point of the source? [2-second pause] Globalization's losers — labor, environmentalists, students — could no longer be ignored.
