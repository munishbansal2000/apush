# U9 — Cram Session: Maya + Jay (Fish Audio)
# Episode 99: Ten Questions, One Unit. ~8 min.
# Draft v1 (from scratch).
# Read note: Cram format — no boxes on the episode sheet. Ten rapid-fire questions, the period thesis, and the DBQ prediction. No pause marks appear in this script — self-test silence is added in production. The polarization beat names real drivers, strictly historical, no punditry. Jay is the student: he retrieves, he guesses, he gets things wrong; Maya corrects and lands the memory hooks. The em dash in the closing tagline is a held beat: leave a full breath between Jay's setup line and Maya's landing line. Do not rush it. Strip this header and the read note before TTS.
# Pronunciation: RAY-gun (Reagan), gor-bah-CHOFF (Gorbachev), glahz-NOHST (glasnost), peh-reh-STROY-kah (perestroika), NAHF-tah (NAFTA), shah-BOV-skee (Schabowski)

Maya: Last time: the presidencies and the polarization, the American argument still going. This time: no new material. Ten questions, the whole unit, rapid-fire. 1980 to the present. Answer all ten out loud and you're ready. About eight minutes. Go.

Jay: The unit where everything is recent enough that my parents remember it. Hit me.

Maya: Question one: Reaganomics in one sentence. And one statistic the critics cite.

Jay: One sentence: slash taxes and regulations, pour money into the military, and let the market rip. Supply-side economics, the idea that money freed up at the top flows down through the whole economy.

Maya: The critics' statistic?

Jay: The national debt roughly tripled. From under a trillion to about two point seven trillion. Tax cuts plus a military buildup, and the red ink exploded.

Maya: Plus the coalition: the religious right, the Sunbelt, and the Reagan Democrats, working-class voters who left the Democratic Party. The roots run back to Goldwater in 1964 and the conservative think tanks. The historians' argument: did Reagan shrink government, or just redirect it? Question one, landed.

Jay: Question two: glasnost versus perestroika. What was each one trying to do?

Maya: Gorbachev takes power in 1985. The Soviet Union's rotting: stagnation, the technology gap, Afghanistan bleeding it dry. He tries two rescues. Your turn.

Jay: Glasnost is the economic one — restructuring? And perestroika is the openness one?

Maya: Swapped. Glasnost means openness: loosen censorship, let people talk, admit the problems out loud. Perestroika means restructuring: reform the economy, make the system work instead of replacing it.

Maya: Rescue attempts. The tragedy: the reforms unleashed the forces that destroyed the whole union. Openness let the nationalist movements organize. Restructuring broke the command economy without building a market one. Gorbachev tried to save the Soviet Union and accidentally ended it. Mix them up and remember this: glasnost opens the mouth, perestroika rebuilds the machine.

Jay: Question three: why did the Berlin Wall fall in 1989? Structural causes, personal causes, or both?

Maya: November ninth, 1989. Walk me through it.

Jay: Both. Structural: the Soviet economy stagnating, the Afghan war bleeding it, the Eastern European satellites going broke, nationalism rising everywhere. Personal: Gorbachev refused to send the tanks, and Reagan spent the eighties pressuring the system. The buildup, the tear-down-this-wall speech.

Maya: The fall itself was an accident. A confused spokesman announced new travel rules, the crowds showed up at the checkpoints, the border guards had no orders, and the Wall just opened. The exam move: causation, long-term structural versus short-term personal. The honest answer is both. Rot made it possible, people made it happen. Then 1991: the failed coup, the flag coming down, the Soviet Union gone.

Jay: Question four: NAFTA. Who supported it, who opposed it, and why?

Maya: 1994, signed by Clinton. The North American Free Trade Agreement — NAFTA. Supporters?

Jay: Business, most economists, consumers. Cheaper goods, bigger markets, American exports to Mexico and Canada. The theory: everybody wins.

Maya: Opponents?

Jay: Labor unions. Factories moving south, wages undercut. Ross Perot and his giant sucking sound of jobs going to Mexico. Environmentalists, warning about a race to the bottom on standards.

Maya: The historians' verdict: consumers and tech won, manufacturing towns lost. The concrete winners and losers of globalization.

Jay: Question five: nine eleven to Afghanistan to Iraq. The one-sentence causal link for each step.

Maya: September eleventh, 2001. Told straight.

Jay: Al Qaeda hijackers hit the Twin Towers, the Pentagon, and a field near Shanksville, Pennsylvania. Afghanistan: the Taliban were sheltering Al Qaeda, so America invaded in October 2001 and toppled them fast. Iraq: 2003. The Bush administration argued Saddam Hussein had weapons of mass destruction. The WMD case collapsed, no stockpiles found, and it became a long occupation.

Maya: At home: the Patriot Act, the Department of Homeland Security. Nine eleven ended the nineties holiday.

Jay: Question six. Explain the two thousand eight crash like I'm fifteen.

Maya: You're sixteen. Close enough.

Jay: Banks gave mortgages to people who couldn't pay them. Subprime mortgages. Then Wall Street bundled thousands of those loans into investments and sold them around the world, so a bad mortgage in Nevada became a German bank's problem. Housing prices fell, the bundles turned toxic, nobody knew who was holding the losses, and the whole financial system froze. Lehman Brothers collapsed in September 2008. For a week it looked like the economy might just stop.

Maya: Plain English, and exactly right. The machine: bubble, bundle, freeze. Remember that triple.

Jay: Question seven: the Troubled Asset Relief Program — TARP. What was it, and why was it controversial?

Maya: October 2008. Talk me through it.

Jay: Congress authorized up to seven hundred billion dollars for the Treasury to buy troubled assets and stabilize the banks. The government bailing out the financial system.

Maya: The controversy?

Jay: It looked like rewarding the arsonists. Banks took insane risks and blew up the economy. They got rescued. Homeowners got foreclosure notices. Later it got used for the auto industry too. Most of the bank money got paid back, but the trust never did.

Maya: The rescue worked as finance and failed as politics. The fallout gave us the Tea Party on the right and Occupy on the left. Compare it with 1929: why no second Depression? The government acted fast and big. Whether it acted fairly — that's the argument.

Jay: Question eight: name two long-term drivers of polarization.

Maya: Strictly historical. No punditry.

Jay: One: media fragmentation. Three networks became cable news became the internet. Everybody picks the reality that agrees with them, and the incentives reward outrage. Two: geographic sorting. People moved into like-minded communities, so you can live your whole life without meeting the other side.

Maya: Two more: inequality and identity. The hard numbers: since about 1979, productivity kept climbing while typical workers' pay barely moved, and union membership collapsed from about a third of private-sector workers in the 1950s to about six percent in recent years. The divide isn't just cultural. It's economic.

Jay: Question nine: how was the nineteen nineties economy different from the two thousands economy?

Maya: The holiday versus the hangover.

Jay: The nineties: the tech boom, the internet going mainstream, globalization with NAFTA and the World Trade Organization, budget surpluses, low unemployment. A holiday from history.

Maya: The two thousands?

Jay: The dot-com bust, nine eleven, then the housing bubble and the crash. Deficits came back, wages stayed flat, manufacturing kept bleeding. The prosperity turned out to be borrowed. Against houses, against the future.

Maya: Continuity and change in one question. The nineties proved globalization could deliver growth. The two thousands proved the bill always comes due.

Jay: Last one. Question ten: Period nine in one thesis sentence. Hit it.

Maya: Your turn first.

Jay: Since 1980, a conservative turn, the Cold War ending, and globalization remade America. While nine eleven, financial crisis, and polarization defined the twenty-first century?

Maya: The period thesis, word for word. The conservative turn remade the economy and the courts. The Cold War's end made America the lone superpower, the unipolar moment. Globalization rewired who won and who lost. Then came the twenty-first century: terror, economic collapse, and polarization. One sentence, the whole unit.

Maya: Lightning round. Three one-line answers. First: the single biggest domestic policy shift of the Reagan era?

Jay: The 1981 tax cut.

Maya: Second: the single image that symbolizes the Cold War's end?

Jay: The Wall coming down on live television, November 1989.

Maya: Third: the single number that explains two thousand eight's politics?

Jay: Ten percent unemployment.

Maya: Tax cuts, the Wall, ten percent. If you remember nothing else, remember those three.

Jay: Prediction beat. Three Document-Based Questions you'd bet on.

Maya: One: evaluate the extent to which the Reagan presidency changed American politics. Two: a nine-eleven-and-aftermath prompt. How the attacks reshaped government power, foreign policy, and daily life. Three: evaluate the government's response to the financial crisis. The thesis: Washington prevented a second Depression by spending, cutting rates, and regulating, but the rescue's unfairness fueled populist backlash.

Jay: The answers are sitting in these ten questions. The debt statistic, the Wall's accident, the TARP fight.

Maya: Ten questions, landed. One more beat: the through-line. Every question tonight was really the same question: what happens when the old order breaks? Reagan broke the New Deal order. Gorbachev broke the Soviet order.

Jay: Then the Wall broke the Cold War order, nine eleven broke the holiday, and two thousand eight broke the trust. See why the thesis works? Conservative turn, Cold War's end, globalization, then the shocks. It's all one arc.

Maya: Period nine is the story of orders breaking with nothing stable replacing them yet. Next time: the whole story, part one. Fourteen ninety-one to eighteen seventy-seven, one arc, full speed.

Jay: The conservative turn, the Cold War's end —

Maya: And the argument that never ends.
