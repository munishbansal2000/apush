Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: the captains-or-robbers verdict. But the workers in the mills had their own verdict, and nobody put it to a vote. These are the people the profits were built on: dangerous work, endless days, pay that vanished every time the economy hiccuped. Four boxes: one, why workers organized at all. Two, the two union models, the Knights of Labor versus the AFL. Three, the three strike dramas: Haymarket, Homestead, Pullman. Four, why labor kept losing. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. About twelve minutes.

Marcus: Start on the shop floor, because that's where every union on this episode sheet was born. Before the war, a craftsman set his own pace, chose his tools, trained for years. After the war, the plant sets the pace for you. Efficiency experts timed every motion, redesigned every workstation, and the skilled work got chopped into tasks a newcomer could learn in a day.

Maya: So the job stopped being yours. You went from making steel to feeding the machine that made steel.

Marcus: That's the word the books use: de-skilling. And the losses ran deeper than pride. When your task needs no training, you become replaceable, and the workplace got more dangerous at the same time. Low pay for long days, and pay that fell off a cliff whenever the economy did: the Panics of 1873 and 1893 meant announced wage cuts, take it or starve. By 1900, nearly one industrial worker in five was a child between ten and fifteen.

Maya: [incredulous] One in five were kids? What were their parents making?

Marcus: An unskilled man might pull eight dollars a week. A skilled woman made five. And the families needed the children's wages because the men's wages wouldn't stretch. So picture the loop: cheap hands, dangerous plants, pay the boss could cut any bad year. Box one, and the fuel for everything else.

Maya: [sheepish] So box one is just the wages. Too little, too long. Checking that one?

Marcus: The wages were real. The deeper grievance was losing control of the work itself.

Maya: [warm tone] I did one twelve-hour volunteer shift in high school, restocking a food bank, and I fell asleep standing at a sink. One shift. These people did it for years.

Marcus: Now watch two different answers to that misery get built. The first is the Knights of Labor, founded 1869. Their bet: take everybody. Skilled, unskilled, women, Black workers, immigrants — one big union, and a broad reform agenda on top of wages: safety rules, an end to child labor, the dream of a more cooperative economy. By the mid-1880s they claimed something like three-quarters of a million members.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Everybody in, plus reform the whole society. Ambitious. What's the catch?

Marcus: They hated strikes. The leadership was reluctant to call them, preferring the patient route. And their size was brittle: so many languages, so many trades, and a top-down leadership under a man named Terence Powderly that kept a tight grip and blocked new leaders from rising. Big tent, thin walls.

Maya: And the second answer is the AFL, 1886. Skilled workers only.

Marcus: Skilled only. The AFL was a federation of craft unions: carpenters with carpenters, cigar makers with cigar makers, each trade guarding its own skill. Its leader was Samuel Gompers, himself a cigar maker. The AFL left the big social program aside and fought for bread and butter: higher wages, shorter hours, better conditions. And unlike the Knights, the AFL was perfectly willing to walk out.

Maya: Your turn. The Knights welcome everyone and avoid strikes. The AFL takes only skilled men and will shut a plant down. Which model actually wins in the 1890s, and why? Think it through.
[10-second pause]

Marcus: [firm] The AFL. The logic is leverage: a skilled craftsman is hard to replace, so when his union strikes, the boss bleeds. An unskilled worker can be swapped for a strikebreaker by morning, so a strike of the replaceable collapses fast. Being numerous meant nothing next to being irreplaceable.

Maya: So the real difference comes down to who could make a strike stick.

Maya: [sheepish] So the AFL was the inclusive one. Open to everybody.

Marcus: Flip it. The AFL was the exclusive one, on purpose. Skilled men only. The Knights were the big tent, and the big tent was about to get blamed for a bomb.

Marcus: [dramatic] Chicago, spring 1886. Workers at the McCormick Reaper Works are on strike, and the company hands their jobs to strikebreakers. On May 3rd, police and company guards open fire on the strikers. The next evening, a rally gathers at Haymarket Square, peaceful at first, called to support the eight-hour day and protest the shooting.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And somebody throws a bomb.

Marcus: [serious tone] Into the police ranks. The blast kills several officers, the police fire into the crowd, and civilians die too. Then the trials: eight anarchists convicted on what even the books call scanty evidence. Four were hanged. The story that stuck: eight anarchists stood trial, but the public read the bomb as the labor movement's.

Maya: [sheepish] So the Knights get the blame even though they weren't the bombers.

Marcus: Blamed for radicalism they had no control over, and the membership bled out through the 1890s. After Haymarket, "union" sounded like "bomb" to a lot of Americans, and that fear became labor's permanent headwind. Keep that fear in your pocket. It decides the next two stories.

Maya: Two boxes down.

Marcus: [dramatic] Summer 1892. Homestead, Pennsylvania. Carnegie's steelworks, and the contract of the Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers, a craft union under the AFL's wing, has just expired. Carnegie wants it dead. He's conveniently out of the country, and he leaves the plant to Henry Clay Frick, a man with no love for unions. Frick builds a fence around the works, locks the men out, hires strikebreakers, and brings in a small private army of Pinkerton guards.

Maya: [incredulous] A private army? Like an invasion?

Marcus: [dramatic] The workers were waiting on the riverbank when the guards tried to land. Shots, then a full day of fighting, and the Pinkertons surrendered. The workers took their own plant back. It looks like the biggest labor victory of the century.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And then the federal troops crushed them, right? Like 1877?

Marcus: [firm] That's the trap, and it's the one the exam sets every year. Homestead belonged to Pennsylvania's own state militia: the governor called out about eight thousand National Guardsmen, they retook the plant, and Frick reopened it with non-union men. Federal troops were 1877, and they'll be Pullman.

Maya: [sheepish] Homestead belonged to the state militia. Writing that on my hand.

Marcus: Write it big. Washington sat that one out.

Marcus: [ominous] Two years later, 1894. The Panic of 1893 has gutted the economy, and George Pullman's railroad-car company cuts wages, again and again. But Pullman, Illinois, is a company town: Pullman built it in 1880, owns every house, and the rent comes straight out of the paycheck. Wages fall. Rents don't.

Maya: [playful] There's a song about this. "Sixteen Tons," the coal miner one: I owe my soul to the company store. Pullman was the company store with streets.

Marcus: It really was. Three organizers get fired, about three thousand workers walk out, and they call in the American Railway Union and its leader, Eugene Debs. Debs skipped the Pullman plant and had his members refuse to handle any train carrying a Pullman car. The country's railroads seize up.

Maya: A boycott that paralyzed the whole rail system. You can't get more leverage than that.

Marcus: And the response was enormous too. The courts issued injunctions against the strike, twice, and here's the bitter detail: the injunctions leaned on the Sherman Antitrust Act, the law written to break up trusts. The government aimed antitrust law at the workers. Then President Cleveland sent in federal troops. About two dozen strikers were killed, the strike was crushed, and the Supreme Court blessed the whole thing the next year in In re Debs.

Maya: So Debs watches the army break his union and decides the system can't be fixed from inside?

Marcus: The suppression of Pullman turned Debs into a socialist, and he ran for president on that ticket more than once. Real exam tip, on the error students actually make: if a question asks how Washington used antitrust law in the 1890s, point it at the unions. The Sherman Act's first real victims were strikers.

Marcus: Now stack the three stories and the pattern answers box four: why labor kept losing. The courts made strikes illegal on paper before a shot was fired, first with injunctions and then with In re Debs blessing the government. The troops, state or federal, always stood ready for the owners. Haymarket's fear turned the public against every strike as radicalism, and the juries and newspapers followed. And there was always a line of desperate men ready to take the jobs, with Pinkertons hired to protect them.

Maya: And the bosses mostly just waited out the bad years? Pay cuts in the panics, then crush the strike?

Marcus: That was the playbook, and it worked for forty years. One line forward, since the contrast matters: the Wagner Act of 1935 finally gave workers real legal protection for the right to organize. But that's a different era. For this one, labor lost every big fight.

Maya: Four boxes, let's land them. One: why workers organized. Dangerous de-skilled work, long days, low pay, and pay the bosses cut every panic. The money mattered, but losing control of the work itself was the deeper grievance.

Marcus: Two: the two union models. The Knights of Labor, founded 1869, took everybody and dreamed of reform, but avoided strikes and got blamed for Haymarket. The AFL, 1886, took only skilled craftsmen under Gompers, wanted bread and butter, and would walk out.

Maya: Three: the three dramas. Haymarket, 1886, the bomb that turned the public against labor. Homestead, 1892, the workers beat the Pinkertons and lost to the, uh, state militia? Pennsylvania's troops?

Marcus: [reasonable] Right. State militia at Homestead. Federal troops are 1877 and Pullman.

Maya: [confident tone] Pullman, 1894, the boycott that stopped the railroads, broken by injunctions and federal troops, with antitrust law aimed at the workers. And four: why labor kept losing. Courts, troops, public fear after Haymarket, and strikebreakers. All four checked.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it.

Maya: Question one: which union model was better built to win strikes in the 1890s, the Knights' industrial model or the AFL's craft model? Defend your pick.
[10-second pause]

Maya: The AFL's craft model. What the record shows is that skilled craftsmen were hard to replace, so their strikes actually hurt the boss, while the Knights' unskilled members could be swapped for strikebreakers overnight — and the Knights avoided strikes anyway. Which means being hard to replace is what made a strike hurt. Numbers alone couldn't do it.

Maya: Question two: an SAQ hands you three facts. In 1877, federal troops broke the railroad strike. In 1892, state troops broke the Homestead strike. In 1894, federal troops and court injunctions broke the Pullman strike. What's the one-sentence pattern?
[10-second pause]

Maya: The government, federal or state, consistently sided with management, and each defeat added a new weapon: troops first, then injunctions. The pattern is the state enforcing capital's side, louder each time.

Maya: Question three: why did Haymarket damage the labor movement more than the bomb itself did?
[10-second pause]

Maya: Because the convictions made the public link every union to radicalism. What the record shows is that eight anarchists were convicted on scanty evidence, and the Knights — who had nothing to do with the bomb — bled members through the 1890s. Which means the real wound was political. Once Americans feared unions as violent and un-American, courts, juries, and newspapers all tilted against labor, and that tilt decided Homestead and Pullman before they started.

Maya: One more, fast. Beyond low wages, name one more reason workers organized.
[10-second pause]

Maya: De-skilling: losing control of the work itself.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] They lost every battle — and taught the country the price of winning. Next time: immigration and nativism, the new faces in the mills and the backlash they met. Check your boxes.
