Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: the frontier line vanished from the map, and America went looking for a new one overseas. This time: yellow papers and a battleship at the bottom of Havana harbor. The war lasted one summer. The argument about empire never ended. Four boxes: one, why America looked outward. Two, the road to war. Three, the splendid little war. Four, the debate: did 1898 make America an empire? Arguing yes, for the expansionists: Marcus. Arguing no, from the Filipino side: Aguinaldo. Note: the debate is dramatized from the real arguments; the quoted lines are the real words. Circle the ones you couldn't explain. About fourteen minutes.

Marcus: [measured] Start with the psychology. Turner asked: if the frontier made us, who are we now? A senator named Albert Beveridge answered: open a new frontier overseas, recapture the pioneer spirit. The closed frontier didn't end the hunger. It pointed it at the ocean.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So it's not just greed. It's identity?

Marcus: [conversational] It's both, plus fear. The panic of 1893 left factories humming and nobody buying. Businessmen started worrying they'd need customers overseas: places to sell the surplus, raw materials like rubber they couldn't get at home. The same industrial machine from our big-business lesson needed the world.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And the navy had a theory for that.

Marcus: [measured] Captain Alfred Thayer Mahan, 1890: The Influence of Sea Power upon History. His argument in one line: great powers need great navies, and navies need coaling stations and markets to protect. Naval officers worldwide studied him; Roosevelt devoured him. The exam's one-liner: Mahan turned sea power into national destiny.

Maya: [playful] I get seasick on a ferry. Full respect to anyone who crossed an ocean on coal and faith.

Marcus: [conversational] Then the missionaries, who saw empire as a chance to convert the world. Ministers like Josiah Strong gave it theology: in 1885 he wrote that God had made the Anglo-Saxons their "brother's keepers." Add Social Darwinism and Kipling's 1899 poem urging America to take up the "White Man's Burden." Markets, the navy, God, all pushing the same direction.

Maya: [thoughtful tone] So when a question asks why America turned outward, the answer isn't one thing. It's the stack.

Marcus: [confident tone] Stack all four: the panic, Mahan, the missionaries, Beveridge's new frontier. The exam rewards the stack. A single cause is a wrong answer wearing a confident face.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And Hawaii was the rehearsal?

Marcus: [conversational] Hawaii was the rehearsal. American sugar planters took over the economy and overthrew the queen in 1893, then asked Washington to annex them. Cleveland refused; McKinley agreed, and Congress annexed Hawaii in 1898 for Pearl Harbor, the Pacific naval base.

Marcus: [serious tone] Then Cuba. Ninety miles off Florida, and in 1895 it exploded. Falling sugar prices triggered a revolt against Spain. Spain answered with 150,000 troops and reconcentration: rounding civilians into camps to starve the rebels of support. The camps bred disease. Two hundred and twenty-five thousand people died.

Maya: [serious tone] [speaking slowly] Two hundred and twenty-five thousand. Not a war. A catastrophe.

Marcus: [conversational] And two New York newspapers turned it into a crusade. Pulitzer's World and Hearst's Journal competed for readers with sensationalism: lurid, often fanciful accounts of Spanish cruelty, reprinted across the country. Yellow journalism. News designed to sell papers first and be true second.

Maya: [incredulous] Okay, why "yellow"?

Marcus: [conversational] A comic strip. The Yellow Kid, a kid in a yellow nightshirt, a wildly popular cartoon of the nineties. Hearst poached Pulitzer's artist; Pulitzer hired a replacement. Two yellow kids, two rival papers, and the name stuck. [chuckle] The term for fake news was born in a fight over a cartoon.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Did the papers actually push us into war?

Marcus: [measured] They pushed the public, though McKinley didn't want war. He'd fought in the Civil War and knew the cost. Then a Spanish diplomat's stolen letter called him "weak." On February 15, 1898, the battleship Maine exploded in Havana harbor. The yellow press blamed Spain with no evidence, and the slogan wrote itself: "Remember the Maine!"

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So Spain blew it up.

Marcus: [firm] Not exactly. The distinction is the whole lesson. Nobody's fully sure what sank the Maine. The likely cause was a boiler failure, and the naval investigation never conclusively explained it. The papers declared Spain guilty because guilty sold papers. Don't write "Spain sank the Maine" as fact. Write that America believed it, and belief was enough.

Maya: [sheepish] I would've written precisely that.

Marcus: [conversational] Most students do. Which is why it's a trap. Congress declared war in April, and into the war resolution went the Teller Amendment: the United States had no intention of annexing Cuba. Write that down. Teller is the promise. It matters when the promise bends.

Maya: [conversational] Two down: the outward turn, and the road to war. Now the war itself.

Marcus: [driving] It started before Congress even voted. Roosevelt was assistant secretary of the navy, and the secretary was out for the day. On February 25 he cabled the Pacific squadron: be ready to hit the Spanish in the Philippines. When war came, Commodore Dewey was ready. On May 1 he sailed into Manila Bay and destroyed the Spanish fleet in a morning.

Maya: [incredulous] Wait. The Philippines? The war was about Cuba.

Marcus: [confident tone] Cuba was the cause. The Philippines were the surprise. The war was fought over Cuba; the empire was the aftershock. If a question asks what started the war, the answer is Cuba and the Maine. Never the Philippines.

Marcus: [energetic] June: American troops land in Cuba. July 1: the heights outside Santiago, Roosevelt's Rough Riders up San Juan Hill. July 3: the Spanish fleet makes a run for it and gets annihilated. August: Spain asks for terms. Four months, start to finish.

Maya: [conversational] And that's the "splendid little war."

Marcus: [measured] John Hay's words — he was the American ambassador in London: "It has been a splendid little war." Fewer than four hundred Americans died in combat. About two thousand five hundred total, most of them to disease.

Marcus: [serious tone] Splendid for us. Then the bill came due. The Treaty of Paris, signed that December. Spain lets go of Cuba. America takes the Philippines, Guam, Puerto Rico. Twenty million dollars, and nobody asked the Filipinos.

Maya: Your turn. You're McKinley, winter of 1898. The treaty's on your desk. The Philippines: eight thousand miles of ocean away, millions of people who declared their own independence back in June. Sign it, or walk away? Think like it's 1898. [8-second pause]

Marcus: [firm] He signed. McKinley's reported reasoning: the Filipinos were "unfit for self-government." Left alone, they'd fall into civil war and some other empire would swallow them. [beat] And the Senate ratified the treaty by [emphasis] a single vote. One vote, and America owned an empire in the Pacific.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] So the question. Did America do right? Arguing the expansionist case: Marcus. Arguing the Filipino case, from 1899: Aguinaldo. Round one: the case for empire.

Marcus: [passionate] Look at the ledger. The panic of 1893 proved America needed markets beyond its borders. Mahan proved a great power needs bases and a navy to protect them. Beveridge was right: the frontier spirit needed a new frontier. And the Philippines were the stepping stone to China. Walk away, and Germany or Japan takes those islands within the decade. America didn't invent the scramble. It joined it, or got left behind.

Aguinaldo: [intense] You speak of ledgers. We were not on yours. We fought Spain for two years before Dewey's ships arrived. We stood beside your admiral at Manila. On [speaking slowly] the twelfth of June, 1898, we declared our independence and raised our own flag. Then Spain sold us to you for twenty million dollars, and no one asked the Filipinos. Tell me, Marcus: what would your revolution have said to that?

Marcus: [passionate] It would have said: look at the alternative. McKinley believed you were unfit for self-government. His word, not mine. Abandonment meant civil war and a German or Japanese flag over Manila. The missionaries saw souls to save. Kipling saw a burden to carry. The navy saw the base that would open China. Every one of those motives is on the record.

Aguinaldo: [intense] Your motives are on the record. So are ours. Your own countrymen saw it. The Anti-Imperialist League: Mark Twain, Andrew Carnegie, William Jennings Bryan. They called this the Revolution in reverse. You threw off a king across an ocean. Now you're the king across the ocean. Where's the pioneer spirit in that?

Maya: [conversational] Round two: the war. Aguinaldo.

Aguinaldo: [passionate] February 1899. The shooting started between your soldiers and mine, and it hasn't stopped. You told the world this war was about freeing Cuba. The Teller Amendment, your own Congress: no annexation. Then you kept us. Promises, Marcus. That's what empires make instead of treaties.

Marcus: [measured] The book is honest about the tactics: the army used the skills it learned fighting Native Americans. I won't dress it up. Three years of it: more than four thousand American dead, as many as two hundred thousand Filipino. But the same occupation built schools and set up local self-government, town by town. And the threat was real. Those islands were going to belong to somebody. America chose to be the somebody that built schools instead of the somebody that built plantations. On the Teller charge, the record's against me. Congress swore off annexing Cuba, then wrote the Platt Amendment into Cuba's constitution in 1901 and kept the right to march back in. It never annexed the island. It never really left.

Aguinaldo: [intense] Schools don't vote. A government that must be taught to want you is a government that doesn't want you. Your schools teach children in a republic you refuse to recognize.

Maya: [thoughtful tone] The record at home backs him up, too. Bryan ran for president in 1900 on stopping all of this, and lost. The voters heard the anti-imperialist case and chose the empire.

Maya: [conversational] Closing statements. Rebut, don't repeat. Marcus, you first.

Marcus: [intense] The charge is betrayal, and on Cuba the record convicts. Teller promised no annexation; Platt wrote America's right of return into Cuba's constitution. The Philippines are the harder case for my side: no one asked the Filipinos, and twenty million dollars changed hands. But what Washington bought was never run for extraction — no settler colony, no profits shipped home. It built classrooms and town councils, betting self-government would grow into them. Aguinaldo lived the war first and the schools second, and I can't argue him out of that order. So the question was never whether the record is clean. It's whether a republic can keep an empire and stay one. Answer that, and you've got the essay.

Aguinaldo: [passionate] The schools don't answer the charge. We declared our independence in 1898, Marcus — no one needed to give us what we'd already taken. Your courts answer to your flag, not ours. You ask whether 1898 made you an empire. Ask the people you bought. We felt the answer.

Maya: [thoughtful tone] The exam's verdict, both-sided by design. 1898 made America a colonial power: the Philippines, Guam, Puerto Rico, bought and kept, the Philippines until 1946; Cuba freed but leashed by the Platt Amendment, with American troops back three times in twenty years. In 1901 the Supreme Court agreed the Constitution didn't have to follow the flag. Necessity or hypocrisy, that's the essay. Run both sides, then judge.

Maya: [conversational] Four boxes, let's land them. One: the outward turn. The 1893 panic and the hunt for markets, Mahan's sea power, the missionaries and Strong and Kipling, Social Darwinism, Beveridge's new frontier. Hawaii, 1898, Pearl Harbor in the bargain.

Marcus: [conversational] Two: the road to war. Cuba's 1895 revolt, Spain's reconcentration camps, the yellow press and the Yellow Kid. The Maine, February 1898, cause never proven. And the Teller Amendment's promise: no annexation of Cuba.

Maya: [conversational] Three: the splendid little war. Dewey at Manila Bay, May 1. San Juan Hill, July 1. Hay's phrase: "a splendid little war." The Treaty of Paris, December 1898. Cuba free; the Philippines, Guam, Puerto Rico bought for twenty million.

Maya: [conversational] Four: the debate. The Anti-Imperialist League: Twain, Carnegie, Bryan. The Philippines taken over Filipino objections, the three-year war, the Teller Amendment leashing Cuba. Wait, no. Platt. Platt's the leash. Teller's the promise we wouldn't annex. Platt's the one that let us march back in. Checking that one.

Marcus: [conversational] Checked — Teller's the promise, Platt's the leash.

Marcus: [conversational] And the verdict the exam wants: 1898 made America a colonial power. Whether it should have is the argument, and you now own both sides.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it.

Maya: Question one, a stimulus. A New York paper screams "Remember the Maine!" A student writes: "This proves Spain sank the Maine." What's the error, and what does this source actually show? [17-second pause]

Maya: The error is the cause. Nobody ever proved what sank the Maine. The likely culprit was a boiler failure. What the source shows isn't Spanish guilt. It's the yellow press at work, manufacturing the anger that made war politically unavoidable. The question isn't what it claims. It's what it did.

Maya: Question two. Congress touched Cuba twice: once in 1898, once in 1901. One said America would never annex the island. The other said America could intervene whenever it wanted. Name them, in order. [17-second pause]

Maya: 1898: the Teller Amendment, in the war resolution. No annexation of Cuba. A promise. 1901: the Platt Amendment, forced into Cuba's constitution. No treaties without American approval, intervention "when necessary," and the Guantanamo base. The promise, then the loophole. Keep them straight and the SAQ is yours.

Maya: Question three. The "splendid little war" cost fewer than four hundred American combat deaths. The Philippine-American War cost more than four thousand American lives, and as many as two hundred thousand Filipino ones. A student says the second war is the real imperialism story. Agree, and say why in one line. [15-second pause]

Maya: Agree. The Spanish-American War won the empire in a summer. The Philippine-American War showed what keeping it cost. The first war made America a colonial power. The second proved it.

Maya: One more, fast. Mahan, 1890, one line. [5-second pause]

Maya: Sea power makes great powers: build the navy, grab the coaling stations, protect the markets. The book that turned battleships into destiny.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Four months of war. Three years of fighting. And an argument about empire that's still going. Check your four boxes. Next time: the cram, the whole Gilded Age in one sitting. And after that, Unit 6: the Progressives, who looked at everything we just built and decided to fix it.
