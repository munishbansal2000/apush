# APUSH Unit 9, Episode 1: The Reagan Revolution
# Script v4 DRAFT — Debate (Maya moderates, Marcus + Ellis)
# Read note: Maya moderates a debate. Marcus makes the renewal case. Dr. Ellis, a historian of the American economy, makes the critics' case. Maya tracks three boxes on her episode sheet: Reaganomics, the Reagan coalition, and the paradox — and checks each off as the episode earns it. [5-second pause] tags are production notes for real silence in the self-test; they never go to the voice. The em dash in Marcus's closing setup line is a held breath — do not rush it. Strip this header and the read note before TTS.
# Pronunciation: Laffer (LAFF-er), Sunbelt (SUN-belt), stagflation (stag-FLAY-shun), Mondale (mon-DAYL)

Maya: Last time: the long 1970s, a country that felt like it was coming apart. This time: 1980. A former actor walks on stage promising morning in America. Renewal or wrong turn? Three boxes on your sheet. One: Reaganomics, what it was supposed to do. Two: the Reagan coalition, who switched sides and why. Three: the paradox, did he shrink government or just redirect it? Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes. Let's argue.

Maya: The setup: Marcus makes the renewal case. Dr. Ellis, a historian of the American economy, makes the critics' case. I moderate and I keep score. Marcus, open.

Marcus: The renewal case. 1980: inflation over thirteen percent, interest rates near twenty percent. Reagan says get government off people's backs, cut taxes, let the economy breathe. By 1984, inflation near four percent, growth at seven percent. Numbers, not spin.

Ellis: The critics' case. The numbers he's skipping: the debt tripled, from nine hundred billion to two point seven trillion dollars. The tax cuts went overwhelmingly to the top: the top rate dropped from seventy percent to twenty-eight. And the rich-poor gap exploded. The ledger, not the commercial.

Maya: Box one: Reaganomics. The theory, not the slogan. Marcus, mechanics.

Marcus: Supply-side economics. Arthur Laffer's napkin curve says cut taxes on businesses and investors, and they'll invest more. The economy grows, and the growth generates enough new revenue to offset the cuts. Lower rates, bigger pie. Reagan cut the top rate from seventy to fifty percent in 1981, then to twenty-eight in 1986. He deregulated airlines, trucking, banking, the airwaves.

Ellis: The theory assumed growth would pay for the cuts. It didn't. The deficit exploded: tax cuts plus the military buildup, revenues lagging. And deregulation? The savings and loan crisis. Over a thousand failed, costing taxpayers about a hundred sixty billion dollars.

Maya: I thought deregulation helped regular people. Cheaper flights, more choices?

Marcus: Some sectors, yes — airline deregulation made flying cheaper, no question.

Maya: Which Carter signed, 1978. My dad flies for work and never shuts up about cheap tickets.

Marcus: Right, Carter signed it, Reagan took the momentum. But Ellis is right about the savings and loans. Remove rules without watching what fills the vacuum, and people gamble with other people's money.

Ellis: They took federally insured deposits and bet them on junk bonds and bad real estate. When it collapsed, taxpayers paid. Privatize the gains, socialize the losses.

Maya: The "morning in America" ad, the picket fences. Was the prosperity real?

Marcus: For a lot of people. Unemployment fell from nearly eleven percent in 1982 to around five by 1989. The stock market tripled. If you owned assets, the eighties were fantastic.

Ellis: If you owned assets. Real wages for most workers barely budged. The minimum wage sat at three thirty-five the entire decade. The prosperity was real, but narrow.

Maya: Checking box one. Reaganomics: cut taxes to spur investment, deregulate to unleash business. Growth and the inflation drop on one side, the tripled debt and wider inequality on the other. Both sides use real numbers.

Maya: Box two: the coalition. But it didn't start in 1980. Where did this movement come from?

Marcus: Barry Goldwater, 1964. He lost in a landslide, but he put the ideas on the national stage: states' rights, low taxes, anti-communism, suspicion of Washington. Then the pieces assembled. The religious right, Falwell's Moral Majority in 1979, gave it foot soldiers. The Sunbelt, booming while the Rust Belt shrank, gave it the map. The think tanks, like the Heritage Foundation founded in 1973, gave it the policy papers.

Ellis: And the voters. Reagan Democrats: white working-class voters, especially in the South and Midwest, Democratic since the New Deal. Three reasons they switched. The economy was terrible under Carter. Reagan talked values, faith, family, patriotism. And race was in the mix, whether people said it out loud or not.

Maya: So the Reagan Democrats were basically just the racists switching teams?

Ellis: Too simple, and it lets everyone else off the hook. Plenty switched over the economy and the hostage crisis and the gas lines. But race was doing quiet work underneath. You can't tell the story without it, and you can't reduce the story to it.

Marcus: The coded version is the one historians fight about. August 1980: Reagan opens his campaign at the Neshoba County Fair in Philadelphia, Mississippi, miles from where three civil rights workers were murdered in 1964. He says, "I believe in states' rights." Plenty of historians read that as a signal. His defenders say he meant devolving federal power, which he'd preached for years.

Maya: The welfare queen story, wasn't that coded?

Marcus: Reagan talked about a Chicago woman supposedly collecting benefits under dozens of names. Exaggerated. But the image, a Black woman gaming the system, did political work. It let him attack the safety net without mentioning race.

Ellis: Morning in America didn't dawn for everyone. Black unemployment stayed roughly double white unemployment. Reagan didn't say the word AIDS publicly until 1985, by which time thousands had died. Homelessness exploded as federal mental-health support shrank and housing costs climbed.

Maya: So the coalition: economic conservatives, social conservatives, Cold Warriors, plus the Reagan Democrats.

Marcus: Business plus evangelicals plus hawks. That coalition dominated Republican politics for forty years. 1984 proved it: forty-nine states. Only Minnesota and DC went for Mondale, who'd promised, straight out, to raise taxes. Voters punished the honesty.

Ellis: Forty-nine states is real. But turnout barely topped fifty-three percent. Wide, not deep. Plenty of those Reagan Democrats went back to voting Democratic for Congress.

Maya: Did Reagan win the Cold War?

Marcus: The short renewal case: evil empire, the military buildup, Brandenburg Gate in 1987, tear down this wall. Two years later the wall fell. Supporters say the pressure worked.

Ellis: The short critics' case: the Soviet Union was rotting from inside. Stagnant economy, Afghanistan, the nationalities wanting out. Reagan happened to be president when the rot became collapse. Full fight next episode.

Maya: Box three: the paradox. Did he shrink government?

Ellis: Federal spending barely moved as a share of GDP. About twenty-two percent when he arrived, about twenty-one when he left. He didn't shrink it. He redirected it from domestic programs to the military. Defense spending jumped roughly forty percent in real terms.

Maya: So "government is the problem," but he just moved the money around?

Marcus: The critics' strongest point here, and it's fair. Reagan grew the parts he liked and shrank the parts he didn't. The Pentagon got bigger; housing, education, the EPA, the Environmental Protection Agency, got smaller, its budget cut by roughly a quarter. Not small versus big government. Which government.

Ellis: And the debt constrains everything after. Every president since has dealt with the structural deficit he created. By 1989, interest payments alone cost more than most domestic programs combined — the long shadow.

Marcus: My closing verdict: he fixed stagflation — prices soaring while the economy stalled — and restored growth, and made the country feel good about itself again. You can't put that in a table, but it mattered.

Ellis: My closing verdict: he bought prosperity on credit and left the bill for the future. The morning didn't dawn for everyone. The debate never ended because the consequences never ended.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: Reaganomics. Supply-side theory, the top rate from seventy to fifty, then twenty-eight. Wait, fifty first, then twenty-eight, right? Tamed inflation, grew the economy, tripled the debt, widened inequality. Two: the Reagan coalition. Goldwater's ideas, the religious right, the Sunbelt, the think tanks, plus Reagan Democrats who left over the economy, values, and race. Three: the paradox. He didn't shrink government, he redirected it toward defense and away from domestic spending. My verdict: the long shadow is the deficit, the inequality, and the coalition that outlived him. The New Deal said government is the answer. Reagan said government is the problem. Two turning points, opposite directions. Checking all three.

Maya: Two questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One: a historian argues Reagan's policies favored the wealthy over the middle class. Which evidence best supports that claim? [5-second pause] The top rate fell from seventy to twenty-eight percent while real wages stagnated and the debt tripled. The gains concentrated at the top.

Maya: Two: evaluate the extent to which Reagan changed American politics. One sentence, thesis style. [5-second pause] Reagan forged a durable coalition of business conservatives, evangelicals, hawks, and defecting Democrats that reshaped the parties for a generation, though critics argue the economic and social costs undercut the achievement.

Maya: Check your three boxes. The argument about Reagan is still going — that's how you know it mattered. Episode 1 in the books. Next time: the Cold War ends, and the fight over who ended it gets even louder.

Marcus: Morning in America —

Maya: but the bill came due.
