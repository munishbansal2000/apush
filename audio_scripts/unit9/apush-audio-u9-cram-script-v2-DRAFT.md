Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: the war lasted one summer, and the argument about empire never ended. This time: no new material. Six lessons, the whole Gilded Age, in about twelve minutes. Six boxes: integration, labor's defeats, the Exclusion Act, the Omaha Platform, Turner, Teller versus Platt. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now.

Maya: [energetic] One. Carnegie: vertical or horizontal? Rockefeller? [2-second pause]

Jay: [casual] Carnegie went vertical, owned everything. Rockefeller went horizontal, bought everyone.

Maya: [conversational] Clean. The trust was Rockefeller's invention, 1882. Then the Sherman Act outlawed trusts in 1890, and —

Jay: [casual] And Standard Oil got broken up.

Maya: [firm] Mix-up, and the famous one. The Sherman Act changed almost nothing at first: the courts ruled manufacturing wasn't interstate commerce, and Standard Oil became a holding company. Its first victims were strikers, not trusts. Hold that for box two.

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] So why did the whole explosion happen when it did? Why not thirty years earlier?

Maya: [measured] The loop. Railroads needed steel rails; Bessemer's 1856 process made steel cheap; cheap steel built more railroads. Then Drake's 1859 oil well, Edison's 1879 bulb (sold, not invented), and a government that stood back: laissez-faire, land grants, tariffs.

Maya: [energetic] Two. The Knights took everybody and hated strikes. The AFL took skilled men only and would walk out. Which model wins a strike in the 1890s? [2-second pause]

Jay: [casual] The big tent? More members, more muscle?

Maya: [confident tone] Numbers lose. Leverage wins. A skilled craftsman is hard to replace, so his strike hurts; an unskilled worker gets swapped by morning. The AFL's exclusivity was the whole strategy.

Jay: [sheepish] So the AFL was the inclusive one — wait, no. I just said the Knights took everybody.

Maya: [conversational] Not quite, and you caught it yourself. The AFL was the exclusive one, on purpose. The Knights were the big tent, and the big tent got blamed for a bomb. Haymarket, May 4, 1886.

Maya: [serious tone] The Knights weren't the bombers: eight anarchists convicted on scanty evidence, four hanged. After that, "union" sounded like "bomb" to half the country, and that fear decided the next two stories.

Maya: [dramatic] Summer 1892. Homestead. Carnegie's steelworks, Frick in charge, the Pinkertons by barge at dawn, the workers beat them back and took the plant. And then the federal troops marched in and crushed the strike.

Jay: [firm] Not quite. Federal troops were the 1877 strike and Pullman. Homestead was Pennsylvania's state militia, about eight thousand Guardsmen.

Maya: [sheepish] State militia. I set the trap and walked straight into it.

Maya: [confident tone] Pullman, 1894: Debs's boycott stopped the railroads, and Washington broke it with injunctions and federal troops. The detail that stings: the injunctions leaned on the Sherman Act, written to break up trusts. In re Debs, 1895, blessed it.

Maya: [energetic] Your turn. It's 1892, and your union is skilled cigar makers nobody can replace. Strike or no strike, and why? [8-second pause]

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] Strike. The boss can't swap them by morning.

Maya: [confident tone] Scarcity beats headcount.

Maya: [energetic] Three. The old wave was Irish and German. The new wave, from the 1880s: who? [2-second pause]

Jay: [conversational] Italian, Russian, Polish. Catholic and Jewish, mostly. About twenty-eight million between 1870 and 1920.

Maya: [conversational] And the front door opened when?

Jay: [casual] Ellis Island. 1890.

Maya: [firm] Not quite. And here's the one that catches everybody. January 1, 1892. And Angel Island, 1910, wasn't a front door at all. Ellis processed. Angel detained.

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] And 1882 gave America a pair of immigration laws.

Maya: [measured] The famous one sorted people by race: the Chinese Exclusion Act, signed May 6, 1882. Chinese labor immigration suspended for ten years, laborers only, and Congress kept renewing it. The first time Washington shut the door on a whole nationality.

Jay: [sheepish] 1882. I nearly said '83.

Maya: [conversational] One year off, whole point off. The other law nobody remembers: the Immigration Act: a fifty-cent head tax per arriving non-citizen, barring convicts, the mentally ill, anyone likely to become a public charge.

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] Who organized the backlash?

Maya: [measured] The American Protective Association, 1887: anti-immigrant, anti-Catholic, the Know-Nothings' 1880s grandchild. And the Immigration Restriction League, 1894, with its literacy test: a race filter dressed as a reading test.

Maya: [conversational] Three down. Four. The farmers' crisis in one chain: gold standard, deflation, debt. [2-second pause]

Jay: [conversational] Washington capped the money supply with gold while harvests grew, so prices fell and fixed debts got heavier. Plus the railroad, the only road to market.

Maya: [confident tone] And in the South, the crop-lien system: borrow for seed in spring, promise the fall crop as collateral, debt rolling forward forever.

Maya: [energetic] Your turn. Kansas farmer, a thousand dollars at eight percent, wheat prices falling every harvest. What happens to the debt? [8-second pause]

Jay: [casual] It gets heavier. The dollars he repays are worth more than the dollars he borrowed.

Maya: [playful] Deflation turns borrowed dollars into boulders. My bedroom wall is a war room right now, sticky notes everywhere, and my cat just fell asleep on the Homestead box. She doesn't care. [chuckle]

Maya: [conversational] The Grange, 1867: co-ops and Granger Laws. Then the Alliances: a million Southern, two million Plains, a million Colored Farmers'.

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] Did the Black and white Alliances ever actually merge?

Maya: [firm] The trap. Same enemies, separate doors. Four million farmers had the numbers and split on the color line.

Maya: [energetic] Omaha, 1892. Run me through the platform. [2-second pause]

Jay: [casual] Free silver at sixteen to one. Graduated income tax. Direct election of senators. Government ownership of the railroads and the wires.

Maya: [conversational] And "free" silver doesn't mean —

Jay: [sheepish] Free money? The government handing out silver?

Maya: [firm] Not quite, and it's the classic trap. Free coinage: the mint stands open, silver in, silver dollars out. More money, inflation, debts shrink. Debt relief in a silver suit.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] My English teacher swears The Wizard of Oz is about the election that killed Populism: Dorothy's silver shoes, the Scarecrow as the farmer, the Cowardly Lion as Bryan. Historians argue about whether Baum meant it, but it works as a memory device.

Jay: [casual] The Scarecrow's the farmer? That's going straight into my notes.

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] So 1896: Bryan, thirty-six, the Cross of Gold speech, fusion with the Populists. And he lost.

Maya: [measured] To McKinley and Hanna's money machine, 271 to 176. Bryan took the South and the West; McKinley swept the Northeast and Midwest. Fusion was fatal: the Democrats swallowed the issue and the party died. But the platform won: income tax, Sixteenth; senators, Seventeenth. Both 1913.

Maya: [energetic] Five. Who was actually out West? [2-second pause]

Jay: [casual] Homesteaders: 160 acres, ten dollars, Homestead Act 1862. Exodusters out of the South. Miners: Pike's Peak, the Comstock, the Black Hills. Ranchers, long drives from Texas.

Maya: [conversational] Up to a third of the real cowboys were Mexican or African American, which the John Wayne version leaves out. Barbed wire fenced the range; the winters of '85 to '87 killed most of the cattle. And underneath all of them, the ground being cleared.

Jay: [serious tone] The reservations. The buffalo destroyed, the tribes on rations. Little Bighorn, June 1876: the Sioux and Cheyenne wiped out Custer's Seventh.

Maya: [firm] The biggest Native victory of the era, and it changed nothing. The army flooded back and the crushing came faster. Then the paperwork: the Dawes Act. What year?

Jay: [casual] 1887. Allotments, and the "surplus" sold to white settlers.

Maya: [conversational] Carlisle, 1879: the boarding schools that tried to erase the culture and save the child. Now the thesis. 1890: the census reports the frontier line has disappeared. 1893: Turner reads it in Chicago as —

Jay: [conversational] The closing of a great historic movement. The frontier as a process that made Americans American: democracy, self-reliance, individualism.

Maya: [thoughtful tone] Now the verb that decides it: the census never said —

Jay: [firm] Closed. The line disappeared. "Closed" is Turner's reading, and the prep books flatten it to one word.

Maya: [confident tone] Credit Turner for asking it, then list everybody he left out. Limerick, 1987, The Legacy of Conquest: the West as conquest, not process, and the people the thesis never names.

Maya: [energetic] Six. Why did America look outward in the 1890s? Stack it. [2-second pause]

Jay: [casual] The 1893 panic and the hunt for markets. Mahan, 1890: sea power. The missionaries, Strong's "brother's keepers." Beveridge's new frontier.

Maya: [conversational] The stack: one cause alone is a wrong answer in a confident suit. Then Cuba: the 1895 revolt, Spain's reconcentration camps, the yellow press. February 1898, the Maine explodes in Havana harbor.

Jay: [casual] Spain sank it.

Maya: [firm] Legend. The cause was never conclusively explained: likely a boiler failure. The papers declared Spain guilty because guilty sold papers. "Remember the Maine!" And into the April war resolution went the Teller Amendment: no annexation.

Maya: [measured] The Treaty of Paris, that December: Cuba free, the Philippines, Guam, Puerto Rico for twenty million, the Senate ratifying by a single vote. McKinley's reported reasoning: the Filipinos were unfit for self-government. And Hay's phrase for the summer war —

Jay: [casual] "A splendid little war." The Philippine war, right?

Maya: [firm] Mix-up. The Spanish-American War, summer of '98: fewer than four hundred American combat deaths. The Philippine-American War, 1899 to 1902, was the bloody aftershock: more than four thousand American dead, as many as two hundred thousand Filipino.

Jay: [serious tone] And Cuba got the Platt Amendment in 1901.

Maya: [confident tone] Forced into Cuba's constitution: no treaties without American approval, intervention when necessary, Guantanamo. Teller promised, 1898. Platt leashed, 1901. The Anti-Imperialist League, Twain, Carnegie, Bryan, called it the Revolution in reverse.

Maya: [conversational] Six boxes, let's land them. One: the integration machines. Carnegie owned every stage. Rockefeller bought, squeezed, or starved his rivals. Trusts, then holding companies when Sherman outlawed the trusts.

Maya: [conversational] Two: labor's defeats. Haymarket, 1886: the Knights blamed for a bomb they didn't throw. Homestead, 1892: Pennsylvania's state militia broke the strike. Pullman, 1894: injunctions and federal troops, antitrust aimed at the workers.

Maya: [conversational] Three: the Exclusion Act. 1882, laborers only, ten years, renewed and renewed. Plus the head-tax law the same year. Ellis 1892, Angel 1910: processed versus detained.

Maya: [conversational] Four: Populism. Deflation, the railroad, the crop-lien. Omaha 1892: free silver at sixteen to one, the income tax, senators, the wires. 1896: Bryan, the Cross of Gold, fusion, McKinley 271 to 176. The party died; the platform won: Sixteenth, Seventeenth, 1913.

Maya: [conversational] Five: the West. Homesteaders, Exodusters, miners, ranchers. Little Bighorn, June 1876: a battlefield victory, a war lost. Dawes — eighteen eighty... seven. Yes, 1887. Checking that one. My sticky note lied to me once.

Maya: [conversational] Turner, 1893: the closing of a great historic movement. The census measured, Turner concluded. Limerick, 1987: the West as conquest, not process.

Maya: [conversational] Six: the outward turn. The panic, Mahan, the missionaries, Beveridge. The Maine: cause never proven. Teller promised, Platt leashed. The Treaty of Paris: twenty million, one Senate vote.

Maya: [confident tone] Six boxes. All checked.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it.

Maya: Question one. A student writes: "The Sherman Act broke up the trusts, which is why big business faded in the 1890s." Where does that go wrong? [17-second pause]

Maya: Everywhere. The Sherman Act changed almost nothing at first: the courts ruled manufacturing wasn't interstate commerce, and Standard Oil just became a holding company. The law hit strikers first: the Pullman injunctions leaned on it. Big business didn't fade. It reorganized.

Maya: Question two, a stimulus. A census bulletin reports the unsettled area "has been so broken into by isolated bodies of settlement that there can hardly be said to be a frontier line." A student writes: "The 1890 census closed the frontier." What's the error? [17-second pause]

Maya: The verb. The census reported a measurement: the line had disappeared. "Closed" came three years later, from Turner in Chicago, reading the missing line as the closing of a great historic movement. The census reported the line gone. Turner supplied the word.

Maya: Question three. Congress touched Cuba twice: once in 1898, once in 1901. Name them in order, and say what each did. [15-second pause]

Maya: 1898: the Teller Amendment, in the war resolution: no annexation of Cuba. 1901: the Platt Amendment, written into Cuba's constitution: no treaties without American approval, intervention when necessary. The promise, then the leash.

Maya: One more, fast. 1896: who won, and what happened to the Populists' platform? [5-second pause]

Maya: McKinley, 271 to 176. Dead party, winning platform: the Sixteenth, the Seventeenth, 1913.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Six lessons, one sitting, most of the traps defused. Next: Unit 6, the Progressives — they read the whole story and got to work. Check your boxes.
