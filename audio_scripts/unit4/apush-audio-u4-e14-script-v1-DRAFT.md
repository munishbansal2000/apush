# U4-E14 — Maya + Marcus + Tomas (Fish Audio)
# Episode 14: Manifest Destiny and Texas. ~8.5 min.
# Draft v1.
# Read note: Debate format. Maya moderates. Marcus argues the expansionist position; Tomas is a fictional composite voicing the Mexican perspective. No pause tags in this episode. Strip this header and the read note before TTS.
# Pronunciation: O'Sullivan (oh-SUL-ih-vun), Nueces (noo-AY-sis), Tejano (teh-HAH-noh)

Maya: Last time: Seneca Falls rewrote the founding with three extra words. This time, the founding's promise stretched west. A phrase that launched a thousand wagons, and a Mexican province about to become a republic. Eight minutes on the question: destiny or land grab? Two debaters, two maps. You decide.

Maya: Marcus, you're the expansionist. Tomas, you speak for Mexico City. Opening statements. Marcus.

Marcus: In the summer of eighteen forty-five, a New York editor named John O'Sullivan gave the age its slogan. Writing in the Democratic Review, he said America had a "manifest destiny to overspread the continent allotted by Providence for the free development of our yearly multiplying millions." Underneath: a providential, racial claim. Anglo-Saxon Americans carrying democracy across the continent, everyone else expected to join or get out of the way. It wasn't just poetry. Farmers saw fertile soil. Miners chased gold. Religious groups like the Mormons just wanted to be left alone. Thousands risked the Oregon Trail, two thousand miles of dust and disease, for a fresh start. And Texas was the proof of concept. Mexico had invited American settlers in, Stephen Austin's colony from eighteen twenty-two. Then Mexico City turned tyrannical, and the Texans did what Americans do: they revolted.

Maya: Tomas, "providence." Whose providence?

Tomas: Not ours. Not the tribes' either. Manifest Destiny said God gave the continent to the United States. It didn't ask the people already living on it. That whole phrase is a trick. It turns conquest into weather, something that just happens.

Tomas: [solemn] Let me tell you what Texas looked like from Mexico City. Texas was Mexico. A northern province, settled for generations by Tejanos, Mexicans born in Texas. Your "invited settlers" brought something with them that was illegal in my country. Mexico abolished slavery in eighteen twenty-nine. By eighteen thirty, Anglo settlers outnumbered Tejanos and brought enslaved people anyway. When Mexico enforced its own laws, the Law of April sixth, eighteen thirty, no more American immigration, no more enslaved people imported, the colonists revolted. The Alamo's heroes were brave men. They were also fighting, in part, to keep slavery legal on Mexican soil. Not a war of independence. Secession by immigrants.

Maya: Positions staked. Marcus says providence and tyranny. Tomas says theft with better branding. Clash one: how Texas went from Mexican state to the Lone Star Republic.

Marcus: [fierce] The short version: Santa Anna tore up Mexico's constitution and made himself dictator. The Texans, Anglo and Tejano alike, rebelled in eighteen thirty-five. Santa Anna marched north and besieged the Alamo in San Antonio. One hundred eighty-nine defenders held for thirteen days. Travis wrote his "victory or death" letter from inside, and it electrified the United States. They all died. Then Goliad: four hundred Texan prisoners executed on Santa Anna's orders.

Maya: And San Jacinto was the payback?

Marcus: In eighteen minutes. April twenty-first, eighteen thirty-six. Sam Houston's army surprised Santa Anna's camp, shouting "Remember the Alamo! Remember Goliad!" Santa Anna was captured in a private's uniform. Texas was free. The republic survived nine years on debt and bravado, waiting for the United States to take it in.

Tomas: Texas was not free. Santa Anna signed the Treaties of Velasco with a gun to his head, and Mexico repudiated them immediately. For nine years, Mexico considered Texas a rebellious province, not a country. And the republic Texas built was a slave republic from its first constitution. When Mexico banned importing enslaved people in eighteen thirty, the colonists dodged it with paperwork, signing enslaved people to ninety-nine-year "indentures" and keeping them anyway. The revolt secured what the eighteen thirty law had tried to stop.

Marcus: Santa Anna gave every Mexican state a reason to revolt, not just Texas. He scrapped the federal constitution and imposed centralist rule. Zacatecas rose up. Yucatan rose up. Texas wasn't the only province that decided Mexico City had become a dictatorship.

Tomas: And Tejanos bled for Texas too. Juan Seguin led Tejano fighters at San Jacinto, and within a few years the new republic drove him out anyway. The revolution promised liberty and delivered it selectively. Ask the Tejanos whose independence it was.

Maya: The Alamo, that's the John Wayne movie, right? My dad loves that one.

Marcus: Nineteen sixty, yes. The history is messier than the movie. Wayne skipped the slavery part.

Maya: And the eighteen forty-four election was basically a referendum on all this, right?

Marcus: Polk ran on expansion: Texas now, Oregon next, "fifty-four forty or fight" for the northern border. Clay tried to dodge the Texas question and got crushed for it. The voters picked the expansionist. The mandate Polk carried into the war.

Maya: We drove through West Texas once. My dad announced "we're in Texas," and six hours later the landscape still hadn't changed.

Maya: So, clash two: annexation. Texas joins the United States, and it nearly broke American politics. Why?

Marcus: The slavery math. Texas was enormous, and everyone knew it would enter as a slave state. The joint resolution even allowed Texas to be split into as many as five states down the road. Five future Senate delegations. That's why the North panicked. The North saw the Senate balance tipping forever. Abolitionists called annexation a plot to extend slavery. President Tyler tried a straight annexation treaty in eighteen forty-four, and the Senate killed it. No two-thirds majority. So Tyler went around the Senate with a joint resolution, which needed only a simple majority. Congress passed it in early eighteen forty-five. Tyler signed on March first. That December, Texas entered the Union as the twenty-eighth state.

Tomas: Read that back slowly. The Senate rejected annexation by treaty, so the president found a procedural trick to do it anyway. In Mexico City, it looked like exactly what it was: the United States absorbing a province Mexico had never recognized as independent. Mexico broke diplomatic relations. Everyone in both capitals knew what came next.

Maya: So Mexico just let Texas go after San Jacinto? Nine years, no fight?

Tomas: No. Mexico never let go. There were raids, invasions, threats of reconquest for nine years. Mexico was too broke and too divided to retake Texas, but it never signed the loss. Annexation didn't settle the question. It dared Mexico to answer.

Maya: So annexation wasn't the end of the story. It was the provocation.

Maya: Final clash: the border itself. Texas didn't just join. It joined with a map dispute.

Marcus: Texas claimed its southern border was the Rio Grande. That was the line in the Velasco treaties, the line Texas had held for nine years. Mexico said the real border was the Nueces River, a hundred and fifty miles north, the old boundary of the Mexican state of Texas. Between the two rivers lay a strip of disputed land, and both countries planted their flags in it. When Polk sent General Taylor's army into the strip, Taylor built a fort on the Rio Grande across from Matamoros and waited. The border dispute was the fuse. A fuse needs a spark. The spark was Taylor's fort. Next episode is the explosion.

Tomas: The Nueces was the recognized boundary for generations. Texas drew the Rio Grande on a map and called it history. When President Polk sent General Taylor's army into the strip between the rivers, Mexico saw an invasion force, because that's what it was.

Maya: Closing statements. Marcus.

Marcus: Manifest Destiny wasn't a conspiracy. It was millions of people choosing a better life, and a nation growing into the continent's natural shape. Texas chose America. America chose Texas. The alternative was a weak republic caught between Mexico and Britain, and nobody in Texas wanted that.

Tomas: [quiet] My country lost a province the size of France to immigrants who revolted to protect slavery, then watched the United States legalize the theft with a parliamentary trick. In Mexico, schoolchildren learn this as the North American intervention, not a war between equals. We remember who drew the map. Call it destiny if you need to. From Mexico City, it looked like what it was.

Maya: Verdict time. Two maps, two histories. From Washington, eighteen forty-five looked like providence: a sister republic freely choosing the Union, the continent filling in. From Mexico City, it looked like secession and theft: a rebellious province absorbed by procedure, its slavery protected, its border redrawn by the winner. And at home, annexation detonated the slavery math. Every acre of Texas was a Senate vote, and the nation was running out of compromises. Expansion plus slavery equals crisis. That was the period's master equation. Destiny had a price, and the bill was coming due.

Maya: That's Episode 14. Next time: the border dispute becomes a war, a president claims American blood was shed on American soil, and a freshman congressman from Illinois asks to see the spot. Episode 15: the Mexican-American War.

Marcus: Providence provides.

Maya: And Mexico pays.
