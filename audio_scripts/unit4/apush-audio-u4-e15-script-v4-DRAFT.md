# Pronunciation: Guadalupe Hidalgo (gwah-dah-LOOP-ay ee-DAL-goh), Veracruz (veh-rah-KROOS), Matamoros (mah-tah-MOH-rohs), Chapultepec (chah-pool-teh-PEK), Cerro Gordo (SEH-roh GOHR-doh), Monterrey (mon-tuh-RAY), Buena Vista (BWAY-nah VEES-tah), Resaca de la Palma (ray-SAH-kah day lah PAHL-mah), Nueces (noo-AY-sez), Palo Alto (PAH-loh AHL-toh), Ashmun (ASH-mun), Wilmot (WIL-maht), Trist (trist), Niños Héroes (NEEN-yohs EH-roh-ehs)
Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: Texas joined the Union, Taylor's army stood in the disputed strip between the Nueces and the Rio Grande, and the fuse was lit. Now: the explosion. A president tells Congress about American blood on American soil. And a freshman congressman from Illinois writes back: show me the spot. Four boxes: the fuse, the war, the revolt at home, and the treaty that grew the country by a third and poisoned what came after. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. About fourteen minutes, pauses included.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Box one: the fuse. Marcus, you're Polk's man. Make his case. Rafael's our composite: he voices the Mexican civilians who lived it. Rafael gets the rebuttal.

Marcus: [passionate] The case is simple. Texas was a state, admitted December of eighteen forty-five, everything legal. In January, Polk ordered General Taylor's army to the Rio Grande to defend it. On April twenty-fifth, Mexican cavalry surrounded Captain Thornton's dragoons out on patrol. Polk's own message says sixty-three men, sixteen killed or wounded, the rest taken prisoner. The papers argue the exact count. I'll give you the version Congress heard.

Maya: [incredulous] Wait. "American soil"? Whose map says that?

Marcus: [measured] May eleventh, eighteen forty-six. Polk's actual words, from the message itself: Mexico had, quote, "passed the boundary of the United States, has invaded our territory and shed American blood upon the American soil." Two days later, Congress declared war. You don't ambush a nation's soldiers and call it a misunderstanding.

Maya: [confident tone] So Polk declares war on May eleventh.

Marcus: Polk asked for war on May eleventh. Congress declared it two days later.

Maya: [sheepish] He asked; Congress declared. I'll remember that.

Rafael: [passionate] Now the honest version. That strip, Nueces to Rio Grande, about a hundred and fifty miles of it, was disputed ground. Mexico said the border was the Nueces. Texas pointed at the Rio Grande because of papers Santa Anna signed as a prisoner, papers Mexico City never ratified. Polk knew all of it. In January he ordered Taylor across the Nueces; by March he was planted across from Matamoros, waiting for the shot.

Maya: I once sat in a car at the San Diego crossing for three hours, no air conditioning, staring at a fence that decides everything. A line on a map never felt less abstract. Rafael, finish it.

Rafael: [intense] Before the shooting, Polk tried to buy. He sent John Slidell to Mexico City in the fall of eighteen forty-five: five million for the disputed strip, twenty-five million for California, five million more for the lands between. Mexico wouldn't even receive him. When the buying failed, Polk dared Mexico to shoot first. The blood was real. The "American soil" part was the argument.

Maya: Your turn: the ground was disputed, and Congress knew it. The war's opponents couldn't deny the dead, so what did they attack instead?
[10-second pause]
Maya: The soil. The dead were real, but the ground was the argument — was the spot American at all? Lincoln's answer comes later. For now: fuse lit, exploded. One down.

Maya: Box two: the war. Marcus, how'd it go?

Marcus: Fast, and lopsided. The histories tell it like this: Taylor beat the Mexican army at Palo Alto, then at Resaca de la Palma. He took Monterrey. He broke a Mexican counterattack at the hard-fought Buena Vista. Then Winfield Scott landed at Veracruz in March of eighteen forty-seven, the first big amphibious landing in American military history. Cerro Gordo, then Mexico City that September. Twenty-one months, start to finish.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Old Fuss and Feathers. For the uniform?

Marcus: For the plumage. The army's own histories tell it: spit and polish, head to toe. The men named him, and he wore it like a medal.

Rafael: [passionate] Mexico's side of "lopsided": Santa Anna's armies marched underfed and underpaid for a government that couldn't pay them. When it was over, my country had lost about half its territory. At Chapultepec, Mexico's tradition says teenage cadets died defending the castle. The history books will remember the generals. They should remember the cadets.

Maya: Mexico remembers the Niños Héroes, the Boy Heroes — that's the story Mexico tells.

Maya: The camps did the real killing: dysentery, yellow fever, measles. Disease killed far more men than battle did — the histories are clear on that, even where the numbers are foggy — on both sides. The army histories note that Ulysses Grant fought this war as a young officer, and nearly forty years later he wrote in his memoirs. His actual words: "I do not think there was ever a more wicked war than that waged by the United States on Mexico."

Maya: [confident tone] The Alamo, though — that's the siege that kicked this whole war off, right? Texas rises up, and it ends in Mexico City.

Marcus: Different war. The Alamo was eighteen thirty-six, the Texas Revolution — last episode's territory. This war ends with American troops in Mexico City, eleven years later.

Maya: [sheepish] Eighteen thirty-six, Texas Revolution. That's going on my sheet.

Maya: Box three: the revolt at home. Summer of eighteen forty-six: a Democratic congressman from Pennsylvania, David Wilmot, attaches one sentence to a war-funding bill. Real words: "neither slavery nor involuntary servitude shall ever exist in any part of said territory." Marcus, the South heard that. What did it hear?

Marcus: For John C. Calhoun, speaking for the South, it was a door slamming: an institution that's legal in half the states can't be banned from territories that belong to all the states in common. You can't buy land with everybody's money and then tell half the country it can't bring its property.

Rafael: [intense] What the North kept forgetting: Mexico had already banned slavery in that land. Wilmot was banning what was already banned.

Maya: Which is exactly why it exploded. Your turn: if slavery was already illegal there, why did one sentence nearly break Congress? Think about who wanted the West next.
[10-second pause]
What drove the explosion was who'd settle the West next: Northern free-labor men wanted the new land for white settlers, with no slave labor to compete against. Southerners heard the territories closing to them for good. The House passed it again and again. The Senate never did. And the vote ran on the sectional line: North against South.

Maya: [sheepish] So the Wilmot Proviso banned slavery in the new territories, and the South walked out of Congress over it, right?

Marcus: It tried to ban it. It never became law. The House passed it again and again; the Senate never did. There was no walkout. The collision it started is what carries the exam.

Maya: December twenty-second, eighteen forty-seven. A freshman Whig congressman from Illinois, Abraham Lincoln, introduces eight resolutions. Real words again, from the House record: he wants to know "whether the particular spot of soil on which the blood of our citizens was so shed, was, or was not, our own soil, at that time." The question underneath: was that spot American soil at all? Had the people there ever lived under Texas law?

Marcus: Congress never voted on them.

Maya: They nicknamed him "spotty Lincoln." He was a first-term congressman.

Maya: Two weeks later, in January of eighteen forty-eight, the House went further. Real words, from the amendment: the war had been "unnecessarily and unconstitutionally begun by the President of the United States." Eighty-five Whigs voted yes, Lincoln among them, and it carried by a handful.

Maya: The protest wasn't only in Congress. Henry David Thoreau, the Walden guy, published "Civil Disobedience" in eighteen forty-nine: you don't have to obey an unjust government. The famous story says he spent a night in jail refusing a tax over this war.

Maya: The famous story says Thoreau told it himself — that's why it survives.

Marcus: A night in jail doesn't stop an army.

Maya: But the essay outlived the war.

Maya: Plenty of Northerners skipped the philosophy and said the plain version: a war to grow slavery, pushed by the Slave Power pulling Polk's strings. No proof of the strings ever surfaced. But the suspicion never died.

Marcus: Then why did Polk offer to extend the Missouri Compromise line straight to the Pacific? A man bent on growing slavery doesn't volunteer to ban it west of a line.

Maya: Box four: the treaty and the cost. February second, eighteen forty-eight: the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo. Mexico recognized the Rio Grande as the Texas border, and sold California and New Mexico, the Mexican Cession, for fifteen million dollars, with the United States taking over American citizens' claims against Mexico. Present-day California, Nevada, Utah, and big pieces of four more states. The country grew by a third.

Maya: The man who cut the deal, Nicholas Trist: by the standard account, Polk had fired him and ordered him home. He ignored the order and negotiated anyway. Polk hated the disobedience, and signed it anyway. He wanted out of the war.

Rafael: [passionate] The treaty promises the Mexicans living in the ceded lands their property and a road to American citizenship. Promises on paper. We've heard promises before. The grants will become lawyers' fortunes. The families, hired hands on land their grandfathers owned.

Maya: He couldn't have known it that February. But those promises mostly dissolved, in courts, in land commissions, over the decades.

Maya: I know the next part from a song, which Marcus will mock me for. My dad plays Dan Fogelberg on road trips. Fogelberg wrote one literally called "Sutter's Mill." January twenty-fourth, eighteen forty-eight: a carpenter named James Marshall finds gold flakes in a California millrace. Nine days later, the treaty is signed. Polk paid fifteen million for California without knowing what was in that river.

Marcus: For the whole cession — California, New Mexico, the works. Fifteen million, plus the claims.

Maya: [sheepish] The whole cession. My mistake. And he didn't know about the gold yet.

Marcus: Mock you? I didn't know the song. He promised to restore the Independent Treasury, cut the tariffs, settle Oregon, and take California, and by eighteen forty-eight he'd done all four. Judge the presidency by the promises.

Rafael: [intense] Judge it by the cost. About half my country's territory. Thousands dead on both sides, most to disease. You call it a continental nation. We call it the intervention.

Maya: The promises were signed in February of eighteen forty-eight. Paper promises; the courts and land commissions thinned them out for decades after. The land is still there, and so is the wound. The verdict: the war was won in Mexico and lost in Washington. It handed America a question it couldn't answer: slave or free, for every new territory, forever. The Wilmot Proviso asked it in eighteen forty-six. The eighteen forty-eight election dodged it. The battlefield moved from Mexico to Congress, and it stayed there until the Civil War.

Maya: That's the war. Now the hard part: remembering it straight.

Maya: Four boxes, let's land them. One: the fuse. The disputed strip, Thornton's dragoons, Slidell's failed buy, and Polk's message about American blood on what he called American soil. Two: the war. Palo Alto to Mexico City in twenty-one months, the Alamo stays in eighteen thirty-six, the Niños Héroes, and the real killer, disease. Three: the revolt at home. Wilmot's proviso, never a law. Lincoln's spot resolutions, never voted on. And a House majority calling the war unconstitutional.

Maya: [catching] Four: the treaty. Guadalupe Hidalgo, February second, fifteen million dollars plus the claims, the Rio Grande settled, and gold at Sutter's Mill... that was after the treaty, right? No, wait. Nine days before the signing. January twenty-fourth.

Marcus: Gold before the treaty. Nine days before.

Maya: [confident tone] Noted. All four boxes checked.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it.

Maya: One, stimulus. Real words, from Polk's war message of May eleventh, eighteen forty-six: Mexico had, quote, "passed the boundary of the United States, has invaded our territory and shed American blood upon the American soil." On the exam, what's the move with this source?
[10-second pause]
Maya: The move is sourcing. It's a war message, and its job is to get Congress to declare war. Attack the phrase "American soil": it assumes the boundary Polk himself chose, in a strip both sides claimed. Name the sleight: Polk put the army on disputed ground, then called the ground American.

Maya: Two. Wilmot's proviso banned slavery from land where Mexico had already banned slavery. So why did it nearly break Congress?
[10-second pause]
Maya: Same logic as before, in your own words: it ran on who'd settle the West next — free-labor North against a South that heard the territories closing for good. Calhoun's counter: the territories belong to all the states in common. Lincoln's spot resolutions pressed the other wound: was the spot American soil at all? Never voted on, but the question outlived the war. Never a law. The exam asks about the collision the proviso started.

Maya: Three, stimulus. Real words, from Ulysses Grant, writing nearly forty years later: "I do not think there was ever a more wicked war than that waged by the United States on Mexico." On the exam, what's Grant doing with this source?
[10-second pause]
He's handing you the war's moral verdict from the inside: a veteran calling his own war unjust. Pair it with the facts: twenty-one months, Palo Alto to Mexico City, "fast, and lopsided." Disease killed far more than bullets, on both sides — the histories agree on that, even where the numbers get foggy.

Maya: One more, fast. The treaty: date and price.
[10-second pause]
Maya: February second, eighteen forty-eight. Fifteen million dollars, and the United States took over American claims against Mexico.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Polk's war ended at Guadalupe Hidalgo. Its argument didn't end until Appomattox. Check your boxes, all four. Next time: the whole unit in one sitting. Ten questions, no mercy. The Unit 4 cram.
