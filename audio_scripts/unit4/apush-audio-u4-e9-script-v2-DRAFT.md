Maya: Jackson fought two wars at home in the 1830s, and only one needed an army. Last episode: nullification, South Carolina and the tariff. E7 sketched the Bank War; this episode argues it. Marcus speaks for Andrew Jackson. Across from him: Nicholas Biddle, the Bank's president, in his 1832-to-36 voice. Four boxes: the veto message as an argument, Biddle's credit squeeze, the Specie Circular and the land bubble, the Panic of 1837 with the verdict question. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. About fourteen minutes, pauses included. Let's go.

Maya: Quick rules. When you hear Jackson's or Biddle's actual words, you'll hear "real quote" first. Those lines are verified. Everything else from Marcus and Biddle is dramatized: built from what they wrote and did, performed by my guests. I'm the only modern voice here, so anything after 1836 belongs to me.

Maya: Opening statements. Marcus, the General's case.

Marcus: The Second Bank was a private corporation, run for profit, holding the nation's money and ordering every state bank around. Its president decided who got credit, which meant he decided which businesses lived. More than a fourth of its stock sat in foreign hands; the rest with a few hundred of the richest men in America. The General saw concentrated power no republic should tolerate.

Biddle: I ran that bank for thirteen years. It gave this country a sound currency when state banks printed paper by the ream. It restrained reckless lending, steadied the money markets, and safeguarded the government's deposits. Mr. Marcus calls that concentrated power. I call it the reason a banknote was worth the same in Boston and New Orleans. And the President scattered the federal deposits among state banks chosen for loyalty over soundness. If you want corruption, look at the pets, not the monster.

Maya: Box one: the veto message, July tenth, 1832. Not the politics, the argument. Marcus, give me the strongest version.

Marcus: Two pillars first. One: the Bank is a monopoly no Congress had the right to sell, exclusive privilege at the public's expense, a gift to men already rich. Two: it answers to no one, a concentration of power in a few hands, more than a fourth of it foreign-held.

Maya: And the third pillar is the Constitution. Lay it out.

Marcus: Jackson said the Supreme Court doesn't get the last word on the Constitution for the presidency. Real quote: "Each public officer who takes an oath to support the Constitution swears that he will support it as he understands it, and not as it is understood by others."

Maya: Large claim, the president overruling the Court's reading. Biddle, is that the message's weak spot?

Biddle: It is the weak spot. The Court settled the Bank's constitutionality in 1819, in McCulloch against Maryland. For the President to answer thirteen years later with "I understand it differently" is not constitutional reasoning. It is a veto in search of a doctrine. And the message contradicts itself: it calls a national bank convenient for the Government and useful to the people, Jackson's own words, then declares this bank a danger to liberty. Which is it?

Maya: Before Marcus answers, the famous closing passage. Marcus.

Marcus: Real quote: "It is to be regretted that the rich and powerful too often bend the acts of government to their selfish purposes." The charter would, his words, "make the rich richer and the potent more powerful," while the farmers, mechanics, and laborers get nothing.

Biddle: Powerful rhetoric. But rhetoric is not evidence. The message never proved the Bank was badly run, because it was well run. It proved the President could make the rich sound like villains and a veto sound like justice. A campaign speech, not a bank examiner's report.

Maya: Exam trap: students read "foreigners held the stock" and write that the Bank was foreign-owned. More than a fourth was foreign-held, mostly British money, but it was an American corporation under an American charter. Common mistake for this box: the veto didn't kill the Bank that afternoon. The charter ran to 1836. Jackson killed the recharter, not the Bank. One number worth carrying: twelve vetoes, more than all six presidents before him combined.

Maya: One more thing. Henry Clay forced that recharter vote four years early, betting a Jackson veto would cost him the 1832 election. Your turn. You're advising Jackson that summer. Veto and own the fight, or sign and defuse it? Which costs him less? Reason it out. [9-second pause]

Maya: Jackson vetoed, and Clay's trap caught Clay: the rich-and-powerful language played straight to voters, and Jackson won reelection. Box one is checked. No more checkoffs till the recap.

Maya: Box two: the contraction. In 1833 Jackson moved the federal deposits out of the Bank and into friendly state banks, the pets. Biddle answered by tightening credit through 1833 and 1834. Actually, wait. The Panic of 1837, that's right after the deposits came out? Months later?

Marcus: Four years later. The squeeze ran 1833 to 1834, its own event, its own fight. The Panic comes in 1837, under Van Buren.

Maya: Four years. Okay, that changes the shape of it. Biddle, defend the squeeze.

Biddle: When the government rips its deposits out of a bank, that bank must protect the depositors who remain. I called in loans and raised rates, as any banker would. The alternative was to keep lending money I no longer held and collapse entirely. Marcus calls it blackmail. I call it solvency.

Marcus: He used the pain as leverage. Pressure as strategy. It backfired: the country watched the Bank inflict pain on purpose, and anti-Bank feeling grew. Jackson told Van Buren, reported not transcript-verified but widely quoted, "The Bank is trying to kill me, but I will kill it." The Bank War is why he said it.

Maya: February 1834, Biddle, a letter to a federal judge. Real quote — reported, widely quoted, not transcript-verified: "This worthy President thinks that because he has scalped Indians and imprisoned Judges, he is to have his way with the Bank. He is mistaken." You meant that to stay private?

Biddle: I wrote it in anger, and I don't hide from it. A president had used the executive power to break a lawful institution. I was wrong to write so hotly. I was not wrong about the power used against me.

Maya: If the exam asks about the contraction, don't write Biddle as a cartoon villain who crashed the economy for fun. The strong answer holds both: after the deposits were pulled, some tightening was self-defense, and Biddle also used the pain as leverage. Common mistake: the order. The deposits came out in 1833; the contraction came after.

Maya: Box three: the Specie Circular, 1836. Marcus, no spin. The Jackson side's honest version.

Marcus: From now on, federal land is sold for hard currency only: gold and silver coin, what they called specie. No more paper, no more buying public land on credit. The pet banks had shoveled paper at land buyers, and prices were running wild. The Circular was Jackson trying to pop the bubble before it popped the country. Congress killed it in his last days. It was a brake, not a bomb.

Maya: Biddle, the Circular was aimed at your pet-bank argument. Fair hit?

Biddle: The Circular proved my point, not his. Jackson's own chosen banks inflated the land bubble with reckless paper. Then his Circular told every speculator their paper was worthless for the one purchase that mattered. He built the bubble with one hand and burst it with the other.

Maya: Your turn. It's 1836. You hold paper claims on frontier land, and the government just announced it will only take gold and silver. What happens to land prices over the next year, and what happens to the people holding your paper? Reason it out. [9-second pause]

Maya: Prices fall: the books record falling land prices and a drop in federal land-sale revenue after the Circular. The paper holders are left holding promises no land office will honor.

Maya: I keep thinking about my first debit card. Three-hundred-dollar limit, and I still maxed it out on concert merch. Somebody else set the limit, and I still found the edge. That's the pet banks, isn't it? Handed the government's money with nobody watching.

Biddle: An expensive education, but an accurate one.

Maya: Timing trap: the Circular is 1836, the Panic is 1837. Don't reverse them or merge them into one event. Common mistake: the Circular covered federal land only. Students write "Jackson banned paper money." He didn't.

Maya: Box four: the Panic of 1837, and the verdict question. Rules: Biddle, your voice stops in 1836. You never saw the Panic, so give me the principle, what you warned would happen. Marcus, you know the decade through the Panic. The modern verdict is mine, because the afterlife belongs to me.

Biddle: Here is the principle, and I stated it before 1836 ended. Take away the institution that restrains the state banks, and no hand is left to steady them. My contraction of 1833 was the rehearsal: when credit tightened, the whole country felt it within weeks. Remove the Bank, and the next squeeze has no manager.

Marcus: For Jackson's side, the Panic had more than one parent. The pet banks lent paper wildly, and killing the Bank didn't cause that bubble. Two: word from London — their banks pulling credit back across the Atlantic. No president in Washington controls that. Three: cotton prices falling, the crop our planters staked everything on. The Circular was Jackson reaching for the brake. Grabbed too late and too hard, maybe. But he didn't cause the crash single-handed. And I'll admit what I can't prove: exactly how much of 1837 was the Bank's absence versus the bubble. Nobody's settled that cleanly.

Maya: The modern verdict, and it's genuinely split. Killing the Bank removed the one institution that had restrained the state banks for over a decade. But the crash also needed the pet-bank paper bubble and the Circular's hard-money shock — and some historians point across the Atlantic: British credit tightening, cotton prices falling, a crop grown by enslaved labor — and say no president controls that. Van Buren inherited the pile in 1837, and the downturn ran about five years. No single hand did this. If your essay picks one cause, the AP reader has read the other one.

Maya: One rewind, because the party came before the Panic. Jackson's enemies had been gathering since the deposit fight, and in 1834 they formally organized as the Whigs, borrowing the name from the British party that opposed the king's power. Their nickname for Jackson was "King Andrew," and the name was the insult. One thing in common: they thought the president had too much power. The Second Party System takes its shape here, Democrats against Whigs into the 1850s.

Maya: People on my feed still fight about whether Jackson belongs on the twenty-dollar bill. You're the 1830s guy, so you missed that one.

Marcus: The twenty-dollar bill, they put his face on the money? After all that?

Maya: They did.

Maya: Costliest common mistake for this box: "Jackson caused the Panic of 1837." One cause, one sentence, done. The exam rewards the both-sided version: the Bank's death removed the restraint, and the pet banks, the Circular, Britain, and cotton all fed the fire. Hold two causes in one sentence and you're at the top of the rubric.

Maya: The fight's on the record. Now let's see what you kept.

Maya: Four boxes, let's land them. Box one: the veto message, July 1832. Monopoly, foreign stockholders, and the president reading the Constitution for himself. Box two: the contraction. Deposits out in '33, credit squeezed through '34, both men calling it self-defense. Box three: the Circular. Jackson bans paper money in 1836—

Marcus: For federal land. Paper lived on everywhere else.

Maya: For federal land only. Right. Box four: the Panic of 1837. The verdict stays split, no single hand. Plus the Whigs, organized in '34 against "King Andrew." Checking all four.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. Take the full fifteen to twenty seconds.

Maya: One. Stimulus, July 1832. Real quote: "It is to be regretted that the rich and powerful too often bend the acts of government to their selfish purposes." What's the author's purpose, and what does it reveal about his strategy? [15-second pause]

Maya: The purpose is persuasion, not law: the closing of a veto message, aimed at voters as much as senators. The evidence is the matchup, "the rich and powerful" against "the farmers, mechanics, and laborers." Jackson turned a constitutional fight over a bank charter into a moral fight over who the government serves. He was campaigning, not explaining.

Maya: Two. Jackson pulled the deposits in 1833 instead of waiting for the charter to die in 1836. Why was the faster move worth the cost? [18-second pause]

Maya: Because a Bank with its deposits intact could fight back: fund its defense, keep lending, survive to embarrass him. Biddle's contraction proved the Bank would use its remaining power as leverage. Waiting meant three more years of a hostile institution sitting on the government's own money. Jackson paid the price to starve it now.

Maya: Three. The Whigs named themselves after the British party that opposed the king's power. What does that tell you about how they saw Jackson? [20-second pause]

Maya: Their charge was constitutional before it was economic: they saw a president grabbing power like a monarch. The evidence is the nickname "King Andrew," and a coalition whose glue was opposition to Jackson. The name is the argument: executive power as monarchy — Jackson was the tyranny the Whigs existed to check.

Maya: One more, fast. The Panic of 1837: one cause, or no single hand? [8-second pause] No single hand. The verdict stays split.

Biddle: And on the contraction: a bank that cannot defend its depositors' money is no bank at all. A president who yanks the floor out from under it, then blames it for the fall, is the one wielding the power he fears.

Maya: The Bank is gone. The argument never adjourned.

Maya: Check your boxes. Next time: removal. The Cherokee Nation's fight in the courts, in the press, and on the road west. Episode ten: the Trail of Tears.
