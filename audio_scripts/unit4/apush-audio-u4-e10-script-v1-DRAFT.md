# Pronunciation: Sequoyah (sih-KWOY-uh), Worcester (WUUS-tur), Dahlonega (dah-LON-ih-guh), Quatie Ross (KWAY-tee)

Marcus: [solemn] October eighteen thirty-eight. Dawn in Georgia. Soldiers come to the door and give a Cherokee family minutes to pack a lifetime. The most vivid telling comes from a soldier named John Burnett, published on his eightieth birthday, in eighteen ninety. The details hadn't faded. He was twenty-seven during the removal. He remembered it at eighty. "I saw the helpless Cherokees arrested and dragged from their homes, and driven at the bayonet point into the stockades." He described the October morning the wagons rolled west: Chief John Ross leading the people in prayer, the bugle sounding, children rising to wave goodbye to their mountain homes. But the honest footnote: muster records show his company was discharged in eighteen thirty-seven, a year before the removal, and scholars argue over how much he truly saw. A contested account, not certain eyewitness fact.

Marcus: To understand that dawn, you have to understand who the Cherokee were. Not a wandering tribe. A nation. Under their chief John Ross, they made a deliberate bid to survive by playing by American rules. Sequoyah, a Cherokee silversmith, invented a written syllabary for the Cherokee language in eighteen twenty-one, eighty-five characters, each standing for a syllable. Cherokee children learned it in weeks. Within a few years thousands of Cherokee could read and write it. They wrote themselves a constitution in eighteen twenty-seven, modeled on the United States, with an elected council and courts. They started a newspaper, the Cherokee Phoenix, in eighteen twenty-eight, edited by Elias Boudinot, the first Native newspaper in the country, printed in two languages side by side. Ross himself, son of a Scottish father and a Cherokee mother, educated in American schools, would lead the Nation for nearly forty years. They farmed, ran schools, dressed the way their white neighbors dressed. By every standard white America claimed to value, the Cherokee were doing everything right.

Marcus: It didn't matter. In eighteen twenty-nine, gold was found on Cherokee land in Georgia, around Dahlonega. It was America's first gold rush, and it made the state's hunger for Cherokee land unstoppable. The state held lotteries to hand Cherokee land and gold rights to white winners. Georgia passed laws stripping the Cherokee of their rights: they couldn't mine gold, couldn't do business, couldn't testify in court against a white person. John Ross went to Washington and asked President Jackson to recognize Cherokee sovereignty. Jackson's answer was the Indian Removal Act of eighteen thirty: the southeastern nations would trade their homelands for land west of the Mississippi.

Marcus: Georgia's trap had a human face. The state passed a law forcing white people living in Cherokee territory to get a state license and swear loyalty to Georgia. A Vermont missionary named Samuel Worcester refused. He'd lived among the Cherokee for years, preaching, helping with the printing press. Georgia threw him into prison at hard labor. His case became Worcester versus Georgia.

Marcus: The Cherokee fought back the only way left: in court. In eighteen thirty-one, in Cherokee Nation versus Georgia, the Supreme Court called them "domestic dependent nations." Not a foreign country, not fully subject either. A legal limbo. Then in eighteen thirty-two came Worcester versus Georgia, and this time the Cherokee won outright. Chief Justice Marshall ruled that Georgia's laws had no force in Cherokee territory. The law was on the Cherokee's side. And it changed nothing, because a court has no army. Jackson is supposed to have said, "John Marshall has made his decision; now let him enforce it." Supposedly. The line may be legend. The defiance was real. Jackson refused to enforce the ruling and pushed removal anyway. Georgia quietly pardoned Worcester the next year, once the political point was made. The missionary went free. The Cherokee stayed condemned.

Marcus: Then came the paper that made it all "legal." In December eighteen thirty-five, at New Echota, Georgia, about twenty Cherokee men signed away every acre of Cherokee land east of the Mississippi, for five million dollars and new land in the West. None of them were elected leaders of the Nation. They were a minority faction, the Treaty Party, led by Major Ridge. Ridge had fought beside Andrew Jackson at Horseshoe Bend. He wasn't a villain in his own story. He'd watched Georgia swallow Cherokee land piece by piece for thirty years and concluded the Nation couldn't win. Better, he argued, to negotiate the best terms possible than to lose everything to Georgia's lotteries. John Ross called it treason. Both men believed they were saving the Cherokee. Only one of them had the Nation behind him. Ross and the elected government called the treaty fraudulent. Ross carried a petition to Congress with roughly sixteen thousand Cherokee signatures, nearly the whole Nation, begging the Senate not to ratify. The Senate ratified it anyway, in May eighteen thirty-six, by a single vote. The treaty gave the Cherokee two years to leave. Ross didn't stop. In the spring of eighteen thirty-eight he carried a memorial to Congress, signed by roughly sixteen thousand Cherokee, laying out what enforcement would mean: the farms and schools abandoned, the council fires put out, the graves of their fathers left behind. Congress read it and did nothing. The deadline came in May eighteen thirty-eight.

Marcus: What people get wrong about this story: they picture Andrew Jackson personally marching the Cherokee out in eighteen thirty-eight. He didn't. Jackson was out of office. It was Martin Van Buren's administration that carried out the removal, enforcing Jackson's treaty on Jackson's deadline. And there wasn't one trail. There were many detachments, traveling different routes across nine states, over months. The suffering wasn't a single march. It was a system. The government called the whole thing voluntary emigration. Nobody who was dragged from their home at bayonet point used that word.

Marcus: In May eighteen thirty-eight, General Winfield Scott arrived with seven thousand troops and began the roundups. Families were dragged from their homes at bayonet point and herded into dozens of stockades, open-walled prison camps through a brutal summer. Drought made the rivers too low to travel, so thousands sat in those camps for months. The army marched the first detachments west in the summer heat, and people died in shocking numbers. After that, Ross negotiated for the Cherokee to conduct the remaining removals themselves. The Cherokee-led detachments that left that fall were better supplied and better organized. They still buried their dead along every road.

Marcus: The Cherokee weren't alone. The Choctaw, the Creek, the Chickasaw, all forced west under the same law. The Seminole refused and fought a seven-year war in the Florida swamps instead. Then, that October, the forced march began. In Burnett's contested account, they were loaded into six hundred forty-five wagons, "like cattle or sheep," headed west. A thousand miles, in detachments of about a thousand people each, moving a few miles a day. On November seventeenth, a sleet and snowstorm hit with freezing temperatures, and from that day the journey became "a trail of death," in his telling. They slept in wagons and on the ground without fire. He wrote of as many as twenty-two dying in one night of pneumonia, from ill treatment, cold, and exposure. [quiet] Among the dead was the wife of Chief John Ross. Quatie Ross gave her only blanket to a sick child, rode thinly clad through a blinding storm, and died in the night. Roughly four thousand Cherokee died. About one in four of the people forced west.

Marcus: The Nation survived. In Tahlequah, the new capital in what is now Oklahoma, the Cherokee wrote a constitution in eighteen thirty-nine, rebuilt the schools, and started the newspaper again. The Nation endures today. But the wound never closed. The men who signed the Treaty of New Echota, Major Ridge, his son John, Elias Boudinot, were killed by their own people in eighteen thirty-nine. And the question the whole story asks never got answered. What is a Supreme Court victory worth if the president won't enforce it? What is a treaty worth if the people it binds never agreed to it?

Marcus: The name itself is a translation. In Cherokee it was Nunna daul Tsuny: the trail where they cried. The English shortened it. The meaning survived.

Marcus: The one thing to carry forward: the gap between American law and American power. The Cherokee won in the courtroom and lost everything on the ground, because law without enforcement is just words. Every time this country has promised justice on paper and delivered something else in practice, that's the pattern. Learn to spot it. Remember the dawn in Georgia. Remember who was at the door.

Marcus: Next time, a different kind of American fire. While the nation argued about banks and removal, the churches were filling with a religious explosion that would remake everything from schools to prisons. Episode 11: the Second Great Awakening.
