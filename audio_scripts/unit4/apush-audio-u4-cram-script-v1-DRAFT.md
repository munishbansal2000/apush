# U4-CRAM — Maya + Jay (Fish Audio)
# Unit 4 Cram Session: 1800-1848. ~7 min.
# Draft v1.
# Read note: Study Buddies rapid-fire. Maya fires ten cumulative questions across the whole unit; Jay answers, stumbles, gets corrected. Period thesis beat, then the predict-the-DBQ beat, then the sign-off. Pause tags: the self-test beats below carry named [2-second pause] tags, recorded here for G7. Strip this header and the read note before TTS.

Maya: Unit 4. All of it. Eighteen hundred to eighteen forty-eight. Ten questions, no mercy, seven minutes. If you can answer these ten cold, you can pass the unit. If your brain is full, good. If it's not, rewind. Jay, you studied?

Jay: I studied. Whether it stuck is your problem now.

Maya: Question one. Jefferson, strict constructionist, buys Louisiana. How did he justify it?

Jay: He kind of didn't? He thought the Constitution didn't allow it, but the deal was too good. So he used the treaty power. The Senate ratified it like a treaty. And he told himself the country needed it.

Maya: Exactly. Strict construction when it suited him, a treaty when it didn't. The kicker: he actually wanted a constitutional amendment first, and his cabinet talked him out of it. The purchase doubled the country and broke his own rules to do it. Question two. Marbury versus Madison. What did it establish, and why does it still matter?

Jay: Judicial review. The Supreme Court can strike down laws that violate the Constitution. It matters because, uh, it's the whole reason the Court has power over Congress and the president.

Maya: Eighteen oh three, and we're still living under it. Marshall's move: Adams's midnight judges, Marbury's commission never delivered, and the Court grabs the power to say what the law is. Question three. McCulloch versus Maryland. Two holdings. Go.

Jay: One: implied powers. Congress can do what's "necessary and proper," so the Bank is constitutional even though the Constitution never says "bank." Two: states can't tax the Bank, because federal law beats state law. Supremacy.

Maya: Maryland tried to tax the Bank's notes out of existence. Marshall said no. Clean. Question four. Why was the War of eighteen twelve called the Second War of Independence?

Jay: Because the first one didn't fully settle it? Britain was still impressing sailors, messing with trade, holding forts in the West. After eighteen twelve, all of that stopped. Plus it killed the Federalists. The Hartford Convention made them look like traitors.

Maya: And it lit up nationalism. New Orleans made Jackson a hero, the anthem got written, the Era of Good Feelings started. The Federalists' funeral. Question five. The Monroe Doctrine. What did it claim, and could we enforce it?

Jay: Eighteen twenty-three. Europe: stay out of the Western Hemisphere. No new colonies. And we won't mess with yours. And enforce it? Sure, we had a big navy by then—

Maya: Did we?

Jay: ...No. We had basically no navy. Britain's navy did the enforcing because they liked the trade.

Maya: There's the wobble. Adams wrote it. Monroe announced it. Someone else's battleships backed it. Question six. The Missouri Compromise. How did it work, and what was the thirty-six thirty line?

Jay: Eighteen twenty. Missouri comes in as a slave state, Maine comes in free, so the Senate stays balanced. And the line: slavery banned north of thirty-six thirty in the Louisiana territory. Except Missouri itself.

Maya: Except Missouri itself. Good catch. Question seven. Nullification Crisis. Two sides.

Jay: South Carolina, Calhoun's crew, said a state could nullify a federal law it hated, the tariff. Jackson said absolutely not, threatened force, got the Force Bill. Then Clay brokered a compromise tariff and everybody stood down.

Maya: And the toast that started it. Eighteen thirty, Jefferson Day dinner, Jackson looks at Calhoun and says: "Our Federal Union, it must be preserved." Calhoun answers: "The Union, next to our liberty, most dear." The whole crisis in two toasts. But the slavery question hiding inside it got dodged for another thirty years. Question eight. Why did Jackson kill the Bank, and what happened to the economy after?

Jay: He thought it was a monster, a private bank with public money that helped the rich. Vetoed the recharter in eighteen thirty-two, pulled the deposits into his pet banks. Then Biddle, the Bank's president, squeezed credit to prove Jackson wrong. It all blew up into the Panic of eighteen thirty-seven. Under Van Buren, not Jackson.

Maya: Van Buren inherited the crater. And the pet banks lent it all out, speculation everywhere, then Jackson's Specie Circular said land could only be bought with gold and silver. The bubble popped. Nice. Question nine. The Trail of Tears. Legally and humanly.

Jay: Legally: the Indian Removal Act, eighteen thirty, then Worcester versus Georgia said the Cherokee were sovereign, and Jackson ignored it. Then the Treaty of New Echota, signed by like twenty guys who didn't speak for the tribe. Humanly: eighteen thirty-eight, the army rounded up sixteen thousand Cherokee, marched them west in winter. Four thousand died.

Maya: And Ross's petition, sixteen thousand Cherokee names begging Congress to stop it, ignored. [2-second pause] Say the number again.

Jay: [2-second pause] Four thousand.

Maya: Good. Don't round that one down. Last one. Question ten. Name two Second Great Awakening reforms and one abolitionist strategy debate.

Jay: Reforms: temperance and women's rights. Or public schools, asylums. Take your pick, there were a million. The debate: moral suasion versus political action. Garrison said don't touch politics, just convert hearts. The Liberty Party said run candidates, change laws.

Maya: And Seneca Falls, eighteen forty-eight: Stanton, Mott, Douglass, the Declaration of Sentiments, twelve resolutions. Lightning round, three names. One reformer, one abolitionist, one woman at Seneca Falls. Go.

Jay: Uh. Dorothea Dix. Frederick Douglass. Elizabeth Cady Stanton.

Maya: Three for three. Name the paper.

Jay: The North Star?

Maya: Douglass's paper. I want Garrison's.

Jay: The Liberator.

Maya: Liberator. The convention.

Jay: Seneca Falls.

Maya: Ten for ten, minus the wobble on five. Now the period thesis. The sentence the whole unit hangs on: the United States became a continental democracy for white men, and every step of that growth tightened the knot over slavery until compromise stopped working.

Jay: Democracy for white men, and a knot over slavery. That's the unit in one breath.

Maya: Test it. Louisiana Purchase: more land, more slavery fights. Jacksonian democracy: more voters, more Indian removal. The Market Revolution: more factories, more immigrants, more cities, more reformers complaining about all of it. Texas, the Mexican War: more territory, and the Wilmot Proviso asking whether slavery follows. Every expansion feeds the knot.

Jay: So democracy and slavery grew together. The knot.

Maya: The knot. And it snaps in the next unit. Remember that sentence. It's the whole unit in your pocket.

Maya: Now, predict the DBQ. If I were writing your exam, I'd ask this: "Evaluate the extent to which the Jacksonian era expanded American democracy." You've got everything for it. The thesis writes itself: expanded for white men, contracted for everyone else. Bank, nullification, removal, the spoils system, all of it.

Jay: And the backup?

Maya: Backup: the Market Revolution. "Evaluate the social and economic effects of the Market Revolution." Canals, railroads, the factory system, immigration, cities, the middle class, and the backlash. If Jackson doesn't show up, the market will.

Jay: So study both, bet on Jackson.

Maya: Study both, bet on Jackson. Three body paragraphs if it hits: one, the expansion, who got the vote and how. Two, the limits, who got crushed: Native peoples, enslaved people, women. Three, the contradiction: a democracy that ran on exclusion. Graders want the tension, not a hero story. Show them the democracy and the exclusion in the same paragraph. That's the essay.

Jay: Expansion, limits, contradiction. Got it.

Maya: Unit 4: fifteen episodes, one cram, zero mercy. Say the thesis once more.

Jay: Democracy for white men, knot over slavery.

Maya: Sleep. Water. Go get the five. And say the thesis in your sleep. Unit 5 is the Civil War. Bring bandages.

Jay: Bring bandages. Got it.
