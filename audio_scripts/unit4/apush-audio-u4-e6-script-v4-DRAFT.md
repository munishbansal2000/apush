# Pronunciation: EER-ee (Erie); LOH-uhl (Lowell); MEH-rih-mak (Merrimack); muh-KOR-mik (McCormick); Deere rhymes with "deer"; FOOL-tun (Fulton)

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: the compromise held the Union together — now the economy remakes it. Picture America in 1800: most people growing what they eat and hauling everything else by wagon over mud. Forty years later the whole thing runs on markets: strangers buying and selling across a thousand miles. Four boxes on your sheet: the transportation revolution, the factory system and the telegraph, King Cotton's South, and the human cost. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. About thirteen minutes, and they're yours.

Marcus: Start with the roads, because they came first and they were terrible.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Terrible how? Potholes terrible?

Marcus: Worse. Hauling grain overland was ruinously expensive. Freight could cost more than the grain itself. The Lancaster turnpike in Pennsylvania was one of the first big engineered roads, built by private investors who charged tolls, and the National Road pushed west from Maryland toward the Ohio River. But roads stayed slow and pricey.

Maya: So water was the answer.

Marcus: Water was always the answer. Cheap to float things, expensive to drag them. Robert Fulton's steamboat, the Clermont, proved itself on the Hudson in 1807, and within a generation steamboats were running up and down the Mississippi, freight moving against the current.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Hold on. Before the steamboat, boats could only go downstream? The river was a one-way trip?

Marcus: Mostly, yes. Upriver travel was brutal, slow work. The steamboat made the river two-way, and that changed what the whole interior could sell.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And then the big ditch. One canal, and the whole country loses its mind?

Marcus: The Erie Canal. Completed in 1825, Albany to Buffalo, straight across New York State, linking the Hudson River to the Great Lakes. Freight costs from Buffalo to New York City fell by something like ninety percent, and New York City became the country's great port off that one canal.

Maya: I sang the canal song in music class. "Low bridge, everybody down": the mule, the barge, all of it. I had no idea it was about, like, the economy.

Marcus: It was always about the economy. The canal set off a canal craze: everybody digging. But canals were slow, so the next challenger was already coming. The Baltimore and Ohio broke ground in 1828, the first tracks went down by 1829, and by the 1830s steam locomotives were hauling freight faster than any canal boat.

Maya: Your turn. It's 1820 and you're a farmer outside Buffalo with grain to sell. Before the canal, moving it east costs a fortune per ton. After, roughly a tenth. What changes about where you can sell?

[10-second pause]

Marcus: Everything. Before, you sold to whoever was close, because distance ate your profit. After, you can sell in New York City, hundreds of miles away, and still come out ahead. One decision, and the whole revolution's in it: farmers stop growing just to survive and start growing to sell.

Maya: Box one: the transportation revolution. One down.

Marcus: By the mid-1850s, moving goods across the country cost about one-twentieth of what it had in 1825, arriving in a fifth of the time.

Maya: One-twentieth? Not an improvement — a different universe.

Marcus: Exactly. Next: communication.

Maya: Box two, then: the factory system. And I'm calling it: the telegraph has to be older than the canal. News travels faster than boats, so somebody wired the country first.

Marcus: Good logic, wrong order. The canal's 1825; the telegraph's 1844. The boats did the work for two decades before the wires did. Never flip them.

Maya: [sheepish] Noted. And I'm absolutely going to flip them on the test, I can feel it.

Marcus: The manufacturing side starts earlier, with a gun contract. Eli Whitney won a government contract for muskets and machined every part to identical specs instead of crafting each gun by hand. Trigger breaks? Swap in a fresh one. That was interchangeable parts, the American system of manufacturing.

Maya: Your turn. You're a gun owner in 1790 and your musket's trigger snaps. Old world: you need a master gunsmith. New world, every part identical: who can fix it?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Anyone. Same specs, any gun. And once it worked for muskets, it worked for clocks and tools and everything else.

Marcus: And the factory itself came from a memory. Samuel Slater had supervised textile machinery in Britain, sailed to America in 1789, and rebuilt the machines from what he'd memorized. Pawtucket, Rhode Island. America's first factory town.

Maya: [incredulous] Wait — Deere like the tractors? The green ones?

Marcus: Same name, and the tractors came later. John Deere's steel plow, 1837, broke through prairie soil that defeated iron plows, and Cyrus McCormick's mechanical reaper let one farmer do the harvesting work of five. The West got its machines while the East got its mills.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Okay, the mills. Lowell. This is where the girls come in, right?

Marcus: Lowell, Massachusetts. Starting in 1821, big water-powered textile mills went up along the Merrimack River. The famous part was who worked them: young farm women. The Lowell girls. Company boarding houses, strict rules, church on Sundays, the workday run by the clock.

Maya: A dorm run by the factory bell.

Marcus: With worse pay. At first the girls liked the wages and the adventure. Some even published a mill magazine, the Lowell Offering. But in the 1830s the owners cut wages and packed the boarding houses, and the girls fought back: strikes in 1834 and 1836, then the Lowell Female Labor Reform Association, the first women's labor union in the country, demanding a ten-hour day instead of twelve.

Maya: Twelve hours. And they had to organize just to get it down to ten.

Marcus: And by the 1840s many of those farm girls were being replaced by Irish immigrants who'd work for less.

Maya: [sheepish] So when the prompt says "factory system," that's Slater. He built the whole system under one roof.

Marcus: [firm] Slater brought the know-how in his head; Lowell built the whole system under one roof. If the prompt says "factory system," that's Lowell.

Maya: Then, finally, the wires.

Marcus: Samuel Morse's telegraph, 1844. First message, "What hath God wrought?", sent from Washington to Baltimore. By 1850 the lines ran alongside the railroad tracks, and an order that took days now took minutes. But remember your own mistake from two minutes ago: the telegraph showed up late to this revolution, and then it made everything faster.

Maya: Somebody has to grow what those mills spin.

Marcus: Somebody did. Box three. The cotton gin: Eli Whitney again, 1793. It ripped the seeds out of cotton fiber fast, and suddenly cotton was wildly profitable across the Deep South.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And this part always trips me up: the international slave trade gets banned in 1808. So slavery should shrink. Right?

Marcus: It should have. It didn't, and that gap is the exam's favorite trap. The ban ended imports from Africa. It didn't touch the demand. Planters pushed into Alabama, Mississippi, and Louisiana chasing fresh soil, and they fed the expansion with a domestic slave trade, buying enslaved people in the Upper South and forcing them south and west.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] How many people are we talking about?

Marcus: The National Park Service's estimate runs something like a million over the decades. The records are patchy, that's an estimate, not a census. Families torn apart in Virginia and Maryland, marched in chains to the cotton frontier. Some historians call it the Second Middle Passage.

Maya: I don't even know what to do with that number.

Marcus: Sit with it. Cotton became King Cotton: by 1860 it was more than half of everything America exported, three-quarters of the world's supply. The South grew the cotton, the North spun it, the West grew the food, and the canals and rails moved it all between them.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So which region had the leverage?

Marcus: Mutual leverage — and mutual resentment. Every region leaning into its specialty, every region more dependent and more different. The tension the Missouri Compromise tried to hold.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Box three: King Cotton's South. And all of that, the canals, the mills, the cotton — somebody paid for it. Who?

Marcus: Box four: the biggest one. The workers paid first. The revolution created a working class: people who sold their labor for wages and bought everything they needed. They worked past dark for wages that never quite covered the month, and the machines showed no mercy to a tired hand. Some people called them wage slaves, and the name tells you how it felt.

Maya: And the immigrants.

Marcus: Starting in the 1820s and swelling fast: Irish and German, the two biggest groups. The Irish, especially after the potato famine of the 1840s, crowded into northeastern cities and took the factory jobs and railroad work. The Germans were often skilled craftsmen who pushed further west, to places like Cincinnati and St. Louis. Cheap labor powered the revolution. And nativist backlash followed it.

Maya: Don't blob the immigrants, right? Irish: cities, famine, Catholic. Germans: skilled, moved west. The exam rewards the split.

Marcus: Write them split, every time. Now the middle class. Brand new. Lawyers, teachers, clerks, accountants, people who worked with their heads instead of their hands. And with the middle class came a whole new idea of home: separate spheres. The man's world was business and politics; the woman's world was the home, the cult of domesticity.

Maya: Sounds like a cage with nice curtains.

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] That was the ideal. But here's the catch students miss: it was a middle-class story. Working-class women couldn't afford it. They were in the mills and the kitchens, working because the family needed the wages. Don't universalize it.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And the Panic of 1819?

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] The crash that proved booms have teeth. Easy credit everywhere: wildcat banks printing more notes than the gold they held, the Second Bank chartered in 1816 pouring out loans, everybody speculating on western land. Then the bubble popped: foreclosures across the West, banks calling in loans, and hard times for everyone who'd bet on the boom. The market grew the country's wealth and its connections, and the crashes bigger, too.

Maya: Four boxes, let's land them. Box one, the transportation revolution: turnpikes, steamboats, the Erie Canal in 1825, and the B&O breaking ground in 1828.

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] Freight costs collapsing, regions connecting.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Box two, the factory system and the telegraph: interchangeable parts, Slater's memory-machines, the Lowell girls, who wanted, what, an eight-hour day?

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] Ten. They were working twelve.

Maya: Ten. Right. And the telegraph in 1844. Water first, wires later. Box three, King Cotton's South: the gin in 1793, cotton pushing west, and the internal slave trade moving something like a million people.

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] After the 1808 ban. That's the move to watch for.

Maya: Box four, the human cost: wage labor, Irish and German immigrants, the new middle class, the cult of domesticity, and the Panic of 1819 proving booms have teeth.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One. Your stimulus, picture this: an 1830 newspaper ad for canal freight. Buffalo to New York, a tenth of the wagon price. What's the point of a source like that? What does it explain?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The point is the transportation revolution rewiring trade. The evidence is the price collapse, roughly ninety percent, which made it profitable to ship western grain east. And that matters because once moving goods got cheap, farmers stopped growing just to survive and started growing to sell. The market revolution in one page.

Maya: Two. The cotton gin is 1793, and Congress bans the international slave trade in 1808. So why does slavery grow after the ban?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Because the gin made cotton wildly profitable, planters pushed into Alabama, Mississippi, and Louisiana, and with imports banned they built a domestic slave trade, something like a million people forced south and west. The ban cut off the supply from Africa. The demand just found a new supply.

Maya: Three. A Lowell girl works twelve-hour days under boarding-house curfews. A middle-class magazine preaches the cult of domesticity. What's the tension between those two pictures?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The cult said a woman's place was the home, separate from the rough world of work. But that was a middle-class story. The Lowell girls were in the factories because their families needed the wages. Same decade, two different womanhoods, which is why you can't universalize the ideal.

Maya: One more, fast. Why did the telegraph come last in this revolution? What had to exist first?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Water. The goods had to move before the words could matter. The canal's 1825, the telegraph's 1844. Wires sped up what the water had built.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Water moved the goods, wires moved the words —

Marcus: [professional broadcast tone] and the market made the country richer while it pulled it apart.

Maya: [confident tone] Check your boxes. Next time: the common man's president. Andrew Jackson, hero or tyrant.
