# Pronunciation: too-SAN loo-vair-TOOR (Toussaint L'Ouverture), sah-kah-juh-WEE-uh (Sacagawea), mar-BAIR-ee (Marbury), TRIP-uh-lee (Tripoli)

Maya: Last time: independence, the Constitution, and two rival parties arguing over what it meant. Now the other party gets the keys, and the new president starts breaking his own rules almost immediately. Three boxes on your sheet: the 1800 handoff, Marbury and judicial review, Louisiana plus the embargo. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes, and every one of them lands.

Marcus: Start with the handoff. 1800: Adams versus Jefferson, the two parties snapping at each other in public, and Jefferson wins. The shock wasn't the winner. It was the loser leaving quietly. A peaceful transfer of power between rival parties barely existed anywhere on earth.

Maya: Because usually the loser got stabbed?

Marcus: Something like that. Jefferson called it the Revolution of 1800. He meant a revolution of ideas. His party, the Democratic-Republicans, read the Constitution strictly: if the document doesn't spell out a power, the federal government doesn't get to use it. Small government, states in charge.

Maya: So Hamilton's national bank dies in Jefferson's world?

Marcus: In his reading, yes. The Constitution never says bank, so no bank. That was the whole strict-constructionist test.

Maya: And the country was supposed to be farmers. Everybody on their own little farm?

Marcus: That was the dream. An agrarian republic: independent farmers working their own land with their own families. His hero was the yeoman farmer, owned his land, worked it with his family, answered to nobody. Jefferson figured cities bred corruption and factories bred dependence, so keep America rural and keep it virtuous.

Maya: Did he actually shrink it, though?

Marcus: He tried. He killed the whiskey tax and cut the federal debt by about half. But this episode is where Jefferson keeps stretching the government he promised to shrink. Exhibit one is the courts.

Maya: First box down and officially checked.

Marcus: While Jefferson was shrinking things, Chief Justice John Marshall was quietly making the Supreme Court huge. But first, Adams's parting gift: the midnight judges. On his way out, Adams packed the courts with Federalist judges holding lifetime appointments.

Maya: Okay, midnight judges is dramatic.

Marcus: Jefferson read it as sabotage. So his Secretary of State, James Madison, simply didn't deliver some of the commissions. One of the stranded almost-judges, Marbury, sued Madison to get his job.

Maya: So Marbury won, right? He got the job?

Marcus: He lost. Marshall ruled the Court couldn't force Madison to hand over the commissions. But in the same ruling, Marshall claimed something enormous: the Supreme Court decides what the Constitution means. Judicial review.

Maya: He lost the small case and grabbed the huge power in the same ruling?

Marcus: Same ruling. From then on, the Court stood as the final interpreter of the Constitution. Not the states, which is what Jefferson himself had argued back in the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions.

Maya: And Jefferson was stuck with him.

Marcus: Stuck. Federal judges serve for life, so the other party's fingerprints stayed on the courts for decades. The revolution had limits.

Maya: So the courts were the one thing he couldn't shrink.

Marcus: Couldn't touch. Lifetime appointments outlasted his whole presidency.

Maya: Exhibit two.

Marcus: Louisiana. Napoleon had lost Haiti, Toussaint L'Ouverture's revolt took it from him, and he needed cash for his wars. So he offered the entire Louisiana territory for fifteen million dollars.

Maya: But Monroe only carried two million, for New Orleans.

Marcus: Two million for a port. Fifteen million for half a continent. Monroe couldn't call home for instructions, so he said yes.

Maya: He bought half a continent sight unseen.

Marcus: And doubled the country doing it.

Maya: Doubled.

Marcus: Doubled.

Maya: But he's a strict constructionist. The Constitution says nothing about buying land.

Marcus: Nothing. Jefferson knew it and bought it anyway. The land meant farms, and farms meant his agrarian republic. Then he sent Lewis and Clark to map it, two years with the Corps of Discovery, plus Zebulon Pike south.

Maya: Anybody object?

Marcus: Plenty. Critics said the territory was too big to govern, and it reopened the fight over slavery spreading west.

Maya: I once tried to navigate St. Louis with a paper map and landed in the wrong state. Two years in mostly unmapped country, I can't even picture it.

Marcus: Their Shoshone guide could probably picture it. Sacagawea traveled with the expedition the whole way.

Maya: Wait, I know her. She's on the dollar coin. The gold-colored one my grandma collects.

Marcus: The student knows one I don't. Noted, and I'm writing it down. One more stop before the embargo: Tripoli. The Barbary pirates were seizing American ships in the Mediterranean, so Jefferson sent the navy after them. Small war, loud message.

Maya: And then he banned trade with everybody?

Marcus: The Embargo Act, 1807. Britain and France were at war and both were seizing American ships, with Britain impressing American sailors into its navy. Jefferson's idea: cut off all American trade and starve them into respecting our neutrality.

Maya: And how did that go?

Marcus: Badly. American merchants and farmers couldn't sell abroad or buy from abroad, and the economy seized up. Britain barely blinked. The embargo hurt Americans more than it hurt Britain or France, the exact opposite of the plan. And here's the irony: Jefferson had hated Washington's neutrality as a younger man. As president, he kept it.

Maya: And people just smuggled around it, right?

Marcus: They did, and Jefferson sent federal power after the smugglers too. More government muscle for a policy that was already sinking.

Maya: The strict constructionist, running a trade shutdown and a smuggling crackdown.

Marcus: The contradiction is the episode.

Maya: Okay. Boxes first, then I prove I was listening.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: the 1800 handoff. Jefferson beats Adams and the loser leaves quietly. The Revolution of 1800 means strict construction and the agrarian dream.

Marcus: Checked.

Maya: Two: Marbury and judicial review. Adams's midnight judges. Madison sits on the commissions. And Marshall, wait, did Marbury get the job?

Marcus: He didn't. But the Court got judicial review: the final word on the Constitution, not the states.

Maya: Three: Louisiana plus the embargo. Fifteen million, or was it two? Monroe had two million in his pocket.

Marcus: Two was the budget for New Orleans. Fifteen bought the territory and doubled the country. It bent Jefferson's strict construction out of shape.

Maya: And the embargo proved Jefferson right about keeping government small?

Marcus: It proved the opposite. He used enormous federal power to shut down trade, and mostly punished Americans.

Maya: Two questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One: a textbook prints a cartoon of Jefferson wrestling a giant map of Louisiana while clutching a copy of the Constitution. The prompt asks: what's the cartoonist's point?

[5-second pause]

Maya: The contradiction. A strict constructionist buying land the Constitution never mentions. The point is the gap between his principles and his presidency, and why he crossed it: land for the agrarian republic.

Maya: Two: why does Marbury v. Madison matter more than Marbury himself?

[5-second pause]

Maya: Because Marbury lost his case, and Marshall still walked away with judicial review. The Court became the final interpreter of the Constitution.

Maya: Check your three boxes. Episode one in the books. Next time: the War of 1812. Impressment. An anthem. And a doctrine that told Europe to keep out.

Marcus: A president who shrank the government —

Maya: and stretched it to fit a continent.
