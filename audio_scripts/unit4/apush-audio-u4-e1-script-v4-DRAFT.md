Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: the republic got built. It survived a disputed election. A new party took power, with Jefferson promising small government. Three years later he bought half a continent.

Maya: [conversational] The one where America doubles in size. Four boxes on your sheet: Saint-Domingue, the Louisiana Purchase, strict construction, and what the purchase set in motion. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Twelve and a half minutes, and you'll check all four off. Start here: the small-government president stretched the Constitution like taffy, and he knew it.

Marcus: [measured] That contradiction is the whole episode. Jefferson believed the federal government could only do what the Constitution explicitly said. Nothing in there about buying foreign territory.

Maya: [conversational] So by his own rules, this purchase was impossible.

Marcus: [measured] By his own rules, yes. But the map was too tempting. Pressure does things to principles.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Before France could sell it, there was the mess in New Orleans. What happened there?

Marcus: [measured] The Mississippi was the economy's cork. Farmers west of the Appalachians floated grain, whiskey, and pork down to New Orleans under the right of deposit: Spain let American goods pass through and wait in the port. Then late in 1802, Spain's man shut the right down.

Maya: [incredulous] Just... closed the port?

Marcus: [measured] Closed the right. Western farmers panicked, and war fever ran in the western papers.

Maya: Your turn. Jefferson's got no real army and no money for a fight, while the West is screaming for one. You're Jefferson. Threaten war, send troops, or try something quieter? Eight seconds.

[8-second pause]

Marcus: He did the quiet thing. Sent an envoy to buy New Orleans. Not conquer it — buy it. For a president elected arguing against big federal power plays, that was the small-government answer to a big problem.

Maya: [incredulous] Wait, buy it from whom? Spain's running New Orleans.

Marcus: [measured] Right. France held Louisiana and handed it to Spain in 1762. In 1800, Napoleon strong-armed Spain into returning it. Spain protested the resale: the handover came with a promise not to transfer it to a third power. France sold it anyway.

Maya: [conversational] Spain's protest sounds very "we told you not to."

Marcus: [measured] The protest is undisputed. The promise sat in a diplomatic note, and France disputed its treaty force. Protest: fact. Legal weight: murky.

Maya: [conversational] Saint-Domingue first: the revolt that put Louisiana on the market.

Marcus: [measured] Napoleon dreamed of a French empire in the Americas, with Louisiana as its breadbasket: the wheat farm feeding the Caribbean sugar islands. The dream depended on one island: Saint-Domingue, the French colony that became Haiti. One of the richest colonies in the world, worked by enslaved Africans under conditions so brutal the colony kept importing more people to replace the dead.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So what set it off in 1791?

Marcus: [measured] Half a million enslaved people. Their leader became Toussaint Louverture, a formerly enslaved man turned brilliant general. Napoleon sent his brother-in-law Leclerc across with tens of thousands of troops to crush the revolt.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So France lost to mosquitoes?

Marcus: [measured] To Louverture's army and the mosquitoes. Don't shortchange the Haitians. But yellow fever tore through French troops who'd never encountered it, and Leclerc himself died of it in November 1802. Tens of thousands of his men died with him.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Louverture won, then?

Marcus: [measured] He was tricked first. The French lured him to a parley in the summer of 1802, seized him, and shipped him to France in chains. He died that April in a freezing mountain fortress, Fort de Joux, never seeing the independence he'd made possible. The revolution went on without him: Haiti declared independence in 1804, the first Black republic in the Americas.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And Napoleon looks at Louisiana and thinks, what's the point?

Marcus: [measured] Without Saint-Domingue, the breadbasket fed nobody. War with Britain was back on, and Napoleon needed cash. Louisiana went on the market.

Maya: [conversational] Exam worry: students write "slaves couldn't beat a European army." They could.

Marcus: [measured] Write "the Haitian Revolution forced France out of the Americas" and you're fine. Credit only the disease, and you shortchange the people who won it.

Maya: [conversational] Now the purchase itself, bought on borrowed money.

Marcus: [measured] Jefferson sent Robert Livingston to Paris with one job: buy New Orleans, just the city. Then he sent James Monroe, authorized to offer up to ten million dollars for New Orleans and West Florida.

Marcus: [measured] Then Napoleon's foreign minister, Talleyrand, stunned them: forget the city, buy all of Louisiana.

Maya: Your turn. Their orders: New Orleans, ten million, no more. Talleyrand's offering an empire for fifteen, and Napoleon could change his mind any day. Sign or wait? Eight seconds.

[8-second pause]

Marcus: They signed. The treaty was dated April 30, 1803: fifteen million dollars, mostly U.S. bonds plus debts France owed Americans, for about 828,000 square miles. Around three cents an acre. One signature, and the country had doubled.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So the news lands in Washington. Does Jefferson celebrate?

Marcus: [measured] He'd actually drafted a constitutional amendment authorizing the purchase, because his strict-construction conscience was genuinely bothered. Then he shelved it. An amendment would take months, and Napoleon could walk away in that time. The calendar killed it, not the principle.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Did Congress get a vote?

Marcus: [measured] The Senate ratified it twenty-four to seven in October 1803, and the House paid up. America didn't have fifteen million dollars. They borrowed it from British and Dutch bankers at six percent interest.

Maya: [conversational] Borrowing from the British to buy from the French. Awkward family dinner.

Marcus: [measured] The exam error is picturing Jefferson paying cash. The fifteen million was borrowed.

Maya: [conversational] Another trap: students write "Jefferson bought Louisiana from Spain." No. France. Spain's protest is the clue: you don't protest the sale of something you never owned.

Marcus: [measured] The borders were fuzzy too. The treaty drew no map, and nobody knew where Louisiana ended. Spain said the Floridas weren't included; America claimed West Florida anyway. Sixteen years of arguing, until the 1819 Adams–Onís treaty settled the border and Spain gave up Florida entirely.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] When did the handover actually happen?

Marcus: [measured] Three flags. Spain lowered its flag on November 30, 1803. France raised hers for twenty days. Then on December 20, the American flag went up.

Maya: [conversational] Then the Constitution problem: strict construction, bent but not broken.

Marcus: [measured] This one got personal for Jefferson. In a private letter to a Kentucky senator, he wrote that the Constitution "has made no provision for our holding foreign territory, still less for incorporating foreign nations into our union."

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] The president who did the deal wrote that?

Marcus: [measured] In his own hand, summer 1803. He admitted it went "beyond the Constitution," then leaned on his power to make treaties. The treaty power was hook enough.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So how could a strict constructionist do this?

Marcus: [measured] The AP answer: opportunity beat principle, and his own words prove the tension. But he never stopped calling it a stretch. He drafted the amendment, begged Congress to debate it as little as possible, and let the Senate vote carry the weight. The principle bent. In his mind it didn't break.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] What did the Federalists say? Thrilled?

Marcus: [measured] Furious, but about power, not the Constitution. New western states would vote Republican and bury Federalist New England for good. Some New England Federalists started plotting secession: the Essex Junto. They asked Aaron Burr to lead them. It went nowhere, but the panic was real.

Maya: [conversational] The small-government president buys half a continent while the Federalists plot to leave over it. Principles bent everywhere.

Marcus: [firm] Exam tip for this box: don't write "Jefferson abandoned his beliefs." Write "the purchase tested them." He bent a rule he championed, and the amendment he drafted was never voted on.

Maya: [conversational] Last box: what the purchase set in motion.

Marcus: [measured] First, Jefferson sent men to see what he'd bought: the Corps of Discovery, Lewis and Clark, 1804 to 1806, up the Missouri, across the Rockies, to the Pacific and back, guided through the hardest stretches by Sacagawea, the young Shoshone woman.

Maya: [conversational] The Sacagawea gold dollar coin.

Marcus: [measured] The gold one, yes. They came back with maps, journals, and specimens nobody in Washington had seen. The sovereignty layer: mapping it was claiming it. The expedition told Britain and Spain that America was there, had mapped it, and had named it.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Did Lewis and Clark find the Northwest Passage?

Marcus: [firm] They didn't. It doesn't exist. Don't write that they did. The expedition's real product was knowledge: routes, rivers, and the flag planted in the journals.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And the people already there?

Marcus: [measured] The hard center of this box: the purchase bought France's claim, a line on a European map. Dozens of Native nations had lived across that territory for centuries. Nobody asked them. The United States spent the next century clearing that title, treaty by treaty and war by war.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So where does slavery enter this?

Marcus: [measured] It starts here. The land was so vast that when new states started coming out of it, the fight over slavery in the West ignited. In 1820, Jefferson called the Missouri fight over slavery in this very territory "like a fire bell in the night." It "filled me with terror."

Maya: [conversational] So the purchase planted the bomb, and Missouri lit the fuse.

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] The fuse belongs to a later episode.

Maya: [conversational] Four boxes, let's land them.

Maya: [conversational] Box one: Saint-Domingue. The 1791 slave revolt under Toussaint Louverture: yellow fever destroys Napoleon's army, France quits, and that puts Louisiana on the market.

Marcus: [conversational] Good.

Maya: [conversational] Box two: the Louisiana Purchase. 1803, fifteen million, three cents an acre, doubles the country. Livingston and Monroe went to buy New Orleans and signed for everything. Three flags, twenty days.

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] Spanish to French to American. Good.

Maya: [incredulous] Box three: strict construction. Jefferson's own rule said the government only does what's listed. The purchase wasn't listed. He drafted an amendment, shelved it, and leaned on the treaty power. The vote was... twenty-four to seven?

Marcus: [conversational] Twenty-four to seven.

Maya: [conversational] Box four: what the purchase set in motion. Lewis and Clark turned mapping into a sovereignty claim while the Native nations already living there were never asked, and all that new land ignited the slavery fight.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. First: a historian claims the Haitian Revolution was the decisive cause of the Louisiana Purchase. Does the evidence support that?

[15-second pause]

Maya: Yes. Without the disaster in Saint-Domingue, Napoleon keeps his empire dream and Louisiana stays French. The war with Britain mattered, but Haiti removed the reason to keep it.

Marcus: Strong.

Maya: Second. A private 1803 letter reads: "The constitution has made no provision for our holding foreign territory." Who wrote it, and what's the point of the source?

[15-second pause]

Maya: Jefferson, to a Kentucky senator. Even the president who made the deal believed it stretched past his own reading of the Constitution. The contradiction in his own hand is the reason this letter shows up on exams.

Marcus: Nailed it.

Maya: Third: Lewis and Clark crossed the continent, 1804 to 1806. Adventure, or something colder?

[15-second pause]

Maya: Knowledge as the claim. Maps, journals, specimens: science on the surface, sovereignty underneath. The expedition told Britain and Spain the land was claimed.

Marcus: Three for three.

Maya: One more, fast. The 1819 Adams–Onís treaty settles the purchase's fuzzy borders and hands the U.S. Florida. What larger pattern is that treaty evidence of?

Maya: That treaty shows expansion by negotiation and treaty: the border drawn at a desk in 1819 because it was never drawn in 1803.

Maya: [intrigued] Check your four boxes. The small-government president bought half a continent on borrowed money — and the map is still growing. Next time: John Marshall turns the weakest branch into the boss of everyone.
