Maya: Last time: the 13th Amendment finished the legal work, but paper freedom and real freedom turned out to be different things. Now the country has to rebuild itself, and nobody agrees on how. Lincoln's 10% Plan, the Wade-Davis Bill and Johnson's plan, the Black Codes and Congress's takeover, the Reconstruction Acts and the amendments: four boxes on my sheet. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. About 12 minutes, and we'll decide which plan actually won.

Marcus: 1863. The war's still raging, and Lincoln is already planning the peace. His Proclamation of Amnesty and Reconstruction says any Southern state where ten percent of its 1860 voters swear loyalty to the Union and agree to end slavery can form a new government and come back.

Maya: Ten percent? That's it?

Marcus: That was the point. Lincoln's theory was that secession was illegal, unconstitutional, so those states never actually left. They were just rebellious states that needed to get back in line. You don't rebuild a country by punishing it.

Maya: Okay, that's the "malice toward none" guy. I saw that line on a field trip to the Lincoln Memorial, with malice toward none, with charity for all. We spent forty minutes in the gift shop and maybe ten at the actual statue.

Marcus: The Second Inaugural, 1865: no punishment, just reunion. Exam trick, though: attach every plan to a theory. Lincoln's theory was that secession was unconstitutional. That single belief explains the ten percent, the loyalty oath, all of it.

Maya: Checking the first box. Lincoln's 10% Plan: ten percent of the 1860 voters, a loyalty oath, abolish slavery, and the theory that the states never left.

Marcus: One careful thing: the 10% Plan didn't free a single person. Readmission was the whole point, not emancipation. The plan was how fast to let states back in.

Maya: And Congress thought "fast" was the problem.

Marcus: The Radical Republicans, Congress's hardliners, called ten percent a joke. Their answer was the Wade-Davis Bill, 1864: not ten percent, a majority of a state's voters had to swear loyalty, past and present, and the state had to abolish slavery before it could even start. Lincoln killed it with a pocket veto.

Maya: Hold on, pocket veto. I actually know this one. Schoolhouse Rock. The president just doesn't sign it, Congress goes home, and the bill dies. Ten days, right?

Marcus: Exactly right. Congress adjourned with the bill unsigned: no explanation required, no override possible. Now, don't mix up the two numbers: ten percent is Lincoln, lenient. A majority is Wade-Davis, Congress's punishment plan.

Maya: Then Booth's bullet changes everything.

Marcus: April 1865. Lincoln dies, and Andrew Johnson, the Tennessee Democrat Lincoln had picked as his running mate, becomes president.

Maya: Okay, so Johnson was Lincoln's guy. Same plan, same everything?

Marcus: That was the trap. Same leniency on paper, completely different president. Johnson hated the Southern planter aristocracy, he'd grown up poor and they'd looked down on him his whole life. But he had zero sympathy for Black equality either. His plan: pardon almost everyone who'd take the oath, except Confederate leaders and the richest planters. Anyone with property over twenty thousand dollars had to come ask him personally.

Maya: So Lincoln's leniency, plus Johnson's personal score-settling.

Marcus: And none of Lincoln's political touch. The states just had to admit secession was illegal, throw out their Confederate debts, accept that slavery was dead, and ratify the 13th Amendment. That was his price of readmission.

Maya: Checking the second box. Wade-Davis wanted a majority and got pocket-vetoed; Johnson pardons almost everyone, keeps Lincoln's leniency, and charges the 13th Amendment as the price of getting back in.

Marcus: Now watch the word that splits them. Johnson wasn't doing reconstruction. He was doing restoration: put the house back the way it was. Congress wanted reconstruction: rebuild it different. By the end of 1865, barely eight months after Appomattox, Johnson is telling Congress the job is basically done and the new Southern governments are legitimate. All that's left, he says, is seating their delegations.

Maya: Your turn. Before Marcus tells you: the South gets to write the rules for four million freed people, with nobody in Washington watching. What do those legislatures write? Nine seconds.

[9-second pause]

Marcus: Black Codes. 1865 and 1866. Mississippi goes first, and the other ten states of the old Confederacy follow. Vagrancy laws: arrested for standing on a road without enough money. No owning land, no running a business. Break a labor contract and you're hired out to a plantation. It looked enough like slavery that Northerners started asking if they'd won the war and lost the peace.

Maya: And then they elect their old leaders right back.

Marcus: December 1865: Congress comes back to Washington, finds Black Codes on the books, and Johnson announcing the job is done. And early in 1866, Georgia's legislature picks Alexander Stephens, the Confederacy's former vice president, for the U.S. Senate. Other states send Confederate officials too. Your turn: what does Congress do with those delegations? Nine seconds.

[9-second pause]

Marcus: Refuses to seat them. Congress votes not to seat the Southern delegations. You don't lose a war to preserve slavery and then stroll back in like nothing happened. That refusal is the turning point: Congress takes Reconstruction away from the president.

Maya: Checking the third box. Black Codes and the Congressional takeover. Black Codes, Congress refuses to seat the Southern delegations, and the 13th Amendment as the price of readmission.

Marcus: And one thing Congress builds immediately: the Freedmen's Bureau, created in early 1865 before the war even ended. Food, jobs, schools, and the schools lasted. Howard University and Morehouse College get founded during Reconstruction.

Maya: But the land? The forty-acres thing?

Marcus: The dream dies. Sherman had ordered confiscated land redistributed to freedmen, his Special Field Order Number 15. Johnson rescinded it. The "forty acres and a mule" never regained ground. And when Thaddeus Stevens tried a land-redistribution bill in 1867, forty acres for every freedman, it died in committee. Even the Radicals wouldn't vote to take private property.

Maya: Don't call the Black Codes a federal thing, right? They were Southern state laws.

Marcus: Right. That was the whole scandal.

Maya: So Johnson's done pretending, Congress is running the show, and now the amendments.

Marcus: 1866. Johnson tours the country campaigning against the 14th Amendment, telling Northern voters to reject Congress's whole program. It backfires: the midterms hand the Republicans a veto-proof supermajority, and Congress can now overrule him on everything.

Maya: So Congress just sends in the army?

Marcus: The Reconstruction Acts of 1867, passed over Johnson's veto: the South carved into five military districts, federal troops enforcing the new rules. To get back in, each state has to write a new constitution, guarantee Black men the vote, and ratify the 14th Amendment. The 14th, ratified in 1868: birthright citizenship, born here you're a citizen, and for the first time the Constitution protects you from your own state government, not just from Washington.

Maya: Before the 14th, the Bill of Rights only stopped the federal government. Now the states are bound too.

Marcus: Exactly. Then the 15th, 1870: nobody can be denied the vote on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude. In 1870, over six hundred Black men win seats in Southern state legislatures, sixteen go to the U.S. House, and Hiram Revels takes a Senate seat. But the 15th says nothing about women, and that splits the women's movement in half.

Maya: Stanton and Anthony versus everybody else.

Marcus: Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Susan B. Anthony refuse to back it: no women's vote, no support. They form the National Woman Suffrage Association in 1869. Lucy Stone's side backs the amendment anyway, arguing women's suffrage can come state by state, and they form the American Woman Suffrage Association the same year.

Maya: And Johnson? Congress just removes him, right?

Marcus: Tries to. Congress passes the Tenure of Office Act: the president can't fire a cabinet member without the Senate's say-so, written specifically to protect Secretary of War Edwin Stanton, who was on Congress's side. Johnson fires him anyway. The House impeaches him in February 1868. The Senate trial: 35 to 19 for conviction, one vote short of the two-thirds needed. Seven Republicans broke ranks and voted to acquit. Johnson stays, but he's finished. Congress runs Reconstruction without him.

Maya: Checking the fourth box. The Reconstruction Acts and the amendments: five military districts, the 14th and 15th, the women's split, and Johnson impeached and acquitted by one vote.

Marcus: So, which plan won?

Maya: Congress's. Lincoln's leniency died with him, Johnson's restoration collapsed under the Black Codes, and Congressional Reconstruction won: five military districts, three amendments, Black men voting and holding office.

Marcus: And the cost of winning? The win only existed where federal power stood. The amendments were national, but enforcement was local: troops, courts, a North that kept caring. Paper freedom was settled. Real freedom was still being negotiated, county by county.

Maya: Next time: Reconstruction, success or failure. Paper freedom versus real freedom, and we finally call it.

Marcus: Four boxes, let's land them.

Maya: Lincoln's 10% Plan: lenient because he thought secession was illegal, so ten percent of the 1860 voters plus an oath and no more slavery got a state back in. Then Wade-Davis wanted half the voters, and Lincoln pocket-vetoed it. Johnson kept the leniency but made it personal: pardons for almost everyone, the 13th as the price. The South answered with Black Codes and sent... wait, the Confederate vice president to the Senate? Georgia really did that?

Marcus: Georgia's legislature really did that. Alexander Stephens, elected to the U.S. Senate, never seated. Congress refused the whole Southern delegation.

Maya: Right. Congress refuses to seat them, takes over, passes the Reconstruction Acts with five military districts, and the 14th and 15th Amendments. The 15th leaves women out and splits Stanton and Anthony from Lucy Stone's side. Johnson gets impeached for firing Stanton and survives by one vote.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. First: Lincoln's 10% Plan was deliberately lenient. What was his theory, and what did the plan require?

[15-second pause]

Maya: His theory was that secession was unconstitutional, so the Southern states had never legally left. That's why the bar was so low: ten percent of a state's 1860 voters swearing a loyalty oath, plus abolishing slavery, and the state could form a government and come back. Second question, and this one's a source: a Mississippi law in 1865 says a Black man found on a public road without enough money can be arrested for vagrancy and hired out to a plantation. What's the point of this source, what is it showing you?

[15-second pause]

Maya: It shows how the Black Codes worked: vagrancy laws and labor contracts forcing freed people back onto plantations, basically slavery rebuilt under a different name. And it's why Congress stopped trusting the South to reconstruct itself. Third: the 15th Amendment protected Black men's right to vote, and split the women's movement. Who split, and why?

[15-second pause]

Maya: Stanton and Anthony refused to support an amendment that left women out and formed the National Woman Suffrage Association. Lucy Stone's side backed the 15th anyway, arguing women's suffrage could come state by state. The fight was over whether to take half a victory. One more, fast: Lincoln's plan asked ten percent of a state's 1860 voters to swear loyalty. Wade-Davis demanded a bare majority. What did that gap say about who each side trusted?

Maya: Lincoln trusted a small loyal core to pull a state back fast — secession was illegal, so the state never really left. Congress trusted only a Unionist majority, because ten percent looked like letting the rebels run the reunion themselves.

Marcus: Lincoln planned for reunion. Johnson planned for restoration. Congress planned for reconstruction. And the plan that won is the one we're still arguing about.
