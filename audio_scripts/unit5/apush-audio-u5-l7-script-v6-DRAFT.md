# Pronunciation: Antietam (an-TEE-tum), Appomattox (ap-uh-MAT-uks), Vicksburg (VIKS-burg), Chamberlain (CHAYM-ber-lin), Tecumseh (tih-KUM-seh), Minié (min-YAY), Spotsylvania (spot-sil-VAYN-yuh), habeas corpus (HAY-bee-us KOR-pus), Greeley (GREE-lee)

Maya: Last time: the Confederacy fired on Fort Sumter, and both sides expected a short war. They were wrong. Bull Run, 1861: Washington society packed picnic baskets to watch the war end in an afternoon, and drove home through a stampede. Four boxes: why the North didn't win quickly, the turning points of 1863, the homefront, and how Grant and Sherman broke the South. Circle the ones you couldn't explain. Fourteen minutes, check all four off.

Marcus: The Union army was green, mostly ninety-day volunteers under Irvin McDowell, a West Pointer whose plan was too complex for green troops. The Confederates held, Stonewall Jackson earned his nickname standing firm as Union lines broke, and the picnickers fled back to Washington.

Maya: But why didn't the North win quickly? They had everything. Wasn't the population edge something like five to one?

Marcus: Closer to two and a half to one — and roughly three and a half million of the South's people were enslaved, whose forced labor sustained the Confederate war effort. The North held most of the factories, banks, railroads, and the navy. But the South had better generals early and a defensive war with short supply lines, and Lincoln couldn't find a general who would attack.

Maya: Firing generals mid-war sounds like panic, not strategy.

Marcus: McClellan was the worst of it: bigger army, better equipment, and he wouldn't move. Lincoln said if McClellan wasn't going to use the army, he'd like to borrow it. On the Peninsula in 1862, McClellan convinced himself Lee outnumbered him and dug in until Lee drove him off. That timidity cost the Union dearly; exactly how much time, historians still argue.

Maya: So all that industry, all those men — wasted for two years?

Marcus: Until someone used them. The South bet on King Cotton: Britain needed its cotton enough to intervene. But Britain had stockpiled a surplus, needed Northern grain, and bought from Egypt and India instead. No European power intervened, so the Anaconda Plan worked unanswered: blockade the South, take the Mississippi, squeeze.

Maya: Okay, but here's what I don't get. Lincoln's the Great Emancipator. Why wasn't ending slavery the war aim from the start?

Marcus: Because it wasn't, and why it changed is the most testable story here. It starts with the enslaved themselves. From the war's first months, they fled to Union lines in large numbers, forcing emancipation onto the agenda. Congress answered in steps: Confiscation Acts in 1861 and 1862, declaring enslaved people used by the Confederacy "contraband" the Union could seize. Lincoln didn't like the acts, but he didn't veto them.

Maya: So Congress is freeing people and Lincoln's just... watching?

Marcus: He's waiting, for politics and for a victory. Then in August 1862, editor Horace Greeley publishes an open letter demanding Lincoln act on slavery.

Maya: And Lincoln answers him publicly?

Marcus: With the perfect exam hook. "My paramount object in this struggle is to save the Union, and is not either to save or to destroy slavery. If I could save the Union without freeing any slave I would do it." Union first, written weeks before the Preliminary Proclamation, with the draft already in his desk drawer — closing with his personal wish "that all men everywhere could be free."

Maya: So "Great Emancipator" is... complicated.

Marcus: It's honest, once you see both halves. Enslaved people forced the question, Congress answered, Lincoln finally made it policy. That's this episode's story.

Maya: Checking the first box. The North had the resources but not the army, and the war's purpose was changing underneath everyone.

Marcus: September 1862, Antietam. Lee invades Maryland; the result is the bloodiest single day in American history, about twenty-three thousand casualties. Tactically a draw, strategically a Union victory: it ended British and French talk of recognizing the Confederacy.

Maya: Your turn. You're Lincoln after Antietam, sitting on the Emancipation Proclamation for months. Why wait for a victory? Why not just issue it?

[8-second pause]

Marcus: Because a proclamation after a defeat looks like desperation. A victory lets Lincoln reframe the war around freedom: the preliminary proclamation in September 1862, the final one on January first, 1863. Exam trap: it didn't free every enslaved person — only those in Confederate-held territory, exempting the border states.

Marcus: In November 1863, at Gettysburg, Lincoln speaks for two minutes. Two hundred seventy-two words: "government of the people, by the people, for the people, shall not perish from the earth." The new war aim, carved into the national memory.

Marcus: Now the technology, because it explains the slaughter ahead. The rifled musket outranged the old smoothbore, so shoulder-to-shoulder charges became suicide against entrenched defenders. At Hampton Roads, the Confederate ironclad Virginia destroyed two wooden Union warships in a day, then fought the Monitor to a draw. The age of the wooden warship was ending. And the telegraph let Lincoln wire his generals in near real time.

Maya: So attacking was just worse, for everyone?

Marcus: For everyone. Remember that at Gettysburg. July 1863. Lee invades Pennsylvania. On the second day it nearly goes the other way at Little Round Top, the rocky hill anchoring the Union line's left end, where Chamberlain's Maine regiment runs out of ammunition and answers with a bayonet charge downhill.

Maya: Desperate. The bayonet charge from the Gettysburg movie is the only reason I know Chamberlain's name — Jeff Daniels.

Marcus: Then Pickett's Charge: around twelve thousand men across an open field into artillery and rifled muskets. A few reach the stone wall at the Angle, the center of the Union line, but the charge breaks. Lee retreats, and he never mounts another major invasion of the North.

Maya: Hold on, "never another invasion"? The war went two more years.

Marcus: A raid, yes. Jubal Early's men reached Washington's outskirts in 1864. But never another full invasion.

Maya: And Vicksburg the same week.

Marcus: Grant besieges Vicksburg about six weeks until it surrenders on July fourth. The Mississippi is Union, the Confederacy cut in half.

Marcus: On a long-essay question, argue Gettysburg and Vicksburg, then complicate it with Antietam. Students mix these up: Gettysburg ended Lee's offensives; Vicksburg broke the Confederacy in half.

Maya: Checking the second box. Antietam opens the door, then Gettysburg and Vicksburg in the same July week.

Marcus: Third box: the homefront. The Confederacy drafts first, in 1862, but the exemptions say who the war was for. The Twenty Negro Law excused one white man per large plantation, the rich hired substitutes, and armies impressed supplies and enslaved laborers off farms. Poor whites called it a rich man's war and a poor man's fight.

Maya: "A rich man's war and a poor man's fight." That was the phrase in the streets, North and South.

Marcus: 1863. A three-hundred-dollar commutation fee bought your way out, hiring a substitute was separate. Same resentment. July 1863, New York City: draft riots. Irish immigrants, furious at the fee and at emancipation, turn on Black New Yorkers in the streets. Federal troops fresh from Gettysburg put it down.

Maya: And it wasn't just the North. Richmond, April 1863: women riot over bread prices. The Confederacy printed money until it was nearly worthless, and inflation gutted Southern households.

Marcus: While the war raged, Congress legislated the future. With Southern Democrats gone, Republicans passed the economic program the South had blocked for years: the free-labor vision. Homestead Act: a hundred sixty acres to any settler who'd farm it five years. Morrill Act: land and money for agricultural colleges.

Maya: Building colleges mid-war?

Marcus: Pacific Railway Act: land grants for a transcontinental railroad. National Banking Acts: a uniform national currency. The Union wasn't just winning a war; it was building the country's future.

Maya: Wartime dissent ran both directions, then. Draft riots in the North, bread riots in the South.

Marcus: But don't spread the misery evenly: Southern civilians paid for a war their government could barely manage, while the Northern economy actually grew.

Maya: And the North wasn't united behind Lincoln either. The draft riots, an election he expected to lose.

Marcus: One more homefront power: Lincoln suspended habeas corpus, the right to challenge imprisonment before a judge. Thousands arrested without prompt trial, mostly civilians. Constitutionally questionable; he argued the emergency justified it.

Maya: Checking the third box. Drafts, riots North and South, habeas corpus stretched thin, and Congress building the country's future mid-war.

Marcus: Now Grant. His name was made at Fort Donelson ("unconditional surrender") and at Vicksburg.

Maya: That's the Grant Lincoln wouldn't fire?

Marcus: After Shiloh's bloodbath in 1862, when critics demanded his removal, Lincoln reportedly said he couldn't spare this man, because he fights. In March 1864 he's promoted lieutenant general, the first to hold that full rank since Washington, and put over all Union armies.

Maya: A lot of power for one general. Handing one man the whole war doesn't worry you?

Marcus: It worried plenty of people. What did he do with it? He used it.

Marcus: Attrition. The Overland Campaign: Wilderness, Spotsylvania, then Cold Harbor on June third, a frontal assault on entrenched riflemen that kills or wounds thousands in the first hour. Grant keeps coming anyway. The North can replace its losses. The South can't.

Maya: At what price, though? Feeding men in, battle after battle?

Marcus: His critics called him a butcher, and Cold Harbor backs them up.

Marcus: Grant sends Sherman toward Atlanta, a railroad hub. Sherman's weapon is hard war, what textbooks call total war: destroying the South's economic base and its civilians' will to back the war. He ordered Atlanta's civilians out. Atlanta falls September second, 1864, after a long siege. That October, Sherman wired Grant he would "make Georgia howl." In November, as Sherman leaves for Savannah, much of the city burns. Then the March to the Sea.

Maya: Did Sherman actually say "war is hell"?

Marcus: Not during the war. What he told Atlanta's mayor was "war is cruelty, and you cannot refine it."

Maya: Your turn. August 1864: the war looks endless, and on August 23rd Lincoln writes a sealed memorandum saying it seems "exceedingly probable" he won't be reelected, with cabinet members signing it sight unseen. What could flip Northern opinion?

[8-second pause]

Marcus: Atlanta falls September second, morale surges, and in November Lincoln defeats George McClellan — yes, that McClellan, the fired general, now the Democratic candidate. The platform called the war a failure; McClellan disowned the plank, but a Democratic win would have emboldened the peace movement. Follow the chain: Atlanta decides the election, and the election decides the war gets fought to a Union victory.

Marcus: Then the end comes fast. In April 1865 Lee surrenders at Appomattox Court House. Generous terms: Confederates keep their horses, officers keep their sidearms, everyone goes home. Five days later Lincoln is assassinated. The death toll, near seven hundred thousand and still being revised, makes it the deadliest war in American history.

Maya: So on a short-answer question, contrast them: Grant's attrition grinds down the enemy army, battle after battle. Sherman's hard war targets the economy and morale behind the army. Then note the debate: was he fighting the army, or the people?

Maya: Checking the fourth box. Grant's attrition and Sherman's hard war. Atlanta falls, Lincoln wins, Appomattox ends it.

Maya: Four boxes, let's land them: the North's advantages took years to convert; 1863 turned the war; the homefront cracked while Washington legislated; Grant and Sherman broke the South, with Atlanta's fall shaping the election. And the war aim shifted because... the Proclamation? Or was it the Confiscation Acts first?

Marcus: The Acts and the self-emancipation first — the Proclamation was Lincoln catching up to what enslaved people and Congress had already started.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. First: why didn't the North's material advantages produce a quick victory?

[15-second pause]

Maya: Advantages don't win wars by themselves; they convert through trained armies and willing leadership. The proof: green volunteers routing at Bull Run, McClellan idle outside Richmond. A defensive South with better early generals stalemated a stronger but clumsier North for years.

Maya: Second: argue Gettysburg and Vicksburg as the turning point, then complicate it.

[15-second pause]

Maya: July 1863 is the turning point: Gettysburg ends Lee's invasions; Vicksburg takes the Mississippi and splits the Confederacy. Together they destroyed the South's offensive capacity and territorial unity. The complication: Antietam, a year earlier, kept Britain out and made emancipation a Union war aim.

Maya: Third. A Southern newspaper prints Sherman's own line: "War is cruelty, and you cannot refine it." What's the point of this source? Give one piece of evidence that supports the claim Sherman fought the Southern people, not just its army, and one that complicates it.

[15-second pause]

Maya: The evidence cuts both ways. Supporting it: Sherman ordered Atlanta's civilians out and destroyed civilian property; "make Georgia howl" targeted the home front's will. Complicating it: his orders aimed at military-useful property, and few civilians were killed. Hard war broke the South's capacity and will to fight, which is why historians still argue about who it was fought against.

Maya: One more, fast. Why did the draft stir resentment in both the North and the South?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Both drafts looked rigged for the rich: the Twenty Negro Law excused one white man per large plantation; the Union's three-hundred-dollar commutation fee let the wealthy buy out. When the burden tracks wealth, "a rich man's war and a poor man's fight" stops being a slogan and starts being why men riot.

Maya: One thing to carry forward: this war changed what it was about while it was being fought — and the people it was fought over helped change it.

Marcus: Next time: the hundred and eighty thousand Black soldiers who fought for the Union because of the Proclamation, including the 54th Massachusetts — and what they were up against.
