# APUSH Unit 5, Lesson 11: Jim Crow & the New South
## Script v2 DRAFT — Maya + Marcus Interview (expanded from v1 LOCKED)

**Format:** Interview | **Target:** 1440 words, ~8.5 min @ 170 WPM | **CED:** 5.11

# Read note: [5-second pause] = five seconds of silence for the self-test; convert to real silence before TTS. Do not read bracket tags aloud.
# Production note: the closing tagline's em dash is a held breath — do not rush it.
# Pronunciation: Tourgée (toor-ZHAY), nadir (NAY-der), Plessy (PLESS-ee).

Marcus: 1877. Federal troops leave the South. Reconstruction is over. And Southern states set out to undo everything. Not by repealing the amendments. By working around them. The result is Jim Crow.

Maya: Last time: sharecropping, the New South economy, and how debt kept Black farmers tied to the land. This time: the legal machine that locked it all in. 1892. New Orleans. A man named Homer Plessy buys a first-class ticket, sits in the whites-only car, and gets arrested on purpose. He was one-eighth Black, and the railroad was in on the plan. Three boxes today: the disfranchisement machinery, Plessy v. Ferguson, and Ida B. Wells versus lynching. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. About eight minutes, and you'll know exactly how the South rebuilt white rule without repealing a single amendment.

Marcus: The 15th Amendment bans denying votes by race. But states always controlled the mechanics: registration, qualifications, procedures. So Southern states built a machine around the amendment. Poll taxes. You pay to vote. Literacy tests. You read and interpret the Constitution to the registrar's satisfaction.

Maya: That "to the registrar's satisfaction" did all the work.

Marcus: It's doing all the work. In 1890 Mississippi wrote the template, the Mississippi Plan. A poll tax plus a literacy test with an understanding clause: the registrar decides whether you understood what you read.

Maya: So no standard. He just decides.

Marcus: Right. Registrars waved white men through and failed Black men on purpose. And the poll tax: how much did it cost? A dollar or two.

Maya: Sounds like nothing.

Marcus: Several days' wages for a farm laborer. For a sharecropper already in debt, the difference between voting and eating. It hit poor whites too, but registrars had total discretion, so poor whites mostly got waved through.

Maya: In Selma, they make her name every county judge in Alabama before she can register. Is that real?

Marcus: The literacy test, in action. The questions were whatever the registrar felt like asking. Name every judge. Read a section of the Constitution aloud. Copy it down. One wrong answer and you failed, if he wanted you to fail.

Maya: And grandfather clauses.

Marcus: Louisiana's 1898 constitution pioneered it. If your grandfather could vote before 1867, you skip the tax and the test. In theory it's color-blind. In practice every white man's grandfather could vote, and nearly every Black man's grandfather was enslaved.

Maya: That only works if your grandfather was free.

Marcus: That was the sort it was designed to make. And there was a fourth tool: white primaries. Once Black voters were locked out, the South was a one-party region, and the Democratic primary was the only election that mattered. Black voters were barred from voting in it.

Maya: So you can't enter the room where the real decision happens.

Marcus: The amendment said nothing about primaries. That was the whole design.

Maya: Checking the first box. Disfranchisement machinery: Mississippi's template, registrar discretion, the grandfather clause, white primaries.

Maya: So the machine was legal. Anyone try to break it in court?

Marcus: They did. 1892: a citizens' committee in New Orleans picked Homer Plessy deliberately. He was seven-eighths white, light enough to pass, but under Louisiana law one-eighth Black meant Black. He bought the ticket, sat in the whites-only car, announced himself. The conductor had him arrested. They wanted this fight.

Maya: And the railroad just let it happen?

Marcus: The railroad went along with it. Separate cars cost them money: an extra car on every train. So both sides played their parts to get the law in front of the Supreme Court.

Maya: I once got yelled at for sitting in the wrong section of an Amtrak. My uncle picked the wrong car. That conductor was not running a test case, just annoyed.

Marcus: Different century, same seating politics. Plessy's lawyer, Tourgée, argued the 14th Amendment's equal protection killed the Separate Car Act. In 1896 the Court disagreed. Seven to one. One justice sat it out. Justice Brown wrote that separation was fine as long as facilities were equal. Separate but equal.

Maya: Seven to one. Who was the one?

Marcus: Justice Harlan. His dissent has the line students still quote: "Our Constitution is color-blind, and neither knows nor tolerates classes among citizens."

Maya: What a line that is.

Marcus: It took until 1954, Brown v. Board, for the Court to agree with him. Sixty years of legal cover for segregation.

Maya: I don't get why the North let this happen. They won the war.

Marcus: It's the hardest question of the era. Part of it was the Court itself: the justices kept narrowing federal power. In the Civil Rights Cases of 1883 they struck down a law banning discrimination in public places. The Court wasn't a brake. It was an accomplice.

Maya: Part of it?

Marcus: Part was the political deal: the disputed election of 1876, federal troops withdrawn, Reconstruction over. And part was the North moving on. Northern whites had their own racism and their own booms to chase. Black rights stopped being worth the fight to them. Nobody's fully sure of the balance, but the Court, the deal, and the retreat all played a part.

Marcus: The legal machine was one pillar. Terror was the other. From roughly 1880 into the 1930s, white mobs lynched thousands of Black Americans, often in front of crowds, almost never punished.

Maya: And Ida B. Wells said it out loud.

Marcus: A Black journalist in Memphis. She investigated lynchings herself and published the numbers, proving the excuse was a lie. Lynching wasn't about punishing crimes. It was about keeping Black people from voting and from owning land — from rising at all.

Maya: So she publishes that, and...

Marcus: In 1892 a mob destroyed her printing press. She fled North and kept writing from Chicago. Historians call this stretch the nadir: the lowest point of Black life in America.

Maya: Lower than slavery? I don't buy that.

Marcus: It's not worse than slavery. Nothing afterward was worse than owning people. But the nadir was a choice. Slavery was the system the country was born into. The nadir was the system it built after emancipation.

Marcus: Black leaders fought over the answer. Booker T. Washington's Atlanta Compromise speech in 1895: build wealth, get educated, and social power follows. Accept segregation for now, take the economic deal.

Maya: And Wells said you can't negotiate with terror.

Marcus: She did. Du Bois later joined her against Washington: same goal, full equality, different strategy for a system built to stop them.

Maya: Under it all, the New South economy: mills, Birmingham steel, but still cotton and debt.

Marcus: Still sharecropping underneath. And poor whites: standing above Black people gave them status, but the debt trap caught them too. The planters and the industrialists got the profits. Poor whites got racism as a consolation prize.

Maya: The whole machine. Three boxes, let's land them.

Maya: First: disfranchisement machinery. After 1877, Mississippi writes the template in 1890: poll taxes and literacy tests, then Louisiana adds the grandfather clause in... 1898?

Marcus: 1898. And white primaries as the fourth tool.

Maya: Checking the first box. Second: Plessy v. Ferguson, 1896. A staged test case, seven to one, Harlan alone dissenting: "Our Constitution is color-blind."

Marcus: And folded under that box: why the North let it happen: the Court, the 1877 deal, and the retreat.

Maya: Checking the second box. Third: Ida B. Wells and lynching. She proved lynching was terror, not justice. Her press was destroyed, and she kept fighting from Chicago. The nadir: not worse than slavery, but a choice.

Marcus: And underneath it all: Washington says build wealth first, Wells and Du Bois say confront. Poor whites got the consolation prize.

Maya: Checking the third box.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. First, a stimulus-style one. A historian hands you a 1901 Alabama voter-registration form. The applicant has to read a section of the state constitution aloud and explain it. What's the point of this source?

[5-second pause]

Maya: It shows the disfranchisement machinery in action: a literacy test with registrar discretion, built to fail Black applicants and pass white ones without ever naming race.

Maya: Second: Harlan lost seven to one. Why does his dissent still matter?

[5-second pause]

Maya: Because his color-blind Constitution argument became the legal ground the Court finally stood on in Brown v. Board in 1954. He lost the case and won the argument, sixty years later.

Maya: Third: Wells's press got destroyed for what she published. What had her reporting proved about lynching?

[5-second pause]

Maya: That the excuse was a lie. It wasn't about punishing crimes, just terror to keep Black people from voting and owning land.

Maya: Check your three boxes. Next time: the Gilded Age: the North's industrial boom, built on top of everything we just covered.

Marcus: The machine, the ruling, and the woman who refused it — the South's answer to Reconstruction.
