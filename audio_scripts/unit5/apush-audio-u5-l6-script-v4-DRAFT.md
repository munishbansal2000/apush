Maya: Last time: seven debates in Illinois, and the loser won bigger than the winner. This time the whole country votes, and the loser is the Union itself. Four candidates, one election, and seven states walk out before the winner even takes office. Three boxes on your sheet: the Democratic split, the forty-percent victory, and secession winter. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes, and you'll check all three off.

Marcus: April 1860. Charleston, South Carolina. The Democratic Party holds its convention to pick a presidential candidate. Southern delegates demand one plank in the platform: a federal slave code for the territories, Congress actively protecting slavery wherever the flag flies. The Northern Democrats refuse.

Maya: And then they just walk out?

Marcus: Fifty Southern delegates stand up and leave. The convention can't nominate anyone. They try again in Baltimore six weeks later, but the Southerners hold their own convention. Two conventions, two platforms, two candidates. The party that held the country together since Andrew Jackson is broken.

Maya: So Charleston's where the party breaks. What started it?

Marcus: But the break didn't start in Charleston. It started six months earlier, in October 1859, at Harpers Ferry, Virginia. John Brown, the same John Brown from Bleeding Kansas, leads eighteen men to seize the federal arsenal. His plan: take the weapons, arm enslaved people, and start a rebellion.

Maya: And it fails immediately.

Marcus: Within days. Robert E. Lee leads the federal troops that corner him. Brown is captured, tried, and hanged. But his last words become famous: the crimes of this guilty land will never be purged away but with blood. The country splits over his corpse: the South sees proof that Northern abolitionists will invade and kill, the North sees a martyr. By the time the Democrats meet in Charleston, trust is already gone.

Maya: So Charleston is the symptom, not the cause.

Marcus: Exactly. Now the Republicans meet in Chicago. William Seward is the frontrunner, but too radical for some. Abraham Lincoln is the compromise choice: from Illinois, a swing state, moderate enough to win and principled enough to hold the party. Three ballots, and Lincoln takes it. The Northern Democrats nominate Stephen Douglas on popular sovereignty. The Southern Democrats nominate John Breckinridge, the sitting vice president, on a pro-slavery platform. And a fourth group, the Constitutional Union Party, nominates John Bell of Tennessee. Their platform is one sentence: the Constitution as it is, the Union as it is, and the enforcement of the laws.

Maya: So basically, "let's all calm down" as a platform.

Marcus: Four candidates: Lincoln the Republican, Douglas the Northern Democrat, Breckinridge the Southern Democrat, Bell the peacemaker. Lincoln wins with about forty percent of the popular vote.

Maya: Forty percent? In student council you need a majority. How do you win with forty?

Marcus: The Electoral College doesn't care about percentages. It cares about states. Lincoln sweeps the North: eighteen free states, a hundred and eighty electoral votes against a hundred and twenty-three combined for the other three. He wins without carrying a single slave state. In ten Southern states, no Republican ticket was even printed. Back then the parties printed their own ballots, so with no Republican organization in the South, there was simply nothing to vote for.

Maya: He wasn't on the ballot in ten states and still won?

Marcus: And here's the part that ends the argument about the split. Even if the Democrats had run one candidate, Lincoln still wins the Electoral College. The math was demographic, not accidental. The North had the people.

Maya: So the real story is the numbers, not the man?

Marcus: Underneath, the trend: eighteen free states to fifteen slave. Minnesota and Oregon just came in free. Twenty-two million people in the North against nine million in the South, and four million of those nine are enslaved people who can't vote. Every year immigrants pour into Northern cities. Every year new Western states come in free.

Maya: So it's not just Lincoln. It's the future.

Marcus: Lincoln is the symptom. From the Southern perspective, the disease is democracy itself when you're permanently outnumbered. Even if Lincoln keeps his promise, and he pledged not to touch slavery where it existed, the next Republican won't. Or the one after that. The South sees a future where it's a permanent minority, and slavery gets strangled slowly instead of defended.

Maya: I assumed the South seceded because Lincoln was going to free the slaves. Wrong, isn't it.

Marcus: Completely wrong, and it's the most common mistake on this topic. Lincoln promised, explicitly, that he had no constitutional power to touch slavery in the states. The South didn't believe him, and more importantly, they didn't need to. They read the census, not the speeches.

Maya: December 1860. South Carolina goes first.

Marcus: December 20th. South Carolina's convention votes to secede, and its declaration gives two reasons: the North intends war against slavery until it ceases throughout the United States, and if that happens, the equal rights of the states will be lost. Six more states follow by February: Mississippi, Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, Texas. They form the Confederate States of America and elect Jefferson Davis president.

Maya: And the famous question: was it about slavery or states' rights?

Marcus: Read their own words. Every seceding state's declaration names the protection of slavery. And Alexander Stephens, the Confederate vice president, gives the Cornerstone Speech in March. He says the Confederacy's cornerstone rests on the great truth that the Black man is not equal to the white man, that slavery is his natural condition. The states' rights argument came later, after the war. At the time, they were explicit.

Maya: That's not ambiguous at all.

Marcus: It isn't. Now, both sides also had a legal theory. The South argued compact theory: the states made the Union, so the states can unmake it. Lincoln answered that the Union is perpetual, older than the Constitution, and no state can leave it. That argument gets settled the hard way.

Maya: So nobody tried one last deal?

Marcus: One last try at peace. Senator John Crittenden of Kentucky proposes six constitutional amendments and four resolutions: restore the old Missouri line at thirty-six thirty and extend it all the way to the Pacific, protect slavery permanently where it exists. Lincoln kills it privately. He won't accept any expansion of slavery, and without him the Republicans won't touch it. It fails in the Senate in January.
Marcus: Proposed December eighteenth, it died because the sides couldn't meet: Republicans refused any new slave territory, and the cotton states had already decided that leaving beat bargaining.

Maya: And Buchanan?

Marcus: James Buchanan, the outgoing president, spends the secession winter paralyzed. He says secession is illegal, but he also says he has no power to coerce a state back. So he does nothing. Four more states will join the Confederacy after Lincoln takes office and calls for troops, but Buchanan's months are just drift.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: the Democratic split. Charleston, April 1860, the Southern walkout over the slave-code plank, two conventions, four candidates. And the fuse was Harpers Ferry, October 1859: Brown's raid, the trial, the gallows, martyr in the North and invader in the South.

Marcus: Checked.

Maya: Two: the forty-percent victory. Lincoln sweeps the North for a hundred and eighty electoral votes, carries zero slave states, isn't on the ballot in ten Southern states because the parties printed their own tickets. And the split didn't hand it to him: one Democrat still loses.

Marcus: On the sheet.

Maya: Three: secession winter. South Carolina, December 20th, two reasons in its declaration, six states follow by February, Davis elected, Stephens's Cornerstone Speech says the quiet part out loud. Crittenden's last compromise fails, Buchanan drifts.

Marcus: And that's the set.

Maya: Two questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One: how did Lincoln win the presidency with forty percent of the popular vote?

[5-second pause]

Maya: The Electoral College rewards states, not percentages. Lincoln swept the free states for a hundred and eighty electoral votes while the opposition split three ways, and the North's population meant even a united Democratic ticket couldn't stop him.

Maya: Two: South Carolina's secession declaration says the North means war on slavery and the equal rights of the states will be lost. A prompt asks whether secession was about slavery or states' rights. What's the move?

[5-second pause]

Maya: Read their own documents. The declarations and Stephens's Cornerstone Speech name the protection of slavery explicitly. The states' right being claimed was the right to keep slavery. That's the complexity point, not a contradiction.

Maya: Check your three boxes. Episode six in the books. Next time: the war itself. Why the North didn't win quickly, the turning points, and how it ends at Appomattox.

Marcus: The system couldn't process the result —

Maya: so the country broke instead.
