# APUSH Unit 5, Lesson 7: Civil War Turning Points
## Script v2 EXPANDED DRAFT — Story Mode (Marcus-led, Maya interjects)

**Format:** Story Mode (Marcus-led, Maya interjects) | **Target:** 1440 words, 8 min @ 180 WPM | **CED:** 5.8
# Read note: [5-second pause] marks are production notes for real silence in the self-test — they never go to the voice.

---

Maya: Last time: the Confederacy fired on Fort Sumter, and both sides expected a short war. They were wrong. Three boxes today: why the North didn't win quickly, the turning points of 1863, and how total war broke the South. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes, and you'll check all three off.

Marcus: Everyone thought the Civil War would be quick. At Bull Run in 1861, Washington society packed picnic baskets and rode out to watch the battle. They expected an afternoon's entertainment. Instead they got a humiliating Union defeat and the realization this would be a long, bloody war.

Maya: Why didn't the North win quickly? They had everything. Population, factories, railroads, the navy.

Marcus: They had the advantages. But wars aren't won on paper. At Bull Run, the Union army was green, the officers were political appointees, the plan was confused. The Confederates held their ground. Stonewall Jackson earned his nickname there, standing like a stone wall while Union troops broke and ran. The picnickers fled back to Washington. Humiliating.

Marcus: Both sides realized it would be long and brutal. Five times the population. Most of the banks, factories, railroads. Control of the navy. But the South had better generals early. Robert E. Lee. Stonewall Jackson. Brilliant, decisive, aggressive. And the South was fighting a defensive war. Shorter supply lines. Home-field advantage. A population willing to fight to the death.

Maya: And Lincoln couldn't find a general who would fight.

Marcus: He cycled through them. McDowell, McClellan, others. McClellan was the worst. He had a bigger army, better equipment, and he wouldn't attack. Lincoln finally said if McClellan wasn't going to use the army, he'd like to borrow it. The timidity dragged the war on for years.

Maya: So McClellan was just scared?

Marcus: Cautious to a fault, fed bad intelligence. The Peninsula campaign, 1862: McClellan lands over 100,000 men outside Richmond, then convinces himself Lee has twice that number. He didn't. McClellan dug trenches instead of attacking, and Lee drove him off. Lincoln watched a winning hand get folded.

Maya: Checking the first box. Why the North didn't win quickly. Southern generals, defensive war, Lincoln's general problem.

Marcus: The South also bet on King Cotton — that Britain and France needed Southern cotton so badly they'd intervene. But Britain had taken India in 1858 and could grow cotton there instead. The bet failed. No European power intervened.

Maya: So the South was isolated.

Marcus: Diplomatically, yes. The Union strategy was the Anaconda Plan. Blockade the South, take the Mississippi River, squeeze until it collapsed.

Maya: Did the squeeze actually work, or just look good on paper?

Marcus: Eventually. But the turning points came in 1863. September 1862 first. Antietam. Bloodiest single day in American history. 23,000 casualties. Lee invades Maryland. It's a narrow Union victory, but it's enough.

Maya: Enough for what?

Marcus: For Lincoln to issue the Emancipation Proclamation. He'd been waiting for a victory so it wouldn't look like desperation. Antietam gives him the opening. But that's next episode.

Maya: Antietam. September 1862, 23,000 casualties — and opens the door for Emancipation.

Marcus: July 1863. The hinge week. Gettysburg, July first through third. Lee invades Pennsylvania. Three-day bloodbath. Pickett's Charge, twelve thousand five hundred men marching across an open field into Union artillery. Broken.

Maya: Twelve thousand men, walking into cannon fire?

Marcus: The day before, it nearly went the other way. Little Round Top, the Union's far left flank. Colonel Joshua Chamberlain's Maine regiment ran out of ammunition holding the hill, so he ordered a bayonet charge — downhill, against a larger force — and it broke the Confederate attack. If that hill falls, the Union left is in real trouble.

Maya: A bayonet charge? In 1863?

Marcus: Desperation invents tactics. Chamberlain got the Medal of Honor for it. Lee retreats. The South never invades the North again.

Maya: And Vicksburg the same week.

Marcus: Grant besieges Vicksburg for two months until twenty-nine thousand Confederates surrender. The Union takes the Mississippi and the Confederacy is cut in half. Lincoln later put Grant in command of all Union armies.

Maya: Then Gettysburg and Vicksburg. July 1863: Lee retreats, the Mississippi is taken, the Confederacy split.

Marcus: Four months later, at the Gettysburg cemetery dedication, Lincoln gives a 272-word speech: "government of the people, by the people, for the people, shall not perish from the earth." He reframes the war — not just saving the Union, but testing whether democracy itself can survive.

Maya: The Gettysburg Address.

Marcus: The most famous speech in American history. And it connects directly to the turning points. The dead at Gettysburg gave Lincoln the moment to define what they died for.

Maya: Grant's strategy is attrition. Non-stop battles. Cut off supplies. Wear them down. The North can replace soldiers. The South can't. It's brutal math. Southern morale collapses. 1863, bread riots. Women looting grocery stores because inflation made food unaffordable. Resentment that the Confederate government demanded sacrifice but couldn't provide basic needs.

Maya: What about the drafts? Both sides had them, right?

Marcus: The Confederacy drafted men first, in 1862 — but states'-rights governors dragged their feet, and the exemptions told you who the war was for. One white planter for every twenty enslaved people didn't have to serve. The rich could hire substitutes. Poor whites called it a rich man's war and a poor man's fight.

Maya: The Union draft wasn't better.

Marcus: Same substitute provision. Same resentment. 1863, New York City draft riots. Four days. Irish working-class immigrants, furious about the three-hundred-dollar substitute fee that let the rich buy their way out, furious about Emancipation turning it into a war for Black freedom. They lynch Black New Yorkers. Federal troops fresh from Gettysburg have to put it down.

Maya: That's horrifying.

Marcus: Lincoln went further. He suspended habeas corpus — historians estimate between ten and fifteen thousand civilians arrested without a prompt trial, suspected Confederate sympathizers mostly, not draft dodgers. Constitutionally questionable, but he argued the emergency justified it.

Maya: Then Sherman.

Marcus: Grant sends William Tecumseh Sherman to take Atlanta. It's a major railroad hub. Sherman uses total war. Destroy everything. Crops, railroads, cities. Atlanta falls after a long siege, then burns. Then Sherman marches to the sea. Atlanta to Savannah. Destroys food supplies, railroads, infrastructure. Demoralizes the South completely.

Maya: Did Sherman actually say "war is hell"?

Marcus: Not during the war. What he told Atlanta's mayor was "war is cruelty, and you cannot refine it." The march was the argument made real: wreck the food, tear up the rails, burn the workshops. The will to fight goes with them.

Maya: Total war means everyone suffers, not just soldiers.

Marcus: That was the strategy. Break the South's will by making the cost unbearable. It worked. By 1865, Lee was trapped in Virginia. Grant cut off his escape route, and on April 9, Lee surrendered at Appomattox Court House.

Maya: And Grant is generous.

Marcus: The terms are kind. Confederates keep their horses. They go home. No trials, no executions. Lincoln's spirit of malice toward none. Five days later, Lincoln is assassinated. But the war is over.

Maya: How many died?

Marcus: About 698,000, by the latest census-based estimate. Deadliest war in American history — roughly as many Americans died as in every other American war put together.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. North has advantages but South has better generals and defensive position. Lincoln can't find an aggressive commander until Grant. Anaconda Plan squeezes via blockade and Mississippi. Antietam in 1862 is the bloodiest day and opens the door for Emancipation. Gettysburg and Vicksburg in July 1863 are the turning points. Lee retreats, Mississippi is taken, Confederacy split. Grant uses attrition, Sherman uses total war, Atlanta burns, march to the sea. Drafts cause riots on both sides. Appomattox in April 1865, generous terms, Lincoln murdered five days later. About 698,000 dead.

Marcus: That's the chain. The turning points, and why they mattered: Gettysburg ends Southern invasion. Vicksburg splits the Confederacy. Both in the same week.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. First: why didn't the North win quickly despite its advantages?

[5-second pause]

Maya: Better Confederate generals early, defensive advantages, and Lincoln cycling through commanders until he found Grant.

Maya: Second: what made Gettysburg and Vicksburg the turning points?

[5-second pause]

Maya: Gettysburg ended Lee's invasions of the North; Vicksburg gave the Union the Mississippi and cut the Confederacy in half — both in July 1863.

Maya: Third: how did total war and attrition break the South?

[5-second pause]

Maya: Grant's relentless battles bled irreplaceable Southern manpower, while Sherman's destruction of food, rails, and towns collapsed the will to fight.

Maya: One thing to carry forward: the war answered the secession question permanently. No state gets to leave — that question is closed for good.

Marcus: Answered in blood, at terrible cost. Next time: Lincoln frees the enslaved people. Or does he? The paradox of the Emancipation Proclamation.
