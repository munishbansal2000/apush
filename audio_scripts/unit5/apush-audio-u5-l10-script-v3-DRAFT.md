Maya: Last time: paper freedom versus real freedom, and the promise that we'd finally call it. So let's call it. Was Reconstruction a success or a failure? The amendments that still stand, the land that never came, the votes that got traded away. Four boxes: the Reconstruction Amendments, Sharecropping, the Compromise of 1877, and the final verdict. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. About 12 minutes, and we finally call it.

Marcus: Start with the case for success: the evidence is written in the Constitution. Thirteenth Amendment, 1865: slavery abolished. Fourteenth, 1868: birthright citizenship, equal protection. Fifteenth, 1870: Black men can't be denied the vote on account of race. Those three are permanent, the legal foundation for generations of civil rights cases since.

Maya: And roughly a century later, the civil rights movement argued from the 14th and 15th.

Marcus: They'd been waiting in the Constitution the whole time. The success case in one sentence: the law changed forever, even when the country didn't.

Maya: And actual Black men in office, not just rights on paper.

Marcus: The 1870 elections: freedmen helped elect roughly 600 Black men to Southern state legislatures, 16 to the U.S. House, one to the Senate. In the 1870s, two Black senators took their seats: Hiram Revels and Blanche K. Bruce, both from Mississippi, the first Black senators in American history.

Maya: How long did that last? Because the 15th stayed on paper while Black voting collapsed.

Marcus: Where federal power stood, it held: roughly a decade of real multiracial politics in the South. Where it didn't stand, it didn't. The failure case is coming, though.

Maya: Checking the first box. The Reconstruction Amendments: the laws, the officeholders, the whole success case. One down.

Marcus: One more piece from last episode: the Freedmen's Bureau schools. The teachers outlasted the politics. Howard University and Morehouse College were founded during Reconstruction, and they're still here.

Maya: A classic wrong answer I can see myself writing: "the amendments gave Black Americans equality." That's the trap, isn't it?

Marcus: The trap is rights on paper. Paper isn't power, and a grader wants you to know the difference.

Maya: So if an SAQ asks about Reconstruction's successes, anchor on what survived. The 13th, 14th, and 15th never left the Constitution. Analysis, not a list.

Marcus: Now the other side of the ledger. Because the freed people wanted one thing most, and it wasn't an amendment.

Maya: Your turn. Freed people wanted land, a plot to work for themselves. Sherman's order promised it. So what did the federal government do: hand over the land, or hand it back? Eight seconds.

[8-second pause]

Marcus: It handed it back. Sharecropping.

Maya: Wait, I know that phrase. Forty acres and a mule — the Spike Lee logo at the end of his movies. I always wondered what it meant.

Marcus: It's what freed families said they wanted: forty acres and a mule. Some Radical Republicans wanted to break up the plantations and hand the land over, but that never got the votes. Sherman's wartime field order set aside coastal land for freed families. President Johnson rescinded it, pardoned the owners, and the land went back.

Maya: So the Freedmen's Bureau gave people land and then Johnson took it all back?

Marcus: Close, but garbled. Sherman's order was a field order from the war, not a Bureau grant. Johnson rescinded it and returned the land to pardoned Confederates. Most freed people never held any land at all. Failure, right there.

Maya: So no land — then what did most freed families actually end up with?

Marcus: Sharecropping. Planters needed labor and freed people needed land. A family rented a plot and paid with a portion of the crop, usually around half. Seed and tools came on credit from the planter's store, and after the debts got paid, almost nothing was left. The books call it a compromise, and the compromise worked out better for one side.

Maya: It's a debt cycle: you can't leave if you owe the store, and you always owe the store.

Marcus: No land and no capital meant no way out. Economic freedom died in the fine print.

Marcus: Don't write that sharecropping gave freed families independence. It rented them the land they'd been enslaved on, paid for with the crop, and the debt cycle kept them landless.

Maya: If the exam hands you a sharecropping contract as a stimulus, read it like a trap. Who controls the credit, who sets the price, who gets to leave. It's asking about power, not farming.

Marcus: Third box: the law getting hollowed out from the inside, starting with the Court.

Marcus: 1873, the Slaughterhouse Cases. The Court narrows the 14th Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause so it protects only national citizenship rights, not the rights freed people needed from their own states. Then Colfax, Louisiana: a disputed governor's election, Black Republicans occupy the courthouse, and a white mob murders over a hundred of them.

Maya: And the case that came out of Colfax?

Marcus: United States v. Cruikshank, 1876. The 14th Amendment restrains state governments, not private individuals. Congress could protect you from discrimination by a state, but not by a mob. The Enforcement Act, which let federal authorities prosecute vigilante violence, got struck down as it was being used.

Maya: So after Cruikshank, if a white mob murders Black voters, Washington just can't prosecute?

Marcus: Prosecution fell to Southern states run by the people the mobs were protecting. Federal tools against the Klan got weaker, and the violence got worse.

Maya: So how does an election that close actually get decided?

Marcus: Democrat Samuel Tilden wins the popular vote and sits at 184 electoral votes, one short. Republican Rutherford Hayes has 165. Twenty votes are disputed: Florida, Louisiana, South Carolina, plus one Oregon elector. A congressional commission awards every disputed vote to Hayes. He wins 185 to 184.

Maya: That only happens with a deal behind it.

Marcus: The Compromise of 1877. Republicans get the presidency. Southern Democrats get federal troops pulled out of South Carolina and Louisiana, the last states under military Reconstruction. By then, Democrats calling themselves Redeemers had already retaken most Southern legislatures, with white terror networks helping.

Maya: And the North just watched?

Marcus: The North had stopped caring. The Panic of 1873 pulled everyone's attention to the economy, and the moral urgency faded.

Marcus: Careful with the idea that Hayes ended Reconstruction all by himself. By 1876 the Redeemers had already retaken most Southern legislatures. The troops were the last prop, not the whole building.

Maya: An LEQ on why Reconstruction failed wants causes with mechanisms. Not "the Court was racist." Cruikshank's state-action limit, with Colfax as the evidence.

Marcus: Fourth box, where the historians stopped agreeing with each other.

Marcus: For the first half of the 1900s, the story, called the Dunning School, said Reconstruction failed because Black suffrage caused corruption and misgovernment. The revisionists flipped it. Eric Foner, in 1988, argued the tragedy wasn't Black incapacity. It was white violence and Northern abandonment. The failure was real, but for fifty years the blame landed in the wrong place. The debate isn't fully settled, but that's where most historians landed.

Maya: Your turn. Eight seconds. You've heard both cases. An essay asks you to evaluate Reconstruction — success or failure. What's your verdict, and what's your strongest evidence on each side?

[8-second pause]

Maya: I'd argue both. The amendments are still law. But the troops left and the land never came, and I can't unhear that.

Marcus: I once watched a student write "there are good points on both sides" as a thesis. The teacher wrote back: that's a coin flip, not an argument.

Maya: That was me. Sophomore year. She wrote "evaluate, not list" in the margin and I've never forgotten it.

Marcus: She was right. So here's the verdict, evaluated. Success: the 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments permanently ended slavery and wrote equality into the Constitution. Failure: sharecropping kept freed people landless, Cruikshank gutted federal enforcement, and the Compromise of 1877 traded the whole project for a presidency. The law held. The power didn't.

Maya: So the answer is genuinely both.

Marcus: Both, genuinely — the exam rewards a verdict with evidence on each side, not a team picked in advance.

Maya: Two wrong answers, one tempting each way. "Reconstruction was a total failure": no, the amendments never left. "It was a success": no, the South the troops left behind became Jim Crow. Both, with evidence.

Marcus: For "evaluate" prompts, plant the verdict in your thesis, even "both, because," then make each paragraph prove one side. "Both" without evidence is a coin flip. "Both" with evidence is the argument.

Maya: Four boxes, let's land them. One: the Reconstruction Amendments. Thirteenth ends slavery, Fourteenth gives citizenship and equal protection, Fifteenth protects Black men's vote. The 1870 elections put roughly 600 Black men in Southern legislatures, 16 in the House. Wait, was the 16 the House or the Senate?

Marcus: House. Senate was one that year: Revels. Bruce came later, in 1875.

Maya: Right. Plus the Bureau schools: Howard, Morehouse, still here. Two: sharecropping. No land redistribution, Johnson rescinds Sherman's order, rent paid with around half the crop, and the debt cycle keeps them landless. Three: the collapse. Slaughterhouse and Cruikshank narrow the 14th, the Compromise of 1877 trades the presidency for the troops, and the North looks away after the Panic of 1873. Four: the verdict. Both — the amendments survived, and everything else got taken back.

Marcus: There's the chain.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. First, a source. An 1870s sharecropping contract: the farmer borrows seed and tools from the planter's store on credit and pays rent with half the crop. What's the point of this source?

[15-second pause]

Maya: It's showing sharecropping as a debt trap. Credit from the planter's store, rent out of the crop: the farmer starts every season owing money, so he can't leave. The economic failure case: freedom without land or capital turned into bondage by debt.

Maya: Second. After the Colfax Massacre, the Supreme Court ruled in Cruikshank that the 14th Amendment restrains state governments, not private individuals. Using that ruling, explain why federal enforcement of Black rights collapsed.

[15-second pause]

Maya: Because Cruikshank took Washington's main weapon away. If the 14th only restrains states, the federal government can't prosecute the white mobs murdering Black voters, so enforcement falls to Southern states run by the mobs' allies. The amendment narrowed, the troops left, and nobody willing to enforce the law remained.

Maya: Third. Evaluate: was Reconstruction a success or a failure?

[15-second pause]

Maya: Both, with evidence. The 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments ended slavery and enshrined equality, and they were still there for the civil rights movement a century later. But sharecropping kept freed people landless, Cruikshank gutted federal enforcement, and the Compromise of 1877 traded the project for a presidency. Constitutional success, political and economic failure.

Maya: One more, fast. Thirteenth, Fourteenth, Fifteenth, one line each. Thirteenth: slavery abolished. Fourteenth: citizenship and equal protection. Fifteenth: Black men can't be denied the vote on account of race.

Maya: Check your boxes.

Maya: Next time: the troops leave, and the South gets to work on its workarounds. Jim Crow and the New South.

Marcus: How the South undid Reconstruction without repealing a single amendment.

Maya: The amendments are still standing.

Marcus: The work is still unfinished.
