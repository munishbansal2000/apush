# APUSH Unit 5, Lesson 5: Lincoln-Douglas Debates
## Script v2 DRAFT — Debate: Maya moderates, Marcus (Lincoln) + Stephen A. Douglas

**Format:** Debate | **Target:** 1440–1520 words, 8 min @ 180 WPM | **CED:** 5.6/5.7

# Read note: Debate format. Maya moderates. Marcus argues Abraham Lincoln's case against slavery's expansion. Stephen A. Douglas argues for popular sovereignty, in his own voice. Dignity rule: Douglas is the Little Giant, a genuinely formidable debater, never a caricature. Both men speak from 1858, with 1858's politics. # Pronunciation: DUG-liss (Douglas)

Maya: 1858, Illinois. Seven debates, three hours each, in front of crowds of thousands with brass bands and picnic baskets. One man is the most famous senator in America. The other is a lanky lawyer nobody outside Illinois has heard of. The prize is a Senate seat. The real stakes are the presidency and the future of slavery. Tonight's question: was popular sovereignty an answer to the slavery question, or an evasion of it? Arguing it was an evasion, that slavery is a moral wrong the nation must confront: Marcus, for Abraham Lincoln. Arguing it was the only democratic answer: Stephen A. Douglas himself.

Marcus: A house divided against itself cannot stand. I believe this government cannot endure, permanently half slave and half free. Those are Lincoln's words, Springfield, June 1858, accepting the Republican nomination. His case is simple. Slavery is a moral wrong. The Founders put it on a course of ultimate extinction. And Douglas's popular sovereignty, letting each territory vote on human bondage, is how the wrong spreads while everyone pretends their hands are clean.

Douglas: The people decide. They call me the Little Giant, and that is my case. The Kansas-Nebraska Act took the slavery question out of Congress, where it poisons everything, and gave it to the settlers who have to live with the answer. That is democracy: the people ruling themselves on the question that touches them most. I do not care whether slavery is voted up or voted down in a territory. I care that the people rule themselves. Mr. Lincoln wants uniformity. Uniformity means disunion, because this country was never uniform and was never meant to be.

Maya: Opening statements are on the table. Before the clash, the scene, because the scene matters. Seven debates, August to October. The format is grueling: one candidate speaks for an hour, the other answers for ninety minutes, then thirty minutes of rebuttal. Thousands show up. Some crowds hit fifteen thousand. They camp overnight. Newspapers print every word, telegraph wires carry the speeches east, and for the first time a Senate race becomes a national event. And remember, in 1858 the people don't elect senators. The state legislature does.

Marcus: Which is how you have to understand everything that follows. Lincoln is the underdog. Forty-nine years old, one forgettable term in Congress, years out of politics, practicing law in Springfield. Douglas is the most powerful Democrat in America, the author of the Kansas-Nebraska Act. Nobody gives Lincoln a chance.

Douglas: And yet he got his chance, because I accepted his challenge. Seven joint appearances. Let the record show I didn't hide from the question.

Maya: Clash round one: Freeport. The second debate, August 27th. Lincoln asks Douglas a question his own advisors begged him not to ask. Marcus, what was the trap?

Marcus: The Dred Scott decision said Congress can't ban slavery in the territories. So Lincoln asks: can the people of a territory, in any lawful way, exclude slavery before they become a state? It's a trap because Douglas has to choose. Say no, and he loses Illinois. Say yes, and he defies the Supreme Court and loses the South.

Douglas: It was no trap, because it was no surprise. I had given the same answer a hundred times from every stump in Illinois before Lincoln ever asked it on a debate stage. I did not invent a new doctrine under pressure. I repeated my settled position. My answer at Freeport: yes, the people can exclude slavery. Slavery cannot exist without local police regulations to protect it. If the settlers refuse to pass those laws, if they use what I called unfriendly legislation, slavery cannot take root among them. The Court can rule whatever it likes. On the ground, the people decide.

Marcus: And the story goes that the night before Freeport, Lincoln's advisors burst into his room and begged him not to ask it. Ask it, they said, and Douglas will answer yes and you'll never be senator. And Lincoln said he was killing larger game. If Douglas answers, he can never be president, and the battle of 1860 is worth a hundred of this.

Douglas: Larger game. Mr. Lincoln sacrificed his own Senate race to wound me for 1860, and he admits it freely. But the South's fury afterward was not because I changed my mind at Freeport. It was because Lincoln forced me to say my settled position on the largest stage in America, where the South could hear every word.

Maya: And the South did hear. Southern Democrats read the Freeport Doctrine as defiance of Dred Scott, as Douglas saying the Court doesn't matter.

Marcus: It pleased Illinois and doomed him South. That's the whole Freeport story in one sentence. Douglas's answer was clever, and it was fatal. The Southern wing of his own party never trusted him again.

Douglas: The Southern wing wanted a federal slave code for the territories, wanted Congress to force slavery into lands where the people didn't want it. That is not popular sovereignty. That is not democracy. I wouldn't give it to them at Freeport, and I wouldn't give it to them at Charleston in 1860 either.

Maya: Clash round two. Douglas kept the Senate seat. Walk me through how.

Douglas: The Illinois legislature elects senators, and the Democrats held the legislature. I won re-election. That is the constitutional mechanism working as designed. Mr. Lincoln got the crowds and the newspapers. I got the seat.

Marcus: And Lincoln got the larger prize. He lost the seat and won the audience that mattered. The debates made him nationally famous. He was invited east. February 1860, Cooper Union, New York: the speech that made him a presidential contender. Two years after losing a Senate race, he was the Republican nominee for president. Douglas won the battle. Lincoln won the war.

Douglas: A war, indeed. Mr. Lincoln's House Divided speech told the South the nation must become all one thing or all the other. The South heard a declaration of war on their institutions, and they were not wrong to hear it.

Marcus: They heard what was there. Lincoln said the nation couldn't permanently endure half slave and half free. He was right. At Alton, in the last debate, he put it this way: you may have a cancer on a person and not be able to cut it out lest he bleed to death, but it is no way to cure it to engraft it and spread it over the whole body. You don't treat a wrong by spreading it.

Maya: I want an honest minute from both of you, because the textbooks flatten this. 1858, race: where did each of you stand?

Douglas: I said openly that I did not believe in Black equality, socially or politically. I was running on white supremacy as much as popular sovereignty. I won't dress it up for a modern audience.

Marcus: And Lincoln was no abolitionist in 1858. He opposed slavery's expansion. He believed it morally wrong and headed for ultimate extinction. But he was not calling for immediate emancipation, and he was not preaching racial equality. Both men operated inside a racist political system. History judged their choices very differently, but the honest version has no heroes without shadows.

Maya: Closing statements, then. Douglas, you first.

Douglas: I offered the country self-government. Let the people of each territory decide their own institutions, as the Founders let the states decide theirs. Mr. Lincoln offered a moral crusade that could only end in uniformity or war. The people of Illinois chose self-government in 1858. I only wish the country had been allowed to keep choosing it.

Marcus: Lincoln offered the truth that Douglas's dodge couldn't survive: you cannot vote on whether human bondage expands and call the result democracy. Popular sovereignty was incoherent the moment Dred Scott said Congress couldn't touch slavery, and Lincoln exposed it in seven debates, in front of thousands of farmers and shopkeepers, for three hours at a time. Douglas kept his Senate seat. Lincoln got the presidency. The country got its answer.

Maya: Verdict. Here is what I take from both of you. The debates did what Congress couldn't: they made both positions plain, in public, for hours, and clarified what popular sovereignty was hiding, a doctrine that sounded democratic and collapsed the moment you asked how it worked. Douglas's answer at Freeport was genuinely clever, and it destroyed him, because cleverness couldn't bridge a moral chasm. Lincoln lost the Senate seat and won everything else: the Eastern audience, the nomination, the presidency, and history's verdict. The loser won bigger than the winner. Next time: four candidates, one election, and the South decides Lincoln's victory means it's time to leave.

Marcus: Seven debates, one question —

Maya: and the loser took the larger prize.
