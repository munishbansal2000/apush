# Pronunciation: Breckinridge (BREK-in-rij); Crittenden (KRIT-en-dun).


Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: seven debates across Illinois — Douglas kept the Senate, Lincoln won the future, and the road to 1860 was open. So let's do it. How does forty percent become a landslide? Why did the South read the results as a death sentence? And how did the winter after the election break the Union? Four boxes on your sheet: the four-way race, the election math, the secession winter, and Fort Sumter. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. About twelve minutes.

Marcus: [measured] Start with the Democrats, because they break first. April 1860, Charleston, South Carolina: the party's convention. Southern delegates demand a platform plank: federal protection for slavery in the territories. Northern delegates, standing behind Stephen Douglas and popular sovereignty, vote it down. Popular sovereignty, remember: the settlers in a territory vote slavery up or down themselves.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And the Southerners just walk out? Out of their own convention?

Marcus: [measured] They walk. The convention deadlocks with no nominee and adjourns. Weeks later they try again, in Baltimore. The Northerners nominate Douglas. The Southern delegates walk out a second time, hold their own convention, and nominate the sitting vice president, John Breckinridge, on slavery in all the territories.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So two Democratic tickets. And then a fourth man shows up?

Marcus: [measured] A third group, anyway. Former Whigs, mostly Southern moderates, form the Constitutional Union Party and nominate John Bell of Tennessee. The platform is the Constitution, the Union, and silence on slavery. Meanwhile the Republicans nominate Abraham Lincoln of Illinois. His line: slavery stays where it is and never spreads west. Free soil, free labor, no expansion — ever.

Maya: [conversational] Let me get the field straight. Lincoln the Republican: no expansion. Douglas the Northern Democrat: popular sovereignty, the settlers decide. Breckinridge the Southern Democrat: slavery in all the territories. Bell: the Union, and let's not talk about slavery.

Marcus: [conversational] There's your field.

Maya: [conversational] One down — the four-way race.

Maya: [firm] So the exam answer is simple: the Democrats split over slavery in 1860.

Marcus: [measured] Name the plank: the split was over whether the platform would demand federal protection for slavery in the territories — that's the line the Charleston walkout was drawn on.

Marcus: [measured] Now, the thing they don't teach you until it matters: the presidency isn't a popular vote.

Maya: [conversational] I know that game. My brother and I used to play the electoral map game: the map matters more than the raw vote total.

Marcus: [measured] Then you're ahead of half the country in 1860. Hold that thought, because the results are about to get strange.

Maya: Your turn. Four candidates, the opposition split three ways, and the North holds most of the electoral votes. Before I give you the numbers: can forty percent actually win a presidency? Think about what picks the president. Start with the map.
[10-second pause]

Marcus: Lincoln: about forty percent of the popular vote, and 180 electoral votes. The other three combined: 123. He swept the free states. Every northern state except New Jersey, which split its electors between Lincoln and Douglas, plus California and Oregon out west.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Hold on. Douglas came in second, with almost thirty percent of the popular vote. Respectable, no?

Marcus: [serious tone] He finished second in popular votes and dead last in electoral votes: twelve, from Missouri and a slice of New Jersey. That gap is the whole lesson of 1860 inside one candidate.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And the South? Did Lincoln get anything down there at all?

Marcus: [measured] Zero popular votes in ten of the eleven states that would become the Confederacy. Virginia was the only exception, and even there he managed about one percent.

Maya: [conversational] So the popular vote was almost decoration. The map was the whole election.

Marcus: [measured] The North held the majority of the electoral votes. Sweep the North and the election is over: no combination of Southern votes can stop you. That was the arithmetic the South stared at all winter.

Maya: [conversational] If the numbers blur on test day, hold the shape: forty percent popular, 180 electoral. When a question asks how a minority candidate wins the presidency, the answer is the Electoral College plus a sectional map.

Marcus: [measured] Careful. That tip is doing real work. The split opposition padded Lincoln's margin, but the map alone decided the election.

Maya: [conversational] Rank the 1860 candidates by electoral votes: Lincoln, Breckinridge, Bell, Douglas.

Marcus: [measured] Now the question the South asked itself all winter: was Lincoln the problem, or just the proof? Look at the trend. Eighteen free states against fifteen slave states. Minnesota and Oregon came in free in 1858 and 1859. The free states already hold the electoral majority, and every new western state is coming in free.

Maya: [conversational] Lincoln was only the first proof of a permanent pattern. Every election from here on would look like this one.

Marcus: [measured] Exactly how the South read it. Lincoln's promise was no power and no plan to touch slavery where it already existed. They didn't believe a word. October 1859 was still fresh: John Brown's raid on the federal arsenal at Harpers Ferry. Colonel Robert E. Lee led the Marines that captured him, Brown was hanged before the year ended, and Northern sympathy for him told the South that Lincoln's promises weren't worth much.

Maya: [conversational] So in their heads, waiting means slow strangulation.

Marcus: [measured] The premium prep book puts it plainly: even if Lincoln kept his promise, many slaveholders wouldn't have been satisfied, because slavery, in their view, needed room to grow to stay economically viable. Containment was a cage, even with the door left open.

Maya: [conversational] December 20, 1860. South Carolina goes first.

Marcus: [measured] First out. Within six weeks, six more follow: Mississippi, Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, Texas. February 1861, their representatives meet at Montgomery, Alabama, form the Confederate States of America, and elect Jefferson Davis president.

Maya: [conversational] I'd guess the declarations hide behind legal language: states' rights, compact theory. Nobody writes "we're leaving over slavery" in an official document.

Marcus: [measured] It's the common mix-up, and the exam punishes it. Mississippi's declaration opens with it: "Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery." And the Confederacy's new vice president, Alexander Stephens, gave a speech saying the new government's cornerstone rests on the great truth that the negro is not equal to the white man: that slavery, subordination to the superior race, is his natural and normal condition.

Maya: [conversational] Nothing coded about that.

Marcus: [measured] It isn't. Whatever people said later, the seceders named slavery themselves.

Maya: [conversational] The exam rewards the seceders' own words: Mississippi's declaration, Stephens's cornerstone speech. The states'-rights-only version is largely the postwar rewrite.

Marcus: [measured] One rescue attempt before the shooting starts. The Crittenden Compromise: a Kentucky senator's plan to redraw the old Missouri line all the way to the Pacific, with slavery protected south of it. Lincoln refused to soften the Republican demand of no expansion, anywhere, and the plan died.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And Buchanan? The actual sitting president? He just watched?

Marcus: [measured] His December message to Congress said secession had no legal right, and in the same message said the federal government had no power to coerce a state back. The message was words with no force, and the secession winter ran out the clock on him.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] I went to Charleston once, on a family trip. We took the little ferry out to the fort. It's tiny, this brick island sitting in the middle of the harbor. I stood there thinking, this started all of it?

Marcus: [measured] April 1861, and the fort is running out of food. Lincoln makes a careful choice: send a ship carrying food for the fort, nothing that shoots, and tell South Carolina it's coming. That hands the decision to Jefferson Davis. Let the food through and look weak, or fire on bread and start a war.

Maya: [conversational] Davis fired.

Marcus: [dramatic] April 12, 1861. Confederate guns open on Fort Sumter, and the fort surrenders after the bombardment. Every man in the fort survived the bombardment. The deaths came afterward, in the surrender salute, when a cartridge exploded — an accident during the ceremony.

Maya: [conversational] So once Lincoln won, the upper South went too — Virginia, the rest of them, right after the election.

Marcus: [measured] This is the order trap the exam sets. The chain runs Sumter, then the call, then Virginia, with Arkansas, Tennessee, and North Carolina following in that order.

Marcus: [serious tone] Days after Sumter, Lincoln calls for 75,000 volunteers to "cause the laws to be duly executed." Virginia, which had sat out the first wave, votes to secede, and Arkansas, Tennessee, and North Carolina follow. The Confederacy doubles. The border slave states — Maryland, Delaware, Kentucky, Missouri — stay put, caught in the middle.

Maya: You decide. Lincoln just called for 75,000 volunteers to put down the rebellion. You're Virginia, a slave state that stayed out of the first secession wave. The president wants your troops to fight the South. What do you do?
[10-second pause]

Marcus: There is no third option. Send troops against your neighbors, or join them. Virginia joins, and Arkansas, Tennessee, and North Carolina follow.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So was the war inevitable? Once Lincoln won, was there any version where nobody shoots?

Marcus: [measured] Lincoln thought he could hold federal property without starting a war: food on the ship, no weapons, and make Davis choose. Davis thought a foreign flag over Charleston harbor couldn't stand. Both sides figured the other would blink. Neither did. "Inevitable" is the exam's favorite argument word, and the strong answer holds both sides at once: the case that the war was coming, and the case that it wasn't.

Maya: [conversational] Four boxes, let's land them. One: the four-way race. Democrats split at Charleston and Baltimore over the slave-code plank, leaving Lincoln, Douglas, Breckinridge, and Bell.

Maya: [confident tone] Checking that one.

Maya: [conversational] Two: the math. About forty percent popular, 180 electoral, because the North's votes were the only ones that counted.

Maya: [conversational] Two down.

Maya: [conversational] Three: the secession winter. South Carolina on December 20, six more states, Montgomery, Davis. Crittenden's plan dies, Buchanan's message is words with no force, and the seceders name slavery themselves.

Maya: [conversational] Third box, checked.

Maya: [conversational] Four: Sumter. Lincoln sends food, Davis fires on April 12, nobody dies in the bombardment, Lincoln calls for 75,000, and the upper South goes: Virginia, Arkansas, Tennessee, North Carolina.

Maya: [conversational] And that's four.

Maya: [incredulous] Wait — Crittenden's plan protected slavery south of the line, all the way to the Pacific? He's giving the South what it wanted. Why would that fail?

Marcus: [measured] Because the price was letting slavery spread, and no-expansion was the one thing Republicans wouldn't sell. Lincoln killed it on that point.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. First, a source. A seceding state declares: "Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery." What's the point of this source?
[10-second pause]

Maya: The point is that slavery drove secession, rather than some abstract argument about states' rights. It's Mississippi's own declaration saying it outright, so when the exam asks about causes, the documents put slavery first.

Maya: Second. Lincoln won about forty percent of the popular vote and 180 electoral votes. Explain the gap.
[10-second pause]

Maya: The presidency runs on electoral votes, not popular votes. Lincoln swept the free states, which held the electoral majority, while three opponents split everything else, and New Jersey even split its electors. Lincoln's forty percent sat in the right states.

Maya: Third. Virginia sat out the first secession wave, then seceded in April 1861. What changed?
[10-second pause]

Maya: Fort Sumter, then Lincoln's call for 75,000 volunteers. The call forced every slave state still in the Union to choose: send troops against the South or join it. Virginia joined, and Arkansas, Tennessee, and North Carolina followed.

Maya: One more, fast. Name the four candidates and give each one's slavery position in a phrase.
[10-second pause]

Maya: Lincoln: no expansion. Douglas: popular sovereignty, the settlers decide. Breckinridge: slavery in all the territories. Bell: the Union, and silence on slavery.

Maya: [confident tone] Check your boxes.

Marcus: [professional broadcast tone] The election was decided by arithmetic. The war was decided by what the arithmetic meant.

Maya: [intrigued] Next time: the war nobody thought would last. Bull Run, the turning points of 1863, and Grant and Sherman breaking the South.
