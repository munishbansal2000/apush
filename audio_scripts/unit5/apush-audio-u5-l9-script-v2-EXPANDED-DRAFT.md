# APUSH Unit 5, Lesson 9: Reconstruction Plans
## Script v3 DEBATE DRAFT — Maya moderates, Marcus vs Thaddeus Stevens

**Format:** Debate (Maya moderates, Marcus vs Thaddeus Stevens) | **Target:** 1440 words, 8 min @ 180 WPM | **CED:** 5.10

# Read note: debate format. Maya moderates; Marcus argues Lincoln's reunion case, Congressman Thaddeus Stevens argues the Radical transformation case. Strip the header and this note before TTS. The em dash in the closing tagline is a held breath — do not rush it.
# Pronunciation: Thaddeus Stevens (THAD-ee-us), Hiram Revels (HY-rum REV-ulz), Edwin Stanton (STAN-tun), John Wilkes Booth (WILKS)

---

Maya: April 1865. The war is over, Lincoln is dead, assassinated at Ford's Theatre by John Wilkes Booth, and four million freed people are no longer enslaved, but the country has no plan for them yet. The victors split over one question: do you restore the South, or transform it? Arguing restoration, Lincoln's heir: Marcus. Arguing transformation, the Radical Republican case: Congressman Thaddeus Stevens of Pennsylvania. Gentlemen, opening statements. Marcus, you're up first.

Marcus: Lincoln's 10% Plan, 1863. The Proclamation of Amnesty and Reconstruction asked two things: ten percent of a state's 1860 voters take a loyalty oath, and the state abolishes slavery. Then it rejoins the Union. Lenient by design. Lincoln held secession was never constitutional: the states never left, so there was nothing to conquer, only wayward citizens to welcome home. Hear the Second Inaugural, March 1865: "With malice toward none, with charity for all." That speech is the plan as policy. And Lincoln knew what he was refusing: Wade-Davis demanded a majority and an ironclad oath, Congress's terms, which would have humiliated the South into permanent resentment. He pocket-vetoed it to keep the border states and moderates inside the tent.

Stevens: Charity for the planters. The 10% Plan asked nothing of the men who built their fortunes on bondage: ten percent could be the overseers and quartermasters while the aristocracy kept its land and its power. Congress answered with the Wade-Davis Bill: a majority, not ten percent, and an ironclad oath. Lincoln pocket-vetoed it. And my constitutional case, not just the moral one: the rebel states committed suicide. They are not wayward sisters to welcome home; they are conquered provinces to rebuild, with new constitutions and new voters. Lincoln's fiction that they never left is what made his leniency possible. It is also what made it fatal. You don't heal a wound by declaring it was never cut.

Maya: Round one: the Black Codes. Congressman, what did the South do with leniency?

Stevens: Precisely what I predicted. The Southern legislatures passed the Black Codes: curfews, labor contracts no freedman could refuse, vagrancy laws letting a sheriff arrest a Black man for idleness and auction his labor. Slavery under another name, enacted the hour the bayonets looked away. And Georgia sent Alexander Stephens, the Confederacy's vice president and author of the Cornerstone Speech, to Congress, as though treason were a credential. Congress refused to seat him. Leniency had failed on its first trial.

Maya: The Codes proved leniency failed. But your side built more than amendments. What answered the Codes on the ground?

Marcus: The Freedmen's Bureau. Food, medicine, and schools for freed people and poor whites alike. Picture it: a rented cabin, a church basement, children and grandparents sounding out letters side by side. Within a few years, something like a quarter-million students. The Bureau died in 1872, but its schools lived on, Shaw University rising in 1865, the first Black college in the South.

Stevens: Schools, yes, and I honor every teacher in them. But a schoolhouse without a farm is a waiting room. The ballot without the soil is a promise the next legislature can starve.

Marcus: The Codes were vile and real. But they were the South's answer to Johnson's clumsiness, not Lincoln's plan. Johnson, a Tennessee Democrat who loathed the planter class because they sneered at his poverty, turned pardons into personal revenge, made wealthy rebels beg at his desk, then declared Reconstruction complete in December 1865, eight months after Appomattox. He carried Lincoln's leniency and none of Lincoln's judgment. Don't convict the 10% Plan for Johnson's execution of it.

Maya: So your defense is Lincoln's plan never got a fair trial — Johnson botched the audition.

Marcus: Exactly. Restoration needed Lincoln's hand, not Johnson's grudge.

Maya: Round two: the 14th Amendment and the Reconstruction Acts. Congressman, your side wrote it. What does it actually do?

Stevens: Four clauses, and I want every student to carry all four. Birthright citizenship: born on this soil, you are a citizen, and no legislature votes that away. Privileges or immunities: the rights of citizenship, which no state may abridge. Due process: no state takes life, liberty, or property without fair proceedings. Equal protection: the law shelters all alike. Add Section 2: a state that blocks men from voting loses seats in the House. It was never enforced as written, a failure I never lived to repair, but the words stand waiting.

Maya: Hold on, the 14th gave everyone the vote? I thought that was the point.

Marcus: The classic mix-up. Not the vote — that's the 15th, 1870, for Black men. The 14th is the revolution in the fine print: before it, the Bill of Rights restrained only the federal government; after it, the Constitution guards your rights against your own state. And the Radicals put teeth behind the parchment. The Military Reconstruction Act carved the South into five military districts, federal troops enforcing the new order, passed over Johnson's veto after the Radicals won two-thirds of Congress in 1866. Transformation with teeth.

Maya: And the Southern states just agreed to all this?

Stevens: They had no choice. Ratifying the 14th was the price of readmission: no amendment, no senators, no House seats. Coercion, plainly. We made the Constitution the admission ticket.

Marcus: Which is exactly why the reunionists flinched. A union rebuilt by ultimatum is a union nursing a grudge.

Stevens: Teeth I applaud. But parchment and patrols without land are a house with no foundation. In 1867 I brought a bill: confiscate the great plantations, grant each freedman forty acres. It died in committee; my own party flinched at touching private property. So the freedman received the ballot and the schoolhouse but no soil beneath them. Sherman had set aside coastal land, forty acres per family, in January 1865. Johnson handed it back to the pardoned rebels. Freedom without the means to make it real is a promise written in water.

Maya: "Forty acres and a mule" — isn't that Spike Lee's company?

Marcus: The phrase predates the company. And the Congressman's sting lands: the economic half of transformation never happened.

Maya: Final round: the impeachment. Marcus, you call the Tenure of Office Act a trap. Explain.

Marcus: Congress wrote it to shield Secretary of War Edwin Stanton, the Radical ally running Reconstruction. Pure bait. Johnson fired Stanton anyway. The House impeached him in February 1868, and the Senate acquitted by one vote — seven Republicans broke ranks, convinced that removing a president over a policy war would poison the republic. Johnson survived as a lame duck. The Radicals spent their fury on one man instead of the system.

Stevens: One vote. Had a single senator held firm, the obstructionist would have fallen and Reconstruction might have run its full course. Instead Washington learned the darker lesson: a president could defy Congress and endure.

Maya: Congressman, you led the prosecution, didn't you?

Stevens: I managed the House case. They carried me into the Senate chamber, for I was dying. I told the senators Johnson had earned removal ten times over. One vote saved him. The republic kept its president and lost its nerve.

Maya: Wait — Congress impeached a president for firing his own cabinet officer? Presidents fire people.

Marcus: Johnson's defense, word for word. But Congress had written the law to protect Stanton specifically. Fire the shield, face the charge.

Maya: Closing statements.

Stevens: I demanded revolution and received reunion with conditions. The amendments are noble, I grant it freely. But a citizenship resting on federal bayonets lasts only until the bayonets go home. Transform the soil or lose the South. I am buried in a Black cemetery in Lancaster to prove I meant it: my epitaph says I chose that ground to illustrate in death the principles I fought for in life, equality of man before his Creator.

Marcus: And the reunion case, in closing. Two thousand Black men held office, Hiram Revels of Mississippi, the first Black United States Senator, in Jefferson Davis's old seat. The 13th killed slavery. The 15th gave Black men the vote, though it split the suffragists: Stanton and Anthony formed the National Woman Suffrage Association when women were left out, against Douglass's plea to wait. Reunion was fast and incomplete. It was also real — real people in real offices, real children in real schools.

Maya: Verdict. The Radicals won the laws: the 14th's four clauses, the five military districts, the amendments. The reunionists won the timeline: quick, lenient, and economically hollow. The Congressman is right that freedom without land stood on bayonets. Marcus is right that the gains were real. The Radicals won the laws; the question was always enforcement. Next time: was Reconstruction a success or a failure? We prosecute that question properly.

Marcus: Restoration or transformation —

Stevens: the nation chose restoration, and the bill came due.
