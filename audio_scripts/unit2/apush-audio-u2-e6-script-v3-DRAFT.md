Maya: Last time: mercantilism, Britain trying to run its empire by the Navigation Acts while salutary neglect let the colonies prosper anyway. But trade wasn't the only thing getting restless. This time: a religious wildfire, two preachers who couldn't be more different, and a Philadelphia printer who thought reason could beat revelation. Three boxes: the two preachers, Franklin's counter-revolution, and the aftershock. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes, starting now.

Marcus: Before the fire, the tinder. By the 1730s, plenty of colonial churches had gone cold: formal, established, ministers reading sermons and sitting down. Back in 1662, New England's Half-Way Covenant let half-committed members baptize their kids: pews stayed full, but membership meant less.

Maya: So church was the DMV. Show up, take a number, nobody's moved.

Marcus: Close enough. A generation was hungry for feeling, for religion that did something to them. The spark: itinerant preachers, town to town, outdoors because no building could hold the crowds.

Maya: I went to an outdoor concert in Phoenix one July. Twenty thousand people, no shade, melting by the second song. Standing through a two-hour sermon in that? Respect.

Marcus: In 1739, a young Englishman in his mid-twenties named George Whitefield crossed the Atlantic and preached in open fields to crowds in the thousands, sometimes twenty thousand or more by the newspapers' counts, in an age with no microphones.

Maya: Wait, how? No speakers, twenty thousand people — how did the back rows hear him?

Marcus: His voice just carried. Benjamin Franklin once tested it in Philadelphia, walking away from a Whitefield sermon to measure how far the voice held, and computed it could reach more than thirty thousand.

Maya: Ben Franklin did a sound check. In 1739.

Marcus: He did, then printed Whitefield's sermons — printers and celebrities make each other famous. Something like eighteen thousand sermons in his lifetime. People call him America's first celebrity.

Maya: So Edwards is the loud one, then, the weeper, acting out his sermons?

Marcus: Backwards. That's Whitefield: the weeping, the acting, the crowds. Edwards barely raised his voice. His weapon was the argument.

Maya: I know his big line, my English teacher put it on a worksheet next to Poe.

Marcus: Then you know the spider. Jonathan Edwards: Massachusetts theologian, deep and careful, everything Whitefield wasn't. July 8, 1741, Enfield, Connecticut: "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God," the most famous sermon in American history.

Maya: "The God that holds you over the pit of hell, much as one holds a spider, or some loathsome insect, over the fire."

Marcus: And the famous account is that he didn't shout it. He read it, nearly flat, barely looking up, while the congregation came apart around him.

Maya: That's scarier, honestly. The calm ones are always scarier.

Marcus: Different weapons, same break from the old style. Whitefield took religion to the fields; Edwards argued you into terror. Both cut out the middleman and put the conversion on the individual.

Maya: Checking box one. The two preachers: Whitefield the showman, Edwards the theologian. The tinder: cold churches, a 1662 membership compromise, and hungry hearts.

Marcus: Box two is the counter-revolution. While Whitefield preached feeling, Benjamin Franklin preached reason. Poor Richard's Almanack, first printed in 1732, ran twenty-five years: work hard, save your money, stay curious, question everything. Behind the jokes, the Enlightenment: reason over revelation, rights from nature, the self-made man as a project, his Autobiography the proof.

Maya: "Early to bed, early to rise" guy. I didn't know that was a whole worldview.

Marcus: And behind the almanac was the Junto, his 1727 debating club: a dozen tradesmen meeting Friday nights to argue morals, politics, philosophy, pooling their books into a lending library. The Enlightenment as a social club.

Maya: So the Awakening crowds are out in fields crying, and the Junto guys are in a tavern going, "Actually, let's reason about this."

Marcus: The Awakening challenged authority through the heart: you don't need the minister, you've had your own conversion. The Enlightenment challenged it through the head: you don't need the old books, you have your own reason. And the Revolution would need both.

Maya: Hold on, the kite. Did he actually fly it in a thunderstorm?

Marcus: The famous story is shakier than the textbooks let on. He proposed the experiment in 1750; French scientists ran the first real trial in 1752. Whether Franklin himself ever stood in a field holding that string, nobody's fully sure. But the electricity work made him an international name before he was a revolutionary.

Maya: Box two, checked: the head to their heart.

Marcus: Box three: the aftershock. The revival fans became New Lights; the old order became Old Lights, and the fights split congregations open. New Light colleges like Princeton were founded to train a different kind of minister.

Maya: Church splits sound small until you think about it: people had to organize. Raise money, pick leaders, argue in public.

Marcus: Right, and that organizing is the aftershock. Ordinary colonists practiced standing up to the established church, and they'd watched it work. And the revival was everywhere at once: the same sermons, the same crowds, from New England down to Georgia. Some historians call it the first thing all the colonies ever experienced together.

Maya: And some don't buy that.

Marcus: Others say that's overstated. The revivals never touched half the colonies, and in some places the fire burned out fast. Which is why the big question is causation: did the Awakening help cause the Revolution?

Maya: Give me both sides.

Marcus: The case for: it trained colonists to defy authority and gave the colonies a shared experience, a rehearsal, some historians say, for 1776. The case against: plenty of patriot leaders were never awakened, the fires died down decades before the war, and maybe both just came from the same restless individualism. I don't think that's settled.

Maya: So the answer is "argue both sides." Very AP of them.

Marcus: Very AP.

Maya: No goodbyes yet.
