Maya: Last time: covenant towns, banished dissenters, King Philip's War. New England, held together by fear of the wrong belief. This time: the colony that ran the opposite experiment. One colony advertised in German. Its founder had been jailed in England for his religion. And by mid-century, arguably the most diverse place in the British Empire. Three boxes: the Holy Experiment, the Pennsylvania Dutch, and the limits of tolerance. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes, and you'll land all three.

Marcus: Start with the man. William Penn, the admiral's son. He became a Quaker around twenty-two, and got jailed in England for it — the Tower of London. And the crown owed his family money. The traditional story says about sixteen thousand pounds, though nobody's found the receipt.

Maya: A colony for a debt. That's one way to collect.

Marcus: 1681: Charles the Second hands Penn a charter for a province west of the Delaware. Penn called it his Holy Experiment. Box one on your sheet. A colony built on Quaker peace and equality, with religious liberty for anyone who acknowledged one Almighty God.

Maya: And the Frame of Government is the constitution part?

Marcus: 1682. An elected assembly, trial by jury, no established church. Radical for the sixteen-hundreds. But here's the thing students miss: the liberty was also a business plan. Empty land pays nothing. Penn needed tens of thousands of settlers to buy land and make the colony work.

Maya: So tolerance as a sales pitch.

Marcus: A sales pitch, yes. In 1681 he published a pamphlet advertising Pennsylvania across Europe, promising cheap land and religious freedom, translated into German and other languages. Within six months he'd parceled out three hundred thousand acres.

Maya: I once bought a concert ticket off a flyer stapled to a telephone pole. A whole colony built from pamphlets. Same energy.

Marcus: Box two: the Pennsylvania Dutch. Who answered the flyer? First the Quakers. Then the Germans: Palatines from the Rhineland, fleeing wars and bad harvests. By the Revolution, roughly a third of Pennsylvania was German. Franklin himself fretted about it in print.

Maya: Pennsylvania Dutch, which is really Deutsch. German. Two years of German class, finally paying rent.

Marcus: It paid off. The Germans came in whole families, and maybe half of them came as redemptioners. They sold years of their labor to pay for the crossing, usually two to seven years. Ship lists filled with German names by the thousand, and by 1739 their own German-language newspaper in Germantown, Christopher Saur's.

Maya: An entire press in German. A whole nation inside a colony.

Marcus: Then the Scots-Irish. Ulster Presbyterians, squeezed by high rents and religious pressure back home. James Logan, Penn's agent, counted six thousand newcomers in a single year in the late 1720s, most of them Scots-Irish. The good land near Philadelphia was taken, so they went west, up the Susquehanna, onto the frontier.

Maya: And the Quakers were fine with that? Pacifists putting fighters on the border?

Marcus: Logan encouraged it. The Scots-Irish became the colony's shield, something like a fourth of Pennsylvania by 1750. Plus the earlier arrivals: Dutch, Swedes, and Welsh Quakers.

Maya: Okay, but what about Penn and the Lenape? The peace is the famous part.

Marcus: The Lenni Lenape: the name means something like "original people." Penn bought land from them instead of seizing it, and he met their leaders under an elm at Shackamaxon. Tradition says, anyway. I don't think anyone's sure the meeting happened the way the paintings show.

Maya: And then he cheated them with the Walking Purchase.

Marcus: Not him. That's the correction everyone needs. Penn died in 1718, back in England. He hadn't set foot in the colony since 1701. In 1737, his sons Thomas and John, with James Logan, pulled the walk.

Maya: The day-and-a-half thing.

Marcus: They produced a deed, unsigned and probably never agreed to, saying the Lenape had sold all the land a man could walk in a day and a half. The Lenape figured a normal walk, maybe forty miles. The Penns cleared a straight path through the woods and hired the colony's fastest runners. One of them covered something like sixty-odd miles. The colony walked away with about twelve hundred square miles, roughly the size of Rhode Island. The Lenape were pushed west.

Maya: So the Holy Experiment's peace lasted just as long as Penn did.

Marcus: I'd call that the honest version.

Maya: Then I'm checking the Holy Experiment: Penn's Frame of Government with liberty as the recruitment pitch, and a peace that died with its founder.

Marcus: Checked.

Maya: Alright, limits. Diversity doesn't mean utopia.

Marcus: Box three: the limits of tolerance. Start with what the land grew. The Middle Colonies — Pennsylvania, New York, the Jerseys, Delaware — grew wheat and corn. The breadbasket of the colonies. Flour shipped to the Caribbean and across the Atlantic. Compare that with tobacco in the Chesapeake and fish in New England. Grain meant small farms and family labor, towns full of millers and merchants instead of plantations.

Maya: And "best poor man's country." That's the phrase, right? An immigrant called Pennsylvania that.

Marcus: An immigrant's phrase, and the exam question is hiding inside it. Cheap land, no established church, a moderate climate. For a poor German farmer, it was the best deal in the Atlantic world. But for whom, exactly?

Maya: Because not everyone got the deal.

Marcus: Thousands of enslaved Africans and African Americans lived and worked in Pennsylvania. Penn himself held enslaved people. Back in 1688, German Quakers in Germantown signed the first protest against slavery in the English colonies, and the Philadelphia meeting set it aside. Catholics were distrusted, especially whenever England was at war with France. And the Lenape — we just walked through what happened to them.

Maya: So was it tolerant, or just crowded with different people?

Marcus: Historians argue about that one, and I don't think it's settled. Some say Pennsylvania wasn't tolerant, just pluralist: lots of groups living side by side, each one suspicious of the others. Tolerance is a principle. Pluralism is a fact. Pennsylvania had the fact.

Maya: And on the test, that's the middle column. New England, Middle, Chesapeake — three different answers to who belongs.

Marcus: One exam-shaped thought, and it's a good one.

Maya: Okay. Let me see if any of this stuck.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: the Holy Experiment. Penn: jailed Quaker, 1681 charter, Frame of Government in 1682, religious liberty as a sales pitch because empty land pays nothing. And the peace with the Lenape that his sons broke with the Walking Purchase in 1737? Penn was already dead?

Marcus: Nineteen years dead. 1718.

Maya: Checking that one. Two: the Pennsylvania Dutch. Germans, actually; their word was Deutsch. About a third of the colony by the Revolution, maybe half of them redemptioners, their own newspaper by 1739. Plus the Scots-Irish on the frontier, something like a fourth of the colony. The Dutch, Swedes, and Welsh were already there. Checking that one too.

Maya: Three: the limits of tolerance. Breadbasket grain instead of tobacco. Best poor man's country: for a poor Protestant farmer, sure. Not for the enslaved, not for Catholics, not for the Lenape. Pluralist, not necessarily tolerant. That one's checked. All three landed.

Maya: Two questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One: a 1730s ship list from Philadelphia. Three hundred names. All German families, and half of them marked "redemptioner." What's the point of this source?

[5-second pause]

Maya: It shows what built Pennsylvania's diversity: not just Penn's pamphlets, but cheap labor. Half those families couldn't afford the crossing, so they sold years of their work up front. Diversity ran on debt.

Maya: Two: a historian writes that Pennsylvania was pluralist rather than tolerant. Using one limit from this episode, explain what she means.

[5-second pause]

Maya: Pluralist means the groups were there; tolerant would mean they were equal. Pick your evidence: the Lenape defrauded in the Walking Purchase, enslaved Africans in a Quaker colony, Catholics distrusted in wartime. Many peoples, one hierarchy.

Maya: Check your three boxes. Next time: the empire's business plan. How mercantilism and the Navigation Acts were supposed to work, and why the smugglers treated them as suggestions.

Marcus: Pennsylvania proved a colony could hold every faith —

Maya: and still not hold them equally.
