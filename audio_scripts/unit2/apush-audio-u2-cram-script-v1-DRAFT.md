# U2 — Cram Session: Maya + Jay (Fish Audio)
# Episode 19: Ten Questions, One Unit. ~8 min.
# Draft v1 (from scratch).
# Read note: Cram format — no boxes on the episode sheet. Ten rapid-fire questions, the period thesis, and the DBQ prediction. No pause marks appear in this script — self-test silence is added in production. Jay is the student: he retrieves, he guesses, he gets things wrong; Maya corrects and lands the memory hooks. The em dash in the closing tagline is a held beat: leave a full breath between Jay's setup line and Maya's landing line. Do not rush it. Strip this header and the read note before TTS.
# Pronunciation: koo-RUHR duh BWAH (coureurs de bois), pah-TROON (patroon), REN-suh-lerz-WAYK (Rensselaerswijck), STY-vuh-sunt (Stuyvesant), WEN-dat (Wendat), see-nyoor (seigneur), meh-tah-KOM (Metacom), ah-beh-NAH-kee (Abenaki)

Maya: Last time: the French and Indian War, the war Britain won so completely it broke its own empire. This time: no new material. Ten questions, the whole unit, rapid-fire. Answer all ten out loud and you're ready. About eight minutes. Go.

Jay: 1607 to 1754. The whole colonial century in ten questions. Hit me.

Maya: Question one: match the model. Spain, France, Holland, England. One sentence each.

Jay: Spain: souls and silver. Missions and mines, biggest empire, most bureaucracy. France: the fur trade. Beaver pelts, the coureurs de bois paddling past the legal posts, Jesuit missionaries doubling as diplomats. Holland: the company colony. The Dutch West India Company running New Netherland as a company town, patroons as feudal lords on the Hudson. England: settler families. Joint-stock companies paying the bills, refugees and servants doing the work, farms replacing forts.

Maya: Spain folded Native peoples into its labor machine, England pushed them off the land. France made allies of convenience, Holland made customers. Question one, landed.

Jay: Question two: why did Jamestown survive when it should have died?

Maya: Winter 1609. Five hundred settlers drop to sixty. The starving time. So why didn't it become Roanoke?

Jay: Tobacco. John Rolfe's sweet-scented strain. The headright system: fifty acres for every person you brought over, which paid people to import labor. Private property: once people owned their own land, they worked it.

Maya: Plus the hard parts: John Smith's work-or-starve discipline, and the Powhatan, first trade, then war. And 1619: the House of Burgesses, the first elected assembly, meeting the same year the first enslaved Africans arrived. The Chesapeake template gets set here: staple crop, imported labor, scattered plantations, always hungry for more workers.

Jay: Question three: Bacon's Rebellion to the slave codes. Walk the chain.

Maya: 1676. Start with the tinder.

Jay: Nathaniel Bacon leads frontier farmers against Governor Berkeley. The frontier wants war on the Indians, tidewater wants peace and the fur trade. The rebels burn Jamestown. Bacon dies of dysentery, the rebellion collapses.

Maya: The part the planters never forgot?

Jay: The rebels were poor whites and enslaved Black people, fighting side by side. A united underclass, which terrified the planter class.

Maya: So the planters decided: never again. They pivoted to enslaved African labor, lifetime and hereditary, a status you could see. Then they wrote it into law. The slave codes of 1705: slavery defined by race, for life, passed to children. Race invented as law. Edmund Morgan's argument: white Virginians bought their freedom with Black slavery.

Jay: Question four: why did the Puritans come, and how did covenant shape their towns?

Maya: 1630. John Winthrop. A city upon a hill. What were they running from?

Jay: Persecution. Archbishop Laud cracking down, plus economic pressure. The Great Migration of the 1630s. They wanted a godly society: covenant theology, a contract with God organizing everything. Towns built around the meetinghouse, church membership tied to voting, town meetings running local life.

Maya: The covenant cut both ways: a community of saints had no room for dissenters. Roger Williams gets banished in 1635, founds Rhode Island, and says the land should be bought from the Natives. Anne Hutchinson gets exiled in 1638 for saying the ministers got grace wrong. Then King Philip's War, 1675. Metacom's uprising. Per capita the deadliest war in American history, and Native power in New England broken forever. The city upon a hill ended up banishing its own and fighting a war of extermination. Aspiration versus reality.

Jay: Question five: what made the Middle Colonies the diversity lab of colonial America?

Maya: Pennsylvania. William Penn's holy experiment.

Jay: Quakers, Germans, the Pennsylvania Dutch which is actually Deutsch, Scots-Irish pushed to the frontier, plus Dutch and Swedes left over. Penn advertised across Europe. Cheap land, religious freedom. Grain economy instead of tobacco, so smaller farms, more towns. The Frame of Government, the long peace with the Lenape.

Maya: And the limits.

Jay: The Walking Purchase of 1737, one of the great land frauds. The peace ends. Catholics distrusted, enslaved Africans present, Native peoples dispossessed in the end anyway.

Maya: Tolerant or merely pluralist? That's the historian's argument.

Jay: Question six: mercantilism in one paragraph. Theory, Navigation Acts, and why the colonists dodged them.

Maya: One paragraph. Breathe first.

Jay: Mercantilists believed the world's wealth was fixed: grab the gold, keep a favorable balance of trade. The Navigation Acts, starting 1651: certain goods could only ship to England, only on English ships, European goods only through England. The colonists dodged them with smuggling. John Hancock made his fortune at it. Bribed customs men. The Molasses Act of 1733, which everybody ignored. And the reason it all worked: salutary neglect. Walpole letting sleeping dogs lie. The system functioned because London never enforced it.

Maya: Which is why 1763 feels like a betrayal. But that's Unit 3's problem. The exam move: evaluate the extent. Did mercantilism help the colonies, with protected markets and naval defense, or hurt them, with higher prices and strangled trade? Argue both sides.

Jay: Question seven: what was the Great Awakening's political aftershock?

Maya: 1740s. George Whitefield drawing crowds of thousands with no microphone. Jonathan Edwards preaching Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God in 1741. What did it teach people?

Jay: More religion, I guess? Fuller churches?

Maya: The trap answer. Churches did split — Old Lights versus New Lights — but the aftershock was political. Ordinary people learned to organize, argue, and walk out — the first intercolonial experience, the same revival happening everywhere at once.

Maya: Alongside it: the Enlightenment. Franklin. Poor Richard's Almanack, the Junto debating society, the electricity experiments. Reason instead of revelation. The Awakening challenged authority through the heart, the Enlightenment through the head. The Revolution would need both. Causation question: did the revival cause the Revolution, or did both grow from the same restless individualism? Pick a side and defend it.

Jay: Question eight: how did assemblies get powerful while nobody in London was watching?

Maya: Follow the money.

Jay: The power of the purse. The assemblies voted the taxes and paid the governor's salary, so the governor answered to them — decades of salutary neglect turning precedent into power. The House of Burgesses staring down governors one budget fight at a time.

Maya: Plus the Zenger trial, 1735. A printer jailed for criticizing the governor, acquitted by a jury after his lawyer argued truth is a defense against libel. The rehearsal for the pamphlet wars. And Anglicization: the more British the colonists felt, reading English papers and drinking English tea, the more they demanded English rights. By 1750 the most democratic legislatures in the empire weren't in London.

Jay: Question nine: the French and Indian War. Why did Britain win, and what did victory cost?

Maya: Start the war.

Jay: 1754. A twenty-two-year-old George Washington ambushes a French party in the Ohio Valley and starts a world war. Then the disaster years: Braddock's defeat in 1755, the British marching in formation through American woods, annihilated. Then Pitt takes over in 1757, pours everything into North America. Quebec falls in 1759. Wolfe and Montcalm both die, New France is finished. Treaty of Paris, 1763: France loses Canada, Britain owns everything east of the Mississippi.

Maya: And the cost?

Jay: The debt. The biggest empire on earth and an empty treasury. Plus Pontiac's Rebellion in 1763: the Ohio peoples refuse British rule the moment the French leave. And the Proclamation Line, London panicking and banning settlement past the Appalachians. Also the Albany Plan of 1754: Franklin's attempt at colonial union, and every colony rejected it. Disunited going in, broke coming out.

Maya: The double legacy: victory and the bill. It's where every grievance of the next unit starts.

Jay: Last one. Question ten: in one sentence, why did 1763 change everything?

Maya: Your turn first.

Jay: Britain won the war and lost the peace? The debt made London end salutary neglect, and the colonies were too used to running themselves.

Maya: Say it like a thesis: a century of growth made the colonies economically vital, culturally diverse, and politically self-confident. So when Britain tried to reassert control after 1763, the old arrangement broke.

Jay: The period thesis.

Maya: The period thesis. Prediction beat: two Document-Based Questions I'd bet on. One: evaluate the extent to which the British colonies had developed a distinct identity and self-government by 1754. Two: evaluate the role of salutary neglect in colonial autonomy. Was the break with Britain a change or a betrayal?

Jay: The answers are sitting in these ten questions. Assemblies, the Awakening, the war's bill.

Maya: Ten questions, landed. Next time: the bill comes due. Taxes, boycotts, and the road to revolution.

Jay: Growth made them valuable —

Maya: And ungovernable.
