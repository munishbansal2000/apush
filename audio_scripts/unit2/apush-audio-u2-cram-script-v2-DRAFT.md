Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: the French and Indian War, the war Britain won so completely that paying for it broke the colonial arrangement. This time: no new material. Ten questions, 1607 to 1763, a century and a half, in about thirteen minutes. The dare: say every answer out loud before Jay does. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now.

Jay: [sheepish] Ten questions. Last cram I called the patroons the patronuses. Different franchise, same confidence.

Maya: [energetic] Redemption arc. Question one: match the model. Spain, France, Holland, England. One sentence each.

[2-second pause]

Jay: [casual] Spain: souls and silver. Missions and mines, the biggest empire, the most bureaucracy.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] France: what did they actually want?

Jay: [casual] Beaver pelts. The fur trade, the coureurs de bois paddling past the legal posts, Jesuit missionaries doubling as diplomats, seigneurs holding long thin farms along the St. Lawrence. And almost nobody there. The crown barred the Huguenots, so New France stayed thin on the ground.

Maya: [conversational] Holland: the company colony.

Jay: [casual] The Dutch West India Company running New Netherland as a company town, patroons as feudal lords on the Hudson. Rensselaerswijck: one family's private colony.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And England?

Jay: [casual] Settler families. Joint-stock companies paying the bills, refugees and servants doing the work, farms replacing forts.

Maya: [conversational] Four models, one coastline. And four Native answers. Spain folded Native peoples into its labor machine. France allied with the Wendat. Holland traded. England pushed them off the land. Question one, landed.

Maya: [energetic] Question two: why did Jamestown survive when it should have died?

[2-second pause]

Jay: [casual] Winter 1609. About five hundred going in, about sixty coming out. The starving time.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] They stayed anyway. What saved it?

Jay: [casual] Tobacco. Rolfe's Caribbean strain, around 1612, sweeter than the native leaf. The headright: fifty acres to whoever paid the passage. The planter who paid, not the servant who sailed. Indentured servants worked it off, four to seven years. Private property: once people owned their own land, they worked it.

Maya: [conversational] Plus John Smith's rule while it lasted: "He that will not work, shall not eat." And the Powhatan: trade first, then war. 1622: Opechancanough's attack, 347 dead, about a quarter of the colony. The company lost its charter in 1624, and Virginia became a royal colony.

Jay: [casual] And 1619: the House of Burgesses meets in the summer, and the White Lion lands "20 and odd" Africans at Point Comfort in late August. The assembly and the landing, same year. The Chesapeake template gets set here: staple crop, imported labor, scattered plantations, always hungry for more workers.

Maya: [conversational] Staple crop, imported labor, hunger for workers. Two down, and the template's set.

Maya: [energetic] Question three: Bacon's Rebellion to the slave codes. Walk the chain.

[2-second pause]

Jay: [casual] 1676. Nathaniel Bacon leads frontier farmers against Governor Berkeley. The frontier wants war on the Indians, tidewater wants peace and the fur trade. The rebels burn Jamestown. Bacon dies of dysentery, the rebellion collapses.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] The part the planters never forgot?

Jay: [casual] The rebels were poor whites and enslaved Black people, fighting side by side. So the planters invented race with the 1705 code. Laws first, racism after.

Maya: [firm] Not quite — that's the trap answer. The 1705 code didn't invent slavery. It consolidated decades of one-off laws: 1640, 1662, 1667. And whether the racism came before the enslavement or grew out of it, that's a live debate, argued both ways. Never teach one direction as settled.

Jay: [casual] The law was already moving that way. 1640: three runaways caught, two white, one Black. The white men get four more years; John Punch gets life. Heimler says it signaled a significant shift toward race-based slavery in Virginia.

Maya: [measured] And the legal template came from the sugar islands. Barbados wrote one of the first comprehensive slave codes in 1661, and Carolina's planters carried it north when they founded the colony in 1663. Virginia's 1705 code gathered its own decades of law into one book. The laws stacked up first: 1662 made slavery hereditary, the child's status following the mother. Then 1705: slavery for life, people held as real estate, property passed to heirs like land.

Jay: [casual] And Morgan's line: white Virginians bought their freedom with Black slavery. Race divided the underclass so it could never unite again.

Maya: [conversational] Freedom and slavery, growing up together. Three down. The chain holds.

Maya: [energetic] Question four: why did the Puritans come, and how did covenant shape their towns?

[2-second pause]

Jay: [casual] 1630. Winthrop's fleet. Archbishop Laud cracking down back home, the economy sour, thousands cross in the Great Migration of the 1630s. They wanted a godly society: covenant theology, a contract with God organizing everything. Towns built around the meetinghouse, church membership tied to voting, town meetings running local life.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And when the second generation couldn't prove conversion?

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] The Half-Way Covenant? 1662, partial membership: baptized, in the pews, but voting and communion stayed off-limits.

Maya: [serious tone] The covenant cut both ways: a community of saints had no room for dissenters. Roger Williams, banished in 1635, he founds Rhode Island and says the land should be bought from the Natives. Anne Hutchinson, exiled in 1638, for saying the ministers got grace wrong.

Jay: [casual] Then King Philip's War, 1675 to '76. Metacom, the son of Massasoit, the sachem who'd made peace with Plymouth in 1621, rises against the English.

Maya: [measured] Britannica calls it one of the bloodiest conflicts per capita in U.S. history. Seventeen white settlements destroyed and fifty more damaged. About six hundred English soldiers killed. Thousands of Native people dead, and many of the captives sold into slavery. Native military power in New England, broken.

Jay: [casual] The city upon a hill, banishing its own and fighting its neighbors. Aspiration versus reality.

Maya: [conversational] And the book everyone read afterward: Mary Rowlandson's captivity narrative, 1682, the English telling of the war. Four down. Covenant, dissent, war.

Maya: [energetic] Question five: what made the Middle Colonies the diversity lab of colonial America?

[2-second pause]

Jay: [casual] Pennsylvania. William Penn's holy experiment. Quakers, Germans, the Pennsylvania Dutch, which is actually Deutsch, Scots-Irish pushed to the frontier, plus Dutch and Swedes left over from New Netherland. Penn advertised across Europe. Cheap land, religious freedom.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And the economy underneath it?

Jay: [casual] Grain instead of tobacco. Smaller farms, more towns, Philadelphia growing into British North America's biggest city. The breadbasket. It got so German that by 1751 Franklin was fretting the Germans would Germanize Pennsylvania instead of the other way around.

Maya: [conversational] Every experiment had its limits.

Jay: [casual] The Walking Purchase of 1737, one of the great land frauds. The Lenape lose the land, the peace ends. Catholics distrusted, enslaved Africans present, Native peoples dispossessed in the end anyway.

Maya: [thoughtful tone] Tolerant or merely pluralist? That's the historian's argument. Five down, five to go.

Maya: [energetic] Question six: mercantilism in one paragraph. Theory, Navigation Acts, and why the colonists dodged them.

[2-second pause]

Jay: [casual] Mercantilists believed the world's wealth was fixed: grab the gold, keep a favorable balance of trade. The Navigation Acts, starting 1651: certain goods could only ship to England, only on English ships, European goods only through England. Tobacco led the enumerated list, and the list kept growing: rice, naval stores. Parliament fenced off colonial factories too: wool, hats, iron.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] What moved underneath the laws?

Jay: [casual] The triangle. But the triangle names a pattern, not the average voyage. Plenty of ships ran two legs and never closed it. New England rum sailed straight to the Caribbean. Most deals were bilateral. The chain was still real: Caribbean sugar fed New England's distilleries, and enslaved labor fed the sugar plantations.

Maya: [conversational] And the dodge?

Jay: [casual] Smuggling. John Hancock made his fortune at it. Bribed customs men. The Molasses Act of 1733, which everybody ignored.

Maya: [playful] I would've been the world's worst smuggler. I confess before anybody asks. [chuckle]

Jay: [casual] And the reason the dodge worked: salutary neglect. The policy ran for decades before it had a name: Edmund Burke coined the phrase in 1775, looking back.

Maya: [measured] Walpole's the name attached to it: "let sleeping dogs lie." Nobody's fully sure he actually said those words. But the neglect was real: understaffed customs, governors looking the other way, 1721 to 1742.

Maya: [firm] The exam move: evaluate the extent. Did mercantilism help the colonies, with protected markets and naval defense, or hurt them, with higher prices and strangled trade? Argue both sides. And remember who buried the theory. Adam Smith, 1776: "Nothing, however, can be more absurd than this whole doctrine of the balance of trade." The economic case against mercantilism lands the same year as the political case against the empire.

Maya: [energetic] Question seven: what was the Great Awakening's political aftershock?

[2-second pause]

Jay: [casual] The 1740s. George Whitefield, mid-twenties, preaching to open fields in 1739, crowds in the thousands, newspapers claiming twenty-five thousand. Jonathan Edwards preaching Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God at Enfield in 1741.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So it packed the churches. What did it teach people?

Jay: [casual] More religion, I guess? Fuller churches?

Maya: [firm] The trap answer. Churches did split — Old Lights versus New Lights — but the aftershock was political. The same revival swept colony after colony, and ordinary people learned to run their own meetings, and to walk out of the ones they didn't like.

Maya: [conversational] Alongside it: the Enlightenment. Franklin. Poor Richard's Almanack from 1732, the Junto debating society, the electricity experiments. Reason instead of revelation. The Awakening challenged authority through the heart, the Enlightenment through the head. And the revivals needed ministers: new colleges rose to train them, Princeton in 1746.

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] So which one caused the Revolution?

Maya: [thoughtful tone] The causation question: did the revival cause the Revolution, or did both grow from the same restless individualism? Pick a side and defend it.

Maya: [energetic] Question eight: how did assemblies get powerful while nobody in London was watching?

[2-second pause]

Jay: [casual] Follow the money. The assemblies voted the taxes and paid the governor's salary, so the governor answered to the people who signed his paycheck. Decades of precedent turning into power.

Maya: [conversational] Every colony's assembly played the same move. Starve the salary until the governor signs. Massachusetts ran the playbook in the 1720s: the royal governor demanded a fixed salary, and the House kept voting his pay year by year instead.

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] And the Zenger trial: the printer who criticized the governor and walked?

Maya: [conversational] 1735. Jailed for criticizing the governor, acquitted by a jury after his lawyer argued truth is a defense against libel. The rehearsal for the pamphlet wars.

Jay: [casual] And Anglicization: the more British the colonists felt, English papers, English tea, the more they demanded English rights. By 1750 the most democratic legislatures in the empire weren't in London.

Maya: [conversational] Eight down. Two to go.

Maya: [energetic] Question nine: the French and Indian War. Why did Britain win, and what did victory cost?

[2-second pause]

Jay: [casual] 1754. A twenty-two-year-old George Washington ambushes a French party in the Ohio country and starts a world war. Then the disaster years: 1755, Braddock marches on Fort Duquesne with nearly fifteen hundred regulars, and a smaller French and Native force shoots the column to pieces from the tree line at the Monongahela. Nearly a thousand killed or wounded. Braddock dies four days later. Washington rides out with four bullets through his coat and two horses shot under him.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And the turnaround?

Jay: [casual] Pitt takes over in 1757 and pours everything into North America. Quebec falls in 1759. Wolfe and Montcalm both die, New France is finished. Treaty of Paris, 1763: France loses Canada, Britain takes Canada and all French territory east of the Mississippi, plus Florida from Spain, while France kept the sugar islands, Guadeloupe and Martinique.

Maya: [serious tone] And the cost?

Jay: [speaking slowly] The debt. An enormous debt. The books won't pin the number, and neither will I.

Maya: [conversational] Plus the land fights. Pontiac's Rebellion in 1763: Detroit first, then six other forts. Over four hundred British soldiers and some two thousand colonists killed or captured before Gage broke it. Neolin was the prophet, Pontiac was the war chief. London draws the Proclamation Line: no settlement past the Appalachians. Not punishment. Triage. Another frontier war was the one thing London couldn't afford.

Jay: [casual] And the Albany Plan of 1754: Franklin's attempt at colonial union. The colonies said no first, guarding their own power, then London said no too. Disunited going in, broke coming out.

Maya: [confident tone] The double legacy: victory and the bill. It's where every grievance of the next unit starts.

Maya: [energetic] Last one. Question ten: in one sentence, why did 1763 change everything?

[2-second pause]

Jay: [casual] Britain won the war and lost the peace? The debt ended salutary neglect, and the colonies were too used to running themselves.

Maya: [confident tone] The period thesis. A century and a half of growth made the colonies economically vital, culturally diverse, and politically self-confident. So when Britain tried to reassert control after 1763, the old arrangement broke.

Maya: Two predictions I'd bet on. One: evaluate the extent to which the British colonies had developed a distinct identity and self-government by 1754. Your turn.

[8-second pause]

Maya: The assemblies holding the purse, the Awakening teaching people to organize, a century and a half of running their own affairs, and an ocean between them and the king. The identity was already distinct; the war's bill just made it a problem.

Maya: Two: evaluate the role of salutary neglect in colonial autonomy. Your turn.

[8-second pause]

Maya: The neglect built the autonomy: a century of assemblies voting money, governors looking the other way, smugglers everybody knew by name. Then 1763, and London tried to enforce everything at once. Was the break a change or a betrayal? Argue it both ways.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Ten questions, landed. Next time: the bill comes due. Taxes, boycotts, and the road to revolution.

Jay: [professional broadcast tone] Growth made them valuable —

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] And ungovernable.
