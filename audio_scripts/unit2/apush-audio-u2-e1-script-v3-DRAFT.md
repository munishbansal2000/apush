# Pronunciation: koo-RUHR duh BWAH (coureurs de bois), ehn-koh-mee-EHN-dah (encomienda), REN-suh-lerz-WAYK (Rensselaerswijck), STY-vuh-sunt (Stuyvesant), WEN-dat (Wendat), KAH-stah (casta), kree-OH-yohs (criollos)

Maya: Last time: the Unit 1 cram — one ocean made one world, on conquest's terms. This time the question isn't who arrived. It's how four empires wanted the same continent and built four different machines to get it. Three boxes on your sheet: Spanish missions, the French fur trade, the company colonies. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes, and you'll check all three off.

Marcus: Start with the one you already know. Spain spent eight episodes building an empire out of two words: souls and silver. Missions to convert Native peoples, mines to enrich the crown. The encomienda handed a colonist a whole town's labor, and every time the towns emptied, the crown rebuilt the machine instead of the goal. Of the four, Spain ruled the most people and ran the most bureaucracy.

Maya: So Spain's model was convert and extract, with paperwork.

Marcus: With the crown's stamp on every page. Missions and mines, plus a labor system the crown kept renaming every time the last one broke. And up in the borderlands, in Florida and New Mexico, the missions ran the show for the better part of a century, until the Pueblos answered in 1680.

Maya: And the mixing had official labels, right? Like the bureaucracy followed you into your family tree?

Marcus: That's the casta system, and it grew out of the same machine. Spain loved a category. Peninsulares at the top: Spaniards born in Spain. Then criollos: Spaniards born in the Americas. Then a whole vocabulary for the mixture: mestizos, mulatos, and dozens of finer slices below them — indios and Africans holding the bottom rung. Your label followed you into court, into church, into every appointment. The paperwork didn't just track labor. It ranked people.

Maya: A whole government of who your grandparents were. France skipped the souls and went for the hats.

Marcus: France played the opposite game. Not land, not souls in bulk — beaver. Europe wanted felt hats, and felt hats needed beaver pelts, so the whole colony bent around the fur trade. The coureurs de bois, the runners of the woods, paddled past the legal posts and learned the languages. Some married into the trading towns.

Maya: Wait — "past the legal posts." They were smugglers?

Marcus: In everything but name. The crown licensed the fur trade; the woodsmen just ignored the license.

Maya: I have to confess something. I own a beaver-felt fedora. Vintage shop, twelve dollars. I've been wearing colonial currency on my head.

Marcus: Your hat was once the engine of an empire.

Maya: So were the French the nice ones? Trading instead of conquering?

Marcus: The convenient ones. The fur trade needed allies, so the French picked sides: guns and trade goods to the Huron, the Wendat, against the Iroquois. Partnership on the ledger, strategy on the map. Ask the Iroquois how nice it felt.

Maya: So alliances of convenience. Nobody was nice, everybody had interests.

Marcus: Write that on the box.

Maya: What about the Jesuits? Were they part of the trade or the soul business?

Marcus: Both, and that's the French trick. The Jesuits lived in Wendat villages for years and learned the language. Then they wrote it all down. The Jesuit Relations are some of the most detailed European writing about Native life anywhere in North America, and the missionaries doubled as diplomats for the trade. You can't write that book from inside a fort.

Maya: Okay, then why was New France so empty? Roughly sixty thousand people against more than a million English. How does a whole empire run that thin?

Marcus: The reasons stack. The crown barred the Huguenots, so France's natural refugees sailed for the English and Dutch instead. The land went out as seigneuries, big feudal grants. Most seigneurs never paid the passage they'd promised their tenants. The crown held the reins directly. And a fur economy never needed farmers. Historians argue about which one mattered most — together, they explain the empty map.

Marcus: Then Holland, the odd one out. New Netherland wasn't really a kingdom's colony. It belonged to the Dutch West India Company: a company town with a flag. The company wanted furs and a harbor, not citizens. The governor answered to merchants in Amsterdam. No king in the chain of command. Stuyvesant ran the place like a company store with a church attached.

Maya: Patroons — wait, was that the Dutch thing? Like, the lords?

Marcus: The Dutch thing, yes. 1629: the company told its investors, ship fifty settlers across and we'll hand you a feudal estate on the Hudson, you as patroon, lord of the manor. Sixteen miles of riverfront if you played it right. The one that actually worked was Rensselaerswijck, up around present-day Albany. Most of the other lords couldn't find tenants.

Maya: Feudal lords on the Hudson. That sounds backwards for the 1600s.

Marcus: Backwards is the word. Meanwhile the town worked. New Amsterdam drew merchants from everywhere, a jumble of languages on the docks. Its doors stood open to people other colonies shut out, because the company needed customers.

Maya: I actually know something here. There's a theory the word Yankee comes from a Dutch name, Jan Kees. True?

Marcus: Nobody's fully sure. It's the kind of etymology that sounds too good to check. Probably tied to the Dutch in New York, but the paper trail runs cold.

Maya: So the student knows one you don't. Noted.

Maya: And then England just takes the whole thing? 1664?

Marcus: 1664. An English fleet sailed into the harbor, Governor Stuyvesant looked at his defenses, and surrendered without a shot fired. The English renamed it New York, after the king's brother. The Dutch snatched it back in 1673, then traded it away for good the next year, swapped for Suriname. One English coastline, New England to Carolina.

Marcus: And England — you lived this box last unit. The callback version: companies paid the bills, refugees and servants did the work, and families on farms replaced soldiers in forts. Spain folded Native peoples into its labor machine. England pushed them off the land.

Maya: Okay, so the essay asks me to compare Spanish and English colonization. What's the move?

Marcus: Collide, don't list. Spain: crown-run, extractive, missions and mines, Native peoples folded into the labor machine. England: company-funded, settler-driven, families and farms, Native peoples pushed aside. Two different hungers, same continent.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: Spanish missions. Souls and silver, missions and mines, the encomienda's long shadow, and the casta ladder: your label followed you everywhere.

Marcus: Checked.

Maya: Two: the French fur trade. Coureurs de bois, the Huron alliance against the Iroquois, the Jesuits living in the villages and writing the Relations.

Marcus: On the sheet.

Maya: Three: the company colonies. Holland — the patroon system. The patroon was the lord, right, not the fifty farmers?

Marcus: The lord. The farmers were his tenants.

Maya: The lord. New Amsterdam, jumble of languages, surrendered in 1664, traded for Suriname. And England under the same box: company money, settler families; and land that got filled instead of traded.

Marcus: And that's the set.

Maya: Two questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One: a textbook prints an excerpt from the Jesuit Relations, a Jesuit's detailed account of Wendat village life. The prompt asks: what's the point of including this source? What does the source itself tell you about the French model?

[5-second pause]

Maya: The point isn't just the facts about the Wendat. It's that the source exists at all. The French lived close enough to write it. A trade empire needed allies, so its missionaries doubled as diplomats. That book doesn't get written from inside a fort.

Maya: Two: match each signature to its empire. Missions. The fur trade. Patroons. Settler families.

[5-second pause]

Maya: Missions: Spain. The fur trade: France. Patroons: Holland. Settler families: England.

Maya: Check your three boxes. Episode one in the books. Next time: from servitude to slavery. 1676, Bacon's Rebellion — poor farmers and enslaved workers rising side by side, and the planters who swore never again.

Marcus: Four empires, four appetites —

Maya: and one continent that paid the bill.
