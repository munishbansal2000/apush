# Pronunciation: POW-uh-tan (Powhatan); poh-kuh-HON-tuhs (Pocahontas); oh-puh-chan-kuh-NOH (Opechancanough); KAL-vert (Calvert); SES-ul (Cecil).

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: the whole unit in one sitting, ten questions, 1607 to 1763, no new material. This time: back to the Chesapeake, where English America started. Not the founding, you know that story. The society tobacco built on top of it. Four boxes: the killing ground, rivers not towns, the planter class, and the legend. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Twelve minutes.

Marcus: [serious tone] The founding you learned in the Jamestown episode, 1607, company money, the starving time, Smith's rule, Rolfe's tobacco, the headright, the Burgesses, 1619 twice over, the 1622 attack. This episode starts where that one ends: the region the template built, over the next hundred years.

Maya: [conversational] Box one: the killing ground. Sounds cheerful.

Marcus: [serious tone] It wasn't. The fevers never left. Malaria, dysentery, typhoid, the same diseases that killed the first settlers kept killing their children. Roughly ten years off a newcomer's life expectancy. Few people lived to forty or fifty.

Maya: [incredulous] Ten years off your life. So the dying was front-loaded? The first year did it?

Marcus: [measured] Just for landing there. The first year was the worst of it, historians call it seasoning, and the seasoning killed. The ones who survived it, and the children born in Virginia after them, built up resistance. That's how the colony eventually stabilized: the dying was worst for the freshly arrived.

Maya: [conversational] So the colony was a machine for importing people and burying them.

Marcus: [measured] For a long time, yes. And the people it imported were mostly young men. More than three out of four Chesapeake migrants came as indentured servants, and nearly half of them didn't survive their term.

Maya: [conversational] So families barely existed.

Marcus: [measured] Barely, early on. Around 1650, men outnumbered women roughly six to one. By 1700 it had narrowed toward three to two. For generations, eligible women married fast and marriages ended fast, many broken by death within seven years. Roughly half the children born in early Virginia didn't live to twenty. Grandparents were a rumor. An eligible woman in 1650 Virginia didn't stay single long, and every bachelor in the county knew it.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Why did anyone keep coming?

Marcus: [measured] Because England was worse for the poor. No land, no work, no future. A seven-year gamble on Virginia beat a certain nothing at home. And the colony couldn't grow any other way. For most of the 1600s, it grew by boats, not by births.

Maya: Your turn. You've survived your seven years, Virginia, 1660. You're free. You want land and a wife. What's standing in your way?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Land's the problem. The headright stacked acres onto the men who imported you, so the good riverfront is taken. And wives are scarce, roughly six men to every woman, remember. You wanted a farm. The system wanted your labor.

Marcus: [measured] And that frustration doesn't stay quiet. You saw what it became, in the episode on servitude and slavery.

Maya: [firm] One exam warning for box one: the trap is the growth story. If a question asks why the Chesapeake grew slowly before 1700, the answer is death. Disease, plus a migration that was mostly young men.

Maya: [conversational] And if it asks why it grew at all — like New England, natural increase doing the work?

Marcus: [firm] Not quite. Natural increase came late to the Chesapeake. Fresh boats fed it.

Marcus: [measured] It did turn, eventually. The native-born generation built up resistance to the fevers, more women arrived, and families started to hold. By 1700, Virginia held nearly sixty thousand people, the most populous English colony in America. The killing ground became a society. But it took most of a century.

Marcus: [measured] Now the map. Tobacco needed fresh soil and river frontage, so plantations strung out along the James, the York, the Rappahannock, long ribbons of big houses, each with its own wharf.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So where's the town? The courthouse, the market?

Marcus: [measured] Forget Boston. These counties never built town meetings, and no meetinghouse anchored a village green. The rivers were the roads. A ship could sail right up to your wharf and load your tobacco, so your neighbors were miles of water away.

Maya: [conversational] My cousin lives out on the Delta, and the river's just how you get places out there, everybody's got a boat, one dock where the driveway should be.

Marcus: [measured] The Chesapeake in one image. And here's why the exam cares: put that map next to New England, clustered towns, the meetinghouse at the center, families, and you've got the classic regional comparison. Same empire, same century, two societies, because the crop and the land system were different. That's the comparison the exam keeps circling back to, and now you can see why.

Maya: [conversational] So Virginia built towns too? River towns, planter-style?

Marcus: [firm] Common mix-up: don't call this a town-building colony. Virginia built no Boston, nothing like it.

Maya: [confident tone] Exam tip for box two: if a stimulus hands you a map of plantations strung along rivers with no towns, read the rivers. The pattern is the answer: scattered, river-based, plantation.

Maya: [conversational] Two boxes down, and that one's earned: rivers not towns, checked.

Marcus: [measured] Box three: the planter class. The headright didn't just bring people over. Fifty acres a head to whoever paid the passage, you know the machine. The effect: men who could import servants by the dozen stacked up thousands of acres of the best riverfront.

Maya: [conversational] So the system manufactured its own aristocracy.

Marcus: [measured] It did. By the late 1600s, a small planter class owned the riverfront, grew the tobacco, and ran the House of Burgesses. And tobacco ate the soil, so the hunger for fresh land never stopped. That hunger is what keeps pushing the frontier, and the frontier is where the last box lives.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Maryland's the same story, right? Catholic refuge, same crop?

Marcus: [measured] Same playbook, different founder. The charter Charles the First granted the Calverts in 1632, and the first settlers landed in 1634. Maryland was something new in English America: the first proprietary colony. Not a company, one owner. The crown figured a single proprietor would answer to the king faster than a board of investors. Calvert wanted a refuge for Catholics, and a profit from tobacco. He got the tobacco.

Maya: [conversational] So Maryland stays Catholic. The refuge works, everybody worships in peace.

Marcus: [measured] Not quite. Protestants outnumbered the Catholics almost immediately, the refuge filled with the people it was founded to shelter Catholics from. The colony passed the Act of Toleration in 1649, protecting Christian worship. The Catholics needed the law in their own colony.

Maya: [firm] Exam warning for box three: the exam re-tests the headright as a trap. The fifty acres followed the man who paid the passage, never the servant who sailed on it.

Marcus: [serious tone] Last box: the legend. John Smith, captured by the Powhatan, about to be executed, and the chief's young daughter throws herself across his body and saves him.

Maya: [conversational] I've seen the Disney movie approximately forty times.

Marcus: [measured] Keep the name and the river. Lose almost everything else. That rescue scene comes from Smith's own book, his Generall Historie, published in 1624, roughly seventeen years after it supposedly happened. His earlier accounts never mention it.

Maya: Your turn. You're Smith in 1624. Your colony's founding is famous, you're selling a book, and Pocahontas is dead. What goes in the new chapter?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The rescue. The story that makes you the hero of your own book.

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] Exactly. And historians split on what it was. Some say he invented it outright. Others say he walked through an adoption ritual he didn't understand, the chief symbolically killing him and raising him as an ally, with the daughter playing her part. Nobody's fully sure.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So the Disney version, the brave girl who stops the war, that's Smith's PR department?

Marcus: [measured] With better songs. The real Pocahontas was captured by the English, married to Rolfe, and shown at court in London. Then she died there. The legend needed a heroine. The colony needed land.

Maya: [conversational] And Pocahontas herself, she married Rolfe, sailed to England...

Marcus: [serious tone] And died there in 1617, about twenty. The legend grew in England while the real story in Virginia ran the other way. The 1622 attack didn't end anything. In 1644, Opechancanough, Powhatan's brother, now an old man, tried again. The English crushed it, captured him in 1646, and he was killed while a prisoner. The treaties that followed drew a boundary and set aside reservation land, and a 1677 treaty added a yearly tribute to the English king. Powhatan power was broken.

Maya: [conversational] So the legend gets told in London while the people in it get pushed off the map.

Marcus: [measured] You need both stories. The rescue made Smith a hero. The wars made the Chesapeake English. The legend England remembered, and the loss England carried out.

Maya: [confident tone] Exam tip for the last box: when the source is Smith writing about Smith, read the author before the story. A stimulus from the Generall Historie tests source criticism: read the author behind the story.

Maya: [conversational] So for the timeline question: 1622. Powhatan's last stand, story over.

Marcus: [firm] Common mistake: 1622 didn't end anything. The 1644 war and the 1646 treaty are the exam's later dates.

Maya: [incredulous] Four boxes, let's land them. One: the killing ground. Malaria, dysentery, typhoid, roughly ten years off a newcomer's life. Men outnumbered women... roughly six to one in 1650, and by 1700 it was down to... three to one?

Marcus: [conversational] Three to two.

Maya: [confident tone] Three to two. Marriages often broken by death within seven years, roughly half the children gone before twenty. Box one, checked. Two: rivers not towns. Plantations strung along the James, the York, the Rappahannock, wharves for roads, no Boston. Box two, checked. Three: the planter class. The headright stacked the riverfront into a few hands, and Maryland ran the same playbook, a Catholic refuge that filled with Protestants. Box three, checked. Four: the legend and the loss. Smith's rescue, first told in 1624, read the author first, then the story. And the real ending: 1644, 1646, reservations. Box four, checked.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. On the long essay: this episode is your regional-comparison paragraph. Chesapeake against New England. Name the crop, the labor, and the map for each, and the comparison writes itself. Graders reward the student who explains the why, not just the what.

Maya: One. For most of the 1600s, the Chesapeake grew by immigration. Why?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Death outran birth — malaria, dysentery, and typhoid cut roughly ten years off a newcomer's life, and the migration was mostly young men, roughly six to one in 1650. High death plus few women means few families, so the population could only grow by fresh boats.

Maya: Two. The stimulus is a passage from Smith's Generall Historie, 1624, describing Pocahontas saving his life. A historian wants to use it as evidence of what happened in 1607. What's the problem?

[10-second pause]

Maya: It's Smith's own late telling, not a neutral record — the rescue appears in none of his earlier accounts; it shows up in 1624, roughly seventeen years later, when he's selling a book. Read the author before the story: historians still split on whether he invented it or misunderstood a ritual.

Maya: Three. Contrast the Chesapeake and New England in one move each: why did the Chesapeake scatter along rivers while New England clustered in towns?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The crop built the map — tobacco needed fresh soil and river frontage, and the headright stacked land onto the importers; New England's covenant towns centered on the meetinghouse. The land system made the settlement pattern: dispersed plantations here, clustered towns there.

Maya: One more, fast. Maryland, 1649: what law, and who was it really for?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The Act of Toleration: worship protected for Christians. Really for the Catholics, already outnumbered in their own refuge.

Maya: [confident tone] Check your four boxes.

Maya: [intrigued] Next time: the regions you just compared stop arguing with each other, and start arguing with London.

Marcus: [professional broadcast tone] The Chesapeake ate its people and fed its planters —

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] and the legend outlived them all.
