Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: Unit 1 in ten questions — one ocean making one world, on conquest's terms — ending with the Pueblo Revolt, 1680, knotted cords and twelve years of saying no. This time: four empires, one continent, four ways to want it. Four boxes on the sheet: Spain's extraction machine, France's fur empire, Holland's company colony, and England's settler play. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Twelve minutes, quiz-style. I drive, you retrieve. Catch me blanking on a box and I owe you fries.

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] Spain first. Souls and silver, right?

Maya: Crown-run souls and silver. Missions to convert, mines to pay for everything else. The crown took a royal fifth of the mine silver, the quinto, twenty percent off the top. And the labor machine: the encomienda, which handed a colonist a whole town's labor in exchange for protecting and Christianizing them. Protecting did a lot of heavy lifting in that sentence.

Jay: And when the towns emptied, they rebuilt the machine instead of changing the goal.

Maya: New paperwork, same hunger. And Spain loved a category. The casta labels: peninsulares at the top, Spaniards born in Spain. Then criollos, Spaniards born in the Americas. Below them, mestizos and mulatos. A full casta series ran to sixteen named mixtures underneath those, with indios and Africans holding the bottom rung. Your label followed you into court, into church, into every appointment.

Jay: A whole government of who your grandparents were.

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] Did the souls and the silver ever fight each other?

Maya: The crown tried to leash the encomenderos once. The New Laws of 1542 said the grants couldn't be inherited, they'd pass back to the crown. The encomenderos in Peru rebelled, killed the crown's own viceroy, and the crown backed down.

Jay: [sheepish] God and gold. So Spain colonized for God, right? The missions prove it.

Maya: [firm] Not quite. That's the trap answer. The missions were real, but the quinto ran on twenty percent silver. The silver always won. The mission towns held the borderlands, Florida and New Mexico, for the better part of a century. Until the Pueblos answered in 1680.

Maya: [confident tone] Box one: Spain extracted, checking that one.

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] France. What did they actually want?

Maya: Beaver. Europe's hat makers paid a fortune for beaver wool, felted into hats that were an expensive status symbol. The whole colony bent around one trade.

Jay: A trading post with a flag.

Maya: Champlain planted Quebec in 1608, one year after Jamestown, same idea flipped inside out. A trading post, not a colony of farms. That one date tells you the whole French plan.

Jay: [sheepish] So 1608 comes first, and it's the English one? Wait, no. Ugh, I always flip these.

Maya: [firm] Not quite. Flip both. Jamestown is 1607, English, one year earlier. Quebec is 1608, French, and it's the trading post. Dates with a job attached beat dates floating alone.

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] And the guys in the woods?

Maya: The coureurs de bois, runners of the woods. They learned the languages, married into the trading towns. The crown licensed the fur trade, and the woodsmen mostly traded right past the license.

Jay: Smugglers with canoes.

Maya: Smugglers in everything but name. Confession: I own a beaver-felt fedora. Vintage shop, twelve bucks. I've been wearing colonial currency on my head.

Jay: Your hat funded an empire.

Maya: Its ancestors did. Now the question you always ask: were the French the nice ones? Trading instead of conquering?

Jay: [sheepish] Kind of? Compared to the encomienda, trading looks gentle.

Maya: [firm] Not exactly. That's the essay trap. The convenient ones. The fur trade needed living partners, so France picked sides: guns and trade goods to the Wendat, who the French called the Huron, against their enemies the Iroquois. Partnership on the ledger, strategy on the map. Ask the Iroquois how nice it felt.

Jay: Nobody was nice. Everybody had interests.

Maya: [serious tone] Alliances of convenience. And the alliances turned into actual war. The Beaver Wars, 1640 to 1701: the Iroquois, armed with Dutch guns, went after the Huron to seize the fur flow. Whole mission villages destroyed in 1649, Jesuits killed. The fur trade didn't just need partners. It armed the rivalries.

Jay: Convenience, with muskets.

Maya: Now: why was New France so empty? France's colonies were vast on paper but thinly peopled, while the English seaboard kept filling in. Your turn. France's whole economy is pelts, trapped by Native partners. Does that colony want more settlers, or fewer? Think it through.

[10-second pause]

Maya: Fewer. Settlers clear land and drive off beaver. The machine needs woods and partners, not farms. And three reasons stack under that. The crown barred the Huguenots, French Protestants, so France's natural refugees sailed for English and Dutch ports instead. The crown ran the colony directly, no assembly to attract anyone. And the fur economy never needed farmers. Historians argue about which mattered most.

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] The Jesuits, though. Soul business or fur business?

Maya: Soul business. The missions in Florida and New Mexico, the Jesuits were all over those.

Jay: Hold on. The Jesuits in the Relations were French. Wendat villages. The woods.

Maya: [sheepish] And I just folded the wrong empire's missionaries in. The Spanish had their missions in Florida and New Mexico. The Jesuits writing the Relations are the French ones, living in Wendat villages for years, learning the language, writing it all down. Both empires wore black robes. I picked the wrong robes.

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] Stimulus check: they hand you a page from the Jesuit Relations, a Jesuit describing Wendat village life in detail. What's the point of the source?

Maya: [confident tone] The point isn't just the facts about the Wendat. It's that the book exists at all. The French lived close enough to write it. A trade empire needed allies, so its missionaries doubled as diplomats. Box two: fur empire, few settlers, Wendat allies. Box two, checked.

Jay: [sheepish] Holland. Patroons. That was the Dutch thing, right? The lords?

Maya: The Dutch thing. 1629: the company told its investors, ship fifty settlers across and we'll hand you a feudal estate on the Hudson, you as patroon, lord of the manor. Sixteen miles of riverfront. Feudal lords in the sixteen hundreds.

Jay: Backwards. Feudalism with better ships.

Maya: The one that actually worked was Rensselaerswijck, up around present-day Albany. Most of the other lords' ventures never got off the ground. But the town worked. New Amsterdam drew merchants from everywhere, a jumble of languages on the docks, even Sephardic Jews who'd fled Brazil. The company let in people other colonies shut out. Customers were customers.

Jay: [sheepish] Even Stuyvesant couldn't shut the doors? He tried, right?

Maya: He tried. English settlers in Flushing wrote the Remonstrance in 1657, asking Stuyvesant to lift his ban on Quaker worship. One of the earliest demands for religious tolerance in the colonies. The company town kept outgrowing its governor.

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] And then England just takes it. 1664?

Maya: 1664. An English fleet sailed into the harbor, Stuyvesant looked at his defenses and surrendered without a shot. The English renamed it New York, for the king's brother, the Duke of York. The Dutch even grabbed it back in 1673, then handed it over again the next year and kept the sugar colony of Suriname instead.

Jay: I know something here. Yankee. There's a theory it comes from a Dutch name, Jan Kees.

Maya: Theories go both ways, and nobody's fully sure. Dutch insulting the English, or the other way around first. The paper trail runs cold, but it's probably tied to the Dutch in New York. Noted: the student knows one the teacher didn't.

Jay: The Hudson, though. Dutch river?

Maya: Named for Henry Hudson — an Englishman sailing for the Dutch. Even the river had mixed loyalties.

Jay: [sheepish] So the patroon thing is the Dutch headright. Fifty acres a head?

Maya: [firm] Not exactly. Different machine, different river. The headright paid fifty acres per passage, worker by worker. The patroon deal was feudal: ship fifty settlers total, and the company hands you a lordship, sixteen miles of riverfront.

Maya: [confident tone] Exam tip for this box: if the SAQ hands you 1629 and fifty settlers, the answer's already in the question. Dutch patroons. Box three: company colony, patroon lords. Box three, checked.

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] England. Why did their model actually fill the map?

Maya: Families. The Chesapeake ships were packed with young men chasing tobacco money, but the model that stuck was households: farms, towns, kids. And joint-stock companies meant the crown risked nothing while the investors risked everything.

Jay: Where do the religious refugees fit? The Puritans weren't chasing tobacco.

Maya: New England. Same settler machine, different fuel. The Puritans were fleeing persecution, not chasing profits, but they still came in families, built towns, and pushed Native peoples off the land just the same.

Jay: The headright. Fifty acres a head.

Maya: Your turn. The headright gives fifty acres to whoever PAID your passage. Who does that reward: the worker, or the boss who bought the ticket? Think it through.

[10-second pause]

Maya: The boss. Bring ten workers, claim five hundred acres. The acres follow the money, not the muscle.

Jay: [sheepish] So on the essay, the servant gets the fifty acres. He did the sailing.

Maya: [firm] Common mix-up, and the classic trap. The fifty acres went to whoever paid the passage. The servant crossed; the boss collected. The labor would change, too: servants first, enslaved Africans later. More on that a few episodes from now. And notice who paid for the whole thing: not the king. London investors, in a joint-stock company chartered by James the First. The merchants' money, the merchants' risk. The English difference in one sentence: Spain's crown licensed and taxed the machine; England's investors bankrolled it.

Jay: And the Natives?

Maya: Pushed aside. The collision line for the long essay: Spain folded Native peoples into its labor machine, and England pushed them off the land. Two hungers, same continent. The move is collide, don't list.

Jay: [sheepish] So the English model wins? More people, more towns?

Maya: [confident tone] Wins at reproducing itself. The map filled and the towns stuck. Three centuries later, we speak English. But winning depends on whose ledger you're reading. Box four: families, companies, the headright. Box four, checked.

Maya: Four boxes almost down. But first, the landing.

Maya: Four boxes, let's land them. Spain: crown-run, souls and silver, the encomienda, the casta labels. France kept it thin: a fur empire with few settlers and Wendat allies. Holland was a company colony: patroons on the Hudson, fifty settlers for sixteen miles of riverfront. Sixteen. I almost said sixty. And England filled the map with company money and settler families on headright land, pushing Native peoples off as it went.

Jay: You almost said sixty.

Maya: I did. Sixteen stuck. Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it.

Maya: One. A 1657 petition from English settlers in Flushing, New Netherland, asks Stuyvesant to let Quakers worship. What's the point of this source?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The Dutch ran a company colony, not a godly one — English settlers petitioning a Dutch governor for somebody else's worship, in a town already full of other languages and peoples. The logic is commercial: a company needs customers more than it needs conformity. Diversity served the trade.

Maya: Two. Why did New France stay thin while the English colonies filled up?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The French model discouraged settlement. Huguenots barred, crown control with no assembly, a fur economy that needed no farmers. Nothing in the machine rewarded bringing families.

Maya: Three. The long essay asks you to compare Spanish and English colonization. The move?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Collide, don't list — Spain's encomienda folded Native peoples into its labor machine, and England's headright rewarded importing workers onto Native land. Extraction needs labor, settlement needs land: the collision.

Maya: One more, fast. A 1629 company charter offers feudal estates for shipping fifty settlers. Which empire, and what's the system called?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The Dutch. The patroon system. The comparison essay is yours now. Four boxes, one collision. Check your boxes. Next time: a swamp in Virginia. One hundred five land in 1607, five hundred become sixty, and one weed changes everything.

Jay: [intrigued] Four empires, four appetites —

Maya: [intrigued] one continent, and the map still shows every bite.
