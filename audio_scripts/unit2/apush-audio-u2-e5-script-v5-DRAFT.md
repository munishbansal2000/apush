# Pronunciation: MEHR-kun-tih-lizm (mercantilism), BOOL-yun (bullion), eh-NOO-muh-ray-ted (enumerated), SAL-yoo-teh-ree (salutary), WAHL-pohl (Walpole), moh-LAS-iz (molasses), vice ad-MIR-al-tee (vice-admiralty)

Maya: Last time: Penn's Holy Experiment, the Pennsylvania Dutch, the limits of tolerance. This time: the money underneath it all. Britain ran its empire on a theory that said the world's wealth was a fixed pile. Grab the pile, fence off the trade. Three boxes: mercantilism, the Navigation Acts, and salutary neglect. Circle the ones you couldn't explain. Twelve minutes. One note first: the Adam Smith line later is his actual words. Walpole's sleeping-dogs line comes to us secondhand.

Marcus: Mercantilism said a nation's power was the pile of gold and silver stacked in its vaults. Bullion, the fancy word for it. And the world's pile was fixed, so one country's gain was another country's loss. The winning move: sell more than you buy. A favorable balance of trade.

Maya: I had a piggy bank shaped like a cannon. Counted the coins every Sunday and refused to spend a single one, because the pile WAS the wealth. Ten-year-old mercantilist.

Marcus: Close enough to the whole theory. Now give it an empire. The colonies got two jobs: ship raw materials to England cheap, and buy England's finished goods dear. And never, ever build the factory yourself.

Maya: Exam tip: when a prompt says "evaluate the extent," build two columns. The graders want the weighing, not the verdict.

Marcus: The deal itself splits historians. Some say it was a decent bargain: protected markets, steady buyers, the Royal Navy guarding the sea lanes for free. Others say a cage: higher prices on European goods, trade boxed onto England's terms, profits draining toward London.

Maya: The cage side has the price data, the good-deal side has the Navy. The exam makes us hold both.

Marcus: Common mistake for box one: don't write that mercantilists were gold hunters. They didn't care who dug it up, only who sat on the pile.

Maya: Mercantilism: wealth is the pile. The pie is fixed. The colonies are the farm and the customer. Checking that one.

Maya: Your turn. You're running England's treasury. Your colonies are getting rich, but they're buying French cloth and Dutch tools, and London's merchants are fuming. What rule do you write?

[10-second pause]

Maya: You'd write the rule that keeps it all in the family: the colonies buy from England, sell to England, and sail on English ships. The rulebook has a name. The Navigation Acts.

Marcus: It started in 1651, aimed at the Dutch: trade with England rides on English ships. 1660, the monarchy restored: the enumerated list. Tobacco leads it — those goods sail to England only, on English ships with mostly English crews. Then 1663, the Staple Act: anything the colonies buy from Europe passes through an English port first.

Maya: So a Virginia planter can't sell his tobacco to the Dutch, even if the Dutch pay more. And French cloth takes the long way through London.

Marcus: And London takes its cut at every stop. The middleman almost always turned out to be London. The list kept growing. Rice joined it. Then, in the early 1700s, naval stores: tar, pitch, turpentine, masts, everything a navy needed. And Parliament fenced off the factories as well: the Wool Act in 1699, the Hat Act in 1732, the Iron Act in 1750. No colonial competition for British manufacturers.

Maya: Exam tip: when a stimulus lists tobacco, rice, and naval stores, the answer is the enumerated list: England only. The manufacturing acts are the other column: what the colonies were forbidden to make.

Marcus: The classic flip to avoid: 1660 is what the colonies could SELL, and on whose ships. 1663 is where they had to BUY from. Don't reverse them.

Maya: Sell and sail in 1660, buy through London in 1663.

Marcus: Then 1733: the Molasses Act. New England's rum distilleries were buying cheap French molasses, so London slapped a steep duty on foreign molasses. Buy British instead.

Maya: Molasses. I only know that word from the 1919 Boston molasses flood. The tank burst, the wave moved at thirty-five miles an hour, twenty-one people died. Sorry, weird flex, but it's the first thing I picture.

Marcus: Same city, same trade. And here's the 1733 version: New England simply didn't pay. Bribed officials, forged paperwork, midnight landings. The duty on the books, the molasses through the back door.

Maya: One ship: English goods to Africa, enslaved people to the Caribbean, sugar back to England. Three legs, neat arrows. Tell me that's really how it worked.

Marcus: The diagram simplifies the voyages. Plenty of ships ran two legs and never closed the triangle. New England rum sailed straight to the Caribbean. Most deals were bilateral, not grand circuits. The triangle names a pattern, not the average voyage.

Maya: But the pattern still runs on people. New England's distilleries need Caribbean sugar. The sugar plantations need enslaved labor. The demand for enslaved workers keeps climbing whether or not any single ship closes the loop.

Marcus: The arrows lied. The chain didn't. And London tightened the screws once: in 1696 Parliament created a Board of Trade and authorized vice-admiralty courts to back the customs men. Judges instead of juries.

Maya: Exam tip: the missing jury is the point. A Boston jury of merchants was never going to convict a smuggling neighbor. No jury, so no convictions.

Maya: The Navigation Acts: 1651, 1660, 1663. Enumerated goods to England only, on English ships. European goods through London first. And underneath, the whole coast dodging. The question is who was watching.

Maya: Your turn. It's 1721, and Robert Walpole runs the Treasury. The customs men beg for more staff. But the trade taxes are filling London's coffers, and a crackdown might kill the goose. What do you do?

[10-second pause]

Maya: You look the other way. For the next twenty years.

Marcus: Box three: salutary neglect. Walpole's supposed line on the colonies was "let sleeping dogs lie."

Maya: Supposed line. You said secondhand.

Marcus: The exact wording comes to us secondhand — nobody's fully sure he said those words. But the neglect was real: customs offices left understaffed, governors looking the other way. The laws stayed on the books while nobody enforced them, and colonial merchants got rich in the gaps.

Maya: "Put your John Hancock here." The idiom's about his signature, but his money came first, right?

Marcus: The fortune had a lot to do with dodging the customs house. In 1768 the customs men seized his ship, the Liberty, and accused him of smuggling. Highly publicized trial — then the charges were dropped for lack of evidence. Accused, never convicted. Whether he was guilty, historians still argue.

Maya: So the empire's most famous signature belonged to a smuggler. Maybe.

Marcus: The name for all of this arrived late. "Salutary neglect" was coined by Edmund Burke in 1775, in a speech to Parliament, looking back at the results: British commerce with the colonies had grown something like twelvefold since 1700. The paradox: the system was built to keep the colonies subordinate, suppliers and customers, never rivals. Left unenforced, they got rich inside it.

Maya: Exam tip: the word "salutary" is the giveaway — it means beneficial. The neglect wasn't laziness, it was policy, and it trained a generation of colonists to govern themselves. The exam wants that line.

Marcus: And the favorite trap: Hancock walked. The charges were dropped. Accused, never convicted.

Maya: Salutary neglect, then: Walpole's maybe-words, the laws on the books while nobody enforces them, and merchants getting rich in the gaps.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: mercantilism — wealth is the pile, the pie is fixed, the colonies ship raw and buy finished, and the argument runs good-deal versus cage. Two: the Navigation Acts — 1651, 1660, 1663. The enumerated list: tobacco, rice, and... hats?

Marcus: Hats were the manufacturing acts. Wool, Hat, Iron. The enumerated goods were the cargo: tobacco, rice, naval stores. Cargo to England, factories fenced off.

Maya: Right. Cargo sailed to England; the factories got fenced off. And the triangle: neat diagram, messier reality. Three: salutary neglect — the maybe-quoted sleeping dogs, Burke naming it in 1775, Hancock's Liberty seized and the charges dropped. The rules were real, the obedience optional. All three landed.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it.

Maya: One. The stimulus is Adam Smith, 1776, and these are his actual words: "Nothing, however, can be more absurd than this whole doctrine of the balance of trade." Which theory is he attacking, and why does the year matter?

[15-second pause]

Maya: He's attacking mercantilism, and the date is the argument. "The whole doctrine of the balance of trade" is mercantilism's beating heart. The economic case against the empire arrives in the same year as the political one: the demolition and the Declaration share a birthday.

Maya: Two. A Boston customs ledger, 1740: a merchant lands French molasses with the duty unpaid. What does one entry like that tell an AP reader about the Navigation Acts?

[15-second pause]

Maya: Strict on paper, porous on the water: the Molasses Act's steep duty sat unpaid, the same way Hancock's charges were dropped. The system's real shape came from enforcement, not statutes. Neglect was the operating system.

Maya: Three. England built an empire on mercantilism, then spent decades barely enforcing it. Paradox, or the whole point?

[20-second pause]

Maya: The paradox IS the point: protected markets and the Navy's guarded lanes made the colonies rich, while unenforced Acts let merchants keep the profits. Neglect turned a cage into something like a partnership, which is why the crackdown after 1763 would feel like a betrayal.

Maya: One more, fast: the name "salutary neglect": who coined it, and when?

[5-second pause]

Maya: Edmund Burke, 1775. The name arrived decades after the policy.

Maya: On the long essay: your "evaluate the extent" paragraph. Protected markets on one side, the cage on the other. Weigh both, or you're only telling half the story.

Maya: Check your three boxes. Next time: the Great Awakening. A young English preacher named Whitefield, an open field, and crowds that came to weep.

Marcus: London wrote the rules —

Maya: and America thrived on the exceptions.
