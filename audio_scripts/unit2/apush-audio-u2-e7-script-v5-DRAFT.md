# Pronunciation: ZENG-er (Zenger), KOZ-bee (Cosby), an-gliss-ih-ZAY-shun (Anglicization), deh-LAN-see (De Lancey), seh-DIH-shus (seditious), LEW-is MOR-is (Lewis Morris)

Maya: Last time: Whitefield's open fields, Edwards's spider sermon, and the Old Lights fighting the New Lights over who owned religion. This time: the quieter trick: how the colonies learned to make the king's own governor come begging for his paycheck. Three boxes: the power of the purse, the Zenger trial, and Anglicization. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Twelve minutes.

Marcus: Start with the strangest power flip in colonial America. The royal governor steps off the boat with the king's commission, a wig, and his orders. And the assembly waiting in Williamsburg holds the one thing his commission doesn't include.

Maya: Money.

Marcus: Money. They vote the taxes. They vote the budget. And in a lot of colonies, they vote the governor's own salary. So the king's representative goes hat in hand to the planters for his paycheck.

Maya: My school's student council once held the bake-sale money hostage until the principal gave us spirit week. Same energy, bigger wigs.

Marcus: Same logic, exactly. And London mostly let it happen. Early 1700s, Walpole runs the show in London, the ocean's wide, England has other wars. Nobody votes for neglect. It just settles in. The Navigation Acts stay on the books; the customs men stay home. Decade after decade, the assemblies practice the same move: we hold the purse, so we hold the power.

Maya: Give me one fight where the purse actually won.

Marcus: Massachusetts, 1720s. Governor Shute demands a permanent salary, voted once and done. The House says no. They'll vote his pay year by year, thanks. A few years later Governor Burnet tries again. In 1728 the House hands him seventeen hundred pounds and still refuses the fixed salary. Every annual vote was a reminder of who the governor worked for.

Maya: And the shape of the whole thing: was it just random town meetings?

Marcus: No, it was English on purpose. Every colony ran a little parliament of its own: an elected lower house, an appointed council, the royal governor on top. That's why colonists felt the English constitution lived in America too. They were running a version of it themselves.

Maya: So when London finally meddled, it wasn't just taxes. It was someone messing with their constitution.

Marcus: And London could still bite. The crown reached back in when it wanted men: press gangs grabbing sailors off the docks to crew warships. In 1747 Boston rioted over it for three days. A British admiral named Knowles sent the gangs through the harbor for sailors, and they took plenty of men who weren't sailors. Hundreds of Bostonians rampaged: the biggest impressment riot colonial North America ever saw.

Maya: So they're loyal British subjects who'll riot for three days when the navy grabs their neighbors.

Marcus: Loyal, not cuddly.

Maya: Your turn. It's 1728. You're sitting in the Massachusetts House. The governor wants his salary voted once, permanently. He's got the king's backing, and he treated annual votes as beneath the crown's dignity. What do you do?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Keep voting it year by year. If he doesn't need your money, he doesn't need you. That paycheck is the leash. Don't hand it over.

Marcus: Exactly. The exam-shaped version: when a question asks why colonists believed they deserved a say over taxes, trace the chain backward. They'd spent fifty years voting the governor's salary. Of course they thought money needed their consent.

Maya: And the trap: don't write that the assemblies ran everything. The king still appointed the governor and the council, and the governor commissioned the judges. The assembly's power was leverage inside the empire, not a flag of its own.

Maya: One box down. Checking that one.

Marcus: Box two is a courtroom. New York, 1735. A German immigrant printer named John Peter Zenger runs a paper, the New-York Weekly Journal, and he spends about a year roasting the royal governor, William Cosby, in print.

Maya: Roasting. In 1734.

Marcus: Relentless. Cosby had dismissed a judge, Lewis Morris, after the judge ruled against him, over a salary dispute. Remember the purse fights. Zenger's paper printed the ugly story. So Cosby does what cornered governors do: he has Zenger arrested for seditious libel. November 1734. Zenger sits in a jail cell for the better part of a year. His wife Anna keeps the paper printing without him.

Maya: Hold on. Hamilton? Like the musical?

Marcus: Same last name, wrong decade. Andrew Hamilton: Philadelphia's most celebrated lawyer. And his argument is the dangerous one. If what Zenger printed was true, it's not libel. Truth is the defense.

Maya: My school's paper has to get every issue approved by the principal first. My journalism teacher says one Supreme Court case from the eighties lets her kill any story she wants.

Marcus: And Zenger's jury had the opposite problem. Nothing was approved in advance; the punishment came after. The judge says no, and he's Cosby's own chief justice, James De Lancey, the man who'd already disbarred Zenger's first lawyers. Under the law, the words are the crime. Truth doesn't matter. Under the old English rule, a true attack was actually worse than a false one, because truth made the scandal bigger.

Maya: So the judge tells the jury the law is the law. What did the jury do?

Marcus: Your turn. You're on that jury. The judge just told you the words are the crime and truth doesn't count. Hamilton's asked you to acquit anyway, because the words were true. What does your verdict actually decide?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Whether you follow the judge's law or decide justice yourselves. The letter of the law, or the liberty behind it.

Marcus: They chose liberty. Acquitted. And I want to be straight with you about this part: the judge never let Hamilton prove the truth in court. And no new law was written that day. On the books, truth was still no defense. What changed was the idea. A colonial jury had decided a printer could criticize a governor and live. Forty years before independence, the colonies were rehearsing the Revolution's favorite argument.

Maya: So the warning for box two: don't write that the trial made truth a legal defense. The law stayed ugly. The jury just refused it that day.

Marcus: Exam tip, same box: when a stimulus hands you a libel prosecution, don't ask what the printer wrote. Ask who decides. Jury or judge. That question echoes for a century.

Marcus: Box three is the paradox. The big word is Anglicization. Gloss it first: colonists copying English life, on purpose.

Maya: Wait. Anglicization. So the colonies were becoming... American? Less British?

Marcus: Opposite. More British. By the 1700s, colonists read English papers, drink English tea, build English-style houses, buy English clocks and wigs and china. They didn't want to look like provincial bumpkins on the edge of the civilized world.

Maya: Trying really hard to be British.

Marcus: And the harder they tried, the sharper the insult got. The more British they felt, the less they'd accept being treated as second-class British. A Virginia planter reads the same papers as a London merchant. Then he gets told his assembly's laws can be vetoed by men who've never seen Virginia.

Maya: That sounds like having it both ways. Loyal when it helps, oppressed when it helps?

Marcus: It was both ways. The loyalty was real, and so was the grievance. And here's the line the colonists drew: we're Englishmen. Not "leave us alone because we're different." Englishmen get assemblies, trials, and presses, and Englishmen don't get pushed around by a Parliament they never elected. Their Britishness was the weapon.

Maya: So the historians' fight: were the colonies already independent by the 1760s?

Marcus: Still going. Some say yes: by mid-century the colonies governed themselves in everything but name. Others say that's hindsight talking, because the colonists still called themselves loyal British subjects and petitioned the king like they meant it. I don't think it's settled.

Maya: Exam tip for box three: Anglicization questions test the paradox. Ask which direction the identity arrow points. If a source shows colonists buying English goods and quoting English law, the answer is more British, not more American.

Maya: And the common mistake: don't file Anglicization under "colonies becoming American." The identity arrow points at London, not away from it.

Marcus: There's the bridge to 1763. A century of voting their own budgets, printing their own papers, thinking of themselves as full Englishmen. Then the war ends and London wants its empire back. New taxes and new rules, with redcoats staying put. It doesn't land like policy. It lands like a promise broken.

Maya: Before the test, let me make sure all three are actually landed.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: the power of the purse. Assemblies voted the taxes and the budget, and in a lot of colonies the governor's salary. Massachusetts fought Shute and Burnet over it in the 1720s instead of granting a permanent salary. The crown could press your sailors, but it couldn't pass a budget without the people it taxed.

Marcus: Leverage, not sovereignty. The right was never written down. It was assumed.

Maya: Two: the Zenger trial, 1735. Governor Cosby jails printer John Peter Zenger for criticizing him. Hamilton gets truth admitted as the defense, and the jury throws the case out.

Marcus: Careful. Hamilton never got to prove it. The judge barred the truth evidence. The jury acquitted anyway, choosing liberty over the law as written. That was the whole point.

Maya: Right. The jury says no to power, and the law on the books stays ugly. Three: Anglicization. The more British the colonists felt, the harder they insisted on being treated as full British subjects, assemblies and presses and all.

Marcus: And the debate rides with it: basically self-governing by the 1760s, or loyal subjects all along. Both halves have evidence.

Maya: All three landed.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. And on the long essay: this whole episode is your "evaluate the extent" paragraph. How far did colonial self-government go? Purse power and Zenger juries on one side, royal governors and vetoed laws on the other. The graders want the weighing.

Maya: One. The stimulus is the Massachusetts House, September 1728. The House grants Governor Burnet seventeen hundred pounds, but refuses the fixed, stated salary he demanded. What's the assembly's move, and why does it matter?

[15-second pause]

Maya: The assembly buys obedience one year at a time. They granted the seventeen hundred pounds but no permanent salary, keeping the annual vote. Because the purse is the power — a governor who comes back for his pay each year can't ignore the people who vote it.

Maya: Two. The stimulus is 1735, and these are Hamilton's actual words, lightly trimmed: "it is not the cause of one poor printer, nor of New-York alone, which you are now trying... It is the best cause. It is the cause of liberty." What's the bigger claim the lawyer is selling?

[20-second pause]

Maya: Press freedom isn't about one printer. "Not one poor printer, nor of New-York alone." He scaled it to every colony. Because a jury that defends a printer's right to criticize a governor defends every colonist's rights. The Revolution's pamphlet wars, forty years early.

Maya: Three. Anglicization made colonists feel more British in the 1700s, and more ready to revolt. Can both be true?

[15-second pause]

Maya: Yes, that's the paradox. They drank the tea and read the English papers, then demanded the rights of Englishmen: assemblies and trials. Because the more British they felt, the more London's post-1763 crackdown felt like a betrayal of the deal. Identity loaded the spring.

Maya: Check your three boxes.

Maya: Next time: the French and Indian War. A twenty-two-year-old named Washington fires a shot in the Ohio country in 1754 that starts a world war.

Marcus: A century of self-rule, learned while London looked away —

Maya: then London looked back.
