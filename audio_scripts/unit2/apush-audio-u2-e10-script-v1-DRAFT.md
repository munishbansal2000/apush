# Pronunciation: POW-huh-tan (Powhatan), poh-kah-HON-tuhs (Pocahontas), ROHLF (Rolfe), BUR-juh-siz (Burgesses), oh-peh-chahn-kah-NOH (Opechancanough), deh-lah-WAHR (De La Warr), al-GON-kwihn (Algonquian)

Marcus: Winter, sixteen oh nine. Inside a wooden fort on a Virginia river, sixty people are still alive. Five hundred had crowded in that fall. The rest are dead: hunger, sickness, cold. The living have eaten the horses. Then the dogs. Then the rats, and the leather off their own shoes. And then, the accounts say it plainly, they dug up the dead.

Marcus: [quiet] Centuries later, archaeologists found a girl in that fort's refuse pit. Fourteen years old. They call her Jane. Her skull carries the marks of a cleaver, cut after death by hands that were trying to survive. Nobody murdered her. Hunger did that. That was the true founding of English America.

Marcus: So how did a colony that ate its own dead become the most populous English colony in America? My thesis, in three parts: Jamestown didn't survive because the plan was good. It survived on luck and a leaf, and the way it survived bent everything that came after. Staple crop, imported labor, scattered plantations: that's the Chesapeake template.

Marcus: Part one: it nearly died. And the reasons were choices, not just bad luck.

Marcus: Spring, sixteen oh seven. About a hundred and five men and boys stepped onto a marshy peninsula in the James River. They'd been sent by the Virginia Company, a joint-stock venture, meaning London investors pooled money chasing profits. The orders: find gold, find a river route to the Pacific, and pick a site you can defend.

Marcus: Defensible, it was. Livable, it wasn't. The peninsula sat in brackish water, drinkable half the year and poison the other half. Mosquitoes bred in the marsh. Malaria, dysentery, and typhoid moved in like they'd been invited. Within months, more than half the men were dead.

Marcus: Many of these men were gentlemen. They'd come to get rich, not to farm. They spent their first summer digging for fool's gold, iron pyrite, while the corn went unplanted. So were they just lazy? That's the easy story. The harder truth is the land itself was killing them, and the worst drought in nearly eight centuries had settled over the region. The tree rings remember it: sixteen oh six to sixteen twelve were the driest years in seven hundred and seventy.

Marcus: What kept them alive at first was trade, and one man's discipline. The local Powhatan Confederacy, some thirty Algonquian-speaking towns under a chief the English called Powhatan, sold them corn. And Captain John Smith, running the colony by sixteen oh eight, laid down the law: "he that will not work, shall not eat." Work or starve. It worked, for a while.

Marcus: Then Smith left. A gunpowder explosion tore his leg in the fall of sixteen oh nine, and he sailed home to England. He never came back. Without him the trade collapsed. The Powhatan, tired of English demands and English raids, stopped selling corn and ringed the fort instead. That winter was the starving time. Five hundred, down to sixty. The survivors gave up and sailed for home, but the new governor, Lord De La Warr, met them coming the other way with fresh supplies and marched everyone back.

Marcus: Part two: the leaf that remade everything.

Marcus: The colony had to answer one question: what could Virginia sell? Not gold, there wasn't any. The answer came from John Rolfe, a planter who started experimenting in sixteen twelve with a sweet-scented tobacco strain from the West Indies, crossed with Virginia's harsh native leaf. The first shipments reached England in sixteen seventeen. Europe was hooked.

Marcus: Tobacco did what gold never could. It was addictive and fashionable, and it grew beautifully in Virginia soil. By the turn of the century, the Chesapeake was shipping tens of millions of pounds of it a year. A weed saved the colony.

Marcus: But a weed also reorganized it. Governor Dale had already loosened the company's grip in sixteen fourteen, giving each man three acres to work for himself, and suddenly men who'd waited for rations had reason to plant. Then, in sixteen eighteen, the company built the great machine for turning people into land: the headright system. Pay for someone's passage across the Atlantic, and you got fifty acres.

Marcus: Now ask who won. If you had money, you could import servant after servant and stack up thousands of acres. If you were the servant, your crossing bought your master fifty acres, not you. The headright didn't just attract settlers. It manufactured a planter class, and it put a price on importing human beings. That price would keep rising.

Marcus: That same year the company gave them something else: in sixteen nineteen, twenty-two elected burgesses, the word burgess an old term for citizen-representative, met in Jamestown's church and formed the first elected assembly in English America. The House of Burgesses. While men were still dying of the summer fevers, they were voting. Self-government and the plantation machine grew up side by side.

Marcus: And sixteen nineteen brought something else into that same harbor: a Dutch ship sold some twenty Africans to Virginia planters. Were they servants or slaves? Historians still argue about their exact legal status. Hold that question; the next episode turns on it.

Marcus: Lord Baltimore's Maryland, founded sixteen thirty-four as a Catholic refuge, ran the same playbook: tobacco, plantations, imported labor. The Chesapeake wasn't one colony's accident. It was a system.

Marcus: Part three: the society the template built. Or warped.

Marcus: First, it scattered people. Tobacco plantations needed river frontage, the rivers were the roads, so settlement stretched out in long, thin ribbons along the James, the York, the Rappahannock. No clustered towns, no meetinghouse at the center, just big houses far apart.

Marcus: Second, it killed people. The fevers never left. Malaria, dysentery, typhoid cut ten years off a newcomer's life expectancy. Half the children born in early Virginia didn't live to twenty. In sixteen fifty, men outnumbered women six to one. The ratio narrowed later, but for generations, families were fragile things: marriages broken by death within a few years, children growing up without two parents, grandparents a rumor.

Marcus: Third, it hungered. The appetite for labor never filled: servants finished their terms and wanted land, the land ran to the planters, the planters wanted more hands. The headright itself fed the cycle: fifty acres for every servant landed, so importing workers and grabbing land were the same business. That hunger drives the next fifty years. Remember it.

Marcus: Now set that beside New England, town-centered, family-based, church-governed, lower death rates, and you have the classic comparison. Same empire, same century, two different societies, because the land and the crop and the labor system were different. That contrast is the comparison the exam keeps circling back to.

Marcus: The climax came in sixteen twenty-two. The peace had held while Pocahontas lived: she'd married Rolfe in sixteen fourteen, sailed to England, and died there in sixteen seventeen. Her father Powhatan died the next year. His successor Opechancanough had watched the tobacco fields swallow his people's land. On March twenty-second, sixteen twenty-two, his warriors struck plantation after plantation in a coordinated dawn attack. Three hundred forty-seven colonists died, a quarter of Virginia.

Marcus: [solemn] Jamestown itself held, but the Virginia Company didn't. In sixteen twenty-four King James revoked the company's charter. Virginia became a royal colony, the king's men in charge now, not the company's. The war dragged on, poisoned wine at a false parley and burned cornfields, until another uprising in sixteen forty-four broke Powhatan power for good.

Marcus: But the legend is where the history gets interesting. John Smith: hero or self-promoter? The famous scene, captured in December of sixteen oh seven, about to be clubbed to death, saved when the chief's young daughter throws herself across his body, comes from Smith's own book, his Generall Historie, published in sixteen twenty-four. Sixteen years after it supposedly happened. By then Pocahontas was famous and dead, and Smith was selling books.

Marcus: So did it happen? Historians split. Some say Smith invented it outright. Others say he walked through a ritual he didn't understand: Powhatan symbolically killing him and raising him again as a subordinate ally, with the chief's daughter playing her assigned part. Either way, the rescue tells you more about Smith than about Pocahontas. Read his book the way you'd read a man writing his own legend, thrilling and unreliable.

Marcus: Next time: the hunger this episode built gets answered. First with servants, four to seven years of labor for an ocean crossing. Then with chains. Sixteen seventy-six, and a rebellion that terrified the planter class into inventing something worse.

Marcus: [pause] The one thing to carry forward: staple crop, imported labor, scattered settlement. Tobacco made Virginia rich. The headright made a few men rich. And the machine they built together, the Chesapeake template, would spend the next century demanding more land and more hands, until the answer it found was slavery.
