# Pronunciation: HWIT-field (Whitefield), ED-wards (Edwards), EN-field (Enfield), HOON-toh (Junto), AWL-muh-nak (Almanack), DEE-iz-um (deism), north-HAMP-tun (Northampton)

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: mercantilism, the Navigation Acts, and salutary neglect. London's money theory, and the loopholes the colonies grew rich in. As promised: a young English preacher named Whitefield, an open field, and crowds that came to weep. Four boxes this time: Whitefield, Edwards, Franklin's counterweight, and the splits the revival left behind. Circle the ones you couldn't explain. Twelve minutes, starting now.

Marcus: Before the fire, the tinder. By the 1730s, plenty of colonial churches had gone cold. Formal, established, ministers reading their sermons and sitting down. Membership was slipping, and the zeal with it.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So what do you do when the pews start emptying?

Marcus: New England tried a compromise back in 1662. The Half-Way Covenant: if you couldn't prove you'd been converted, you could still be baptized and join as a partial member. You got the pews, but voting and communion stayed off-limits.

Maya: Full pews, thinner commitment.

Marcus: That was the trade. And a generation was hungry for religion that did something to them. Then the spark: preachers traveling town to town, preaching outdoors, because no building could hold the crowds.

Maya: [warm tone] I went to an outdoor concert in Phoenix one July. Twenty thousand people, no shade, everyone melting by the second song. Standing through a two-hour sermon in that? Respect.

Marcus: In 1739, a young Englishman in his mid-twenties named George Whitefield crossed the Atlantic and started preaching in open fields. Crowds in the thousands, and by the newspapers' counts, twenty-five thousand in a field.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] No speakers, twenty-five thousand people. How did the back rows hear him?

Marcus: His voice just carried. Benjamin Franklin once stood at the back of a Philadelphia crowd and walked backward down Market Street until the voice blurred. Then he did the math: a semicircle of listeners at that distance, two square feet each, and he computed Whitefield could be heard by more than thirty thousand.

Maya: Ben Franklin did a sound check. In the 1740s.

Marcus: He did. Franklin's press and Whitefield's fame fed each other. The sermons sold newspapers; the newspapers spread the name. Whitefield's biographer Thomas Kidd calls him America's first celebrity.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Was he really the first? Nobody was famous before him?

Marcus: The case for "first" is the paper trail: the fame crossed the ocean on newsprint. The newspapers carried him the way the ships carried everything else.

Maya: [confident tone] Exam tip: when a stimulus describes open-air crowds and weeping, name the method behind it. Emotional preaching against the old establishment.

Marcus: And the funny true detail: he went to a sermon meaning to give nothing. By the end he'd emptied his whole pocket into the collection dish, gold and all.

Maya: [dry] The skeptic never converted — but he did empty his whole pocket.

Maya: Whitefield's the spark, then. The revival starts the day he lands in 1739.

Marcus: [firm] Common mix-up. Don't write that Whitefield started the revival. The fire was already smoldering in the 1730s. He poured the gasoline.

Maya: Your turn. You're a church elder in 1662. Membership is falling because the conversion test is too hard. Your deacons want the halfway rule. What are you risking?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Full pews, thinner faith. Checking that one. Whitefield, box one.

Marcus: Now the other preacher. And he's nothing like Whitefield.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So Edwards is the loud one, the weeper, acting out his sermons?

Marcus: [firm] Backwards. That's Whitefield: the weeping, the acting, the crowds. Edwards barely raised his voice. His weapon was the argument.

Maya: The easy flip to avoid. Edwards argued, Whitefield acted.

Marcus: Jonathan Edwards, a Massachusetts theologian, deep and careful. On July 8, 1741, in Enfield, Connecticut, he preached what's often called the most famous sermon in American history: "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God."

Maya: "The God that holds you over the pit of hell, much as one holds a spider, or some loathsome insect, over the fire."

Maya: He's screaming that, right? Shaking the pulpit, veins out?

Marcus: [firm] Not quite. Don't picture Edwards shouting. Those are his actual words, and the famous account has him reading it nearly flat, barely looking up, while the congregation came apart around him. That stillness is what made it terrifying.

Maya: That's scarier, honestly. The calm ones are always scarier.

Marcus: They used different weapons to make the same break with the old churches. Whitefield took religion to the fields; Edwards argued you into terror. Both cut out the middleman and put the conversion on the individual.

Maya: Someone on TikTok does the Enfield sermon as ASMR. The flat voice, the screaming congregation. Two million likes.

Marcus: [deadpan] Of course that's a thing. But the delivery is the point: feeling was the message. And the message many hearers took was that anyone could be saved, a choice away from the old ideas of predestination.

Maya: [confident tone] Exam tip: if the test hands you the spider line, the question is about style. What matters is the delivery, terror aimed at the formal churches. The theology is secondary.

Marcus: [serious tone] And the revival had casualties. Edwards tightened the rules on who could take communion, and in 1750 his own congregation at Northampton voted him out. The most famous theologian in the colonies, dismissed by his own church.

Maya: Edwards was the opposite of a showman — all argument, no theater — and his own congregation fired him anyway.

Marcus: Franklin was watching all of this, and he offered a different answer entirely. Box three: the counterweight.

Maya: [surprised] "Early to bed, early to rise" guy. I didn't know that was a whole worldview.

Marcus: It was. From 1732, for twenty-five years, Franklin published Poor Richard's Almanack: weather, household tips, and aphorisms about work and thrift. "A penny saved is a penny earned." The jokes carried the Enlightenment: reason over revelation, the self-made man as a project.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And the Junto?

Marcus: In 1727 he started the Junto, a club of tradesmen who met to argue morals and politics and pooled their books until they'd built a lending library — the Enlightenment as a social club. Then the electricity work: the lightning rod, and a reputation that reached across the Atlantic.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] The kite. Did he actually fly it in a thunderstorm?

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] The famous story is shakier than the textbooks let on. Whether Franklin himself ever stood in a storm holding that string, nobody's fully sure. But the experiments were real, and they made him famous across the Atlantic before he was a revolutionary.

Maya: [playful] So the Awakening crowds are out in the fields weeping, and the Junto guys are indoors going, "Actually, let's reason about this."

Marcus: [confident tone] Feeling against reason — that's the split in one line. And the key term is deism: the idea that God wound up the universe like a clock and stepped back. No miracles in the daily weather. If you want to know how the world works, use your reason.

Maya: So Franklin's an Awakening guy too. His press carried the revival, after all.

Marcus: [firm] Common mistake. Don't file Franklin under the Awakening. His press carried the revival, but his mind was on the Enlightenment.

Marcus: [confident tone] Exam tip: Franklin's name on a question points to the Enlightenment. If the revival is sitting in the answer choices, that's the wrong column.

Maya: While the crowds were weeping in the fields, Franklin was indoors reasoning.

Marcus: Then the aftershock. The revival's fans became the New Lights. The old guard, the Old Lights.

Maya: Your turn. Your church just split over the revival. Half the members walk out to start their own congregation. They've never run anything before. What do they have to learn, fast?

[10-second pause]

Maya: How to organize. Raise money, pick leaders, argue in public, defy the established church and survive it.

Marcus: That organizing is the aftershock. Ordinary colonists practiced standing up to authority, and they watched it work. New denominations grew out of the fire: Baptists, Methodists. The revival even founded colleges. Princeton's 1746 charter is usually credited to New Light Presbyterians.

Maya: [confident tone] Exam tip: New Light versus Old Light questions hinge on authority. Who gets to decide what counts as real religion?

Marcus: And here's the debate historians still have. Start with the big claim: the Awakening was the first thing all the colonies ever experienced together. The same sermons, the same crowds, from New England down to Georgia.

Maya: And the pushback?

Marcus: The pushback: it's overstated. The revivals never touched half the colonies, and in some places the fire burned out fast. So the big question: did the Awakening help cause the Revolution?

Maya: Give me both sides.

Marcus: The case for: it trained colonists to defy authority, and gave them a shared experience. A rehearsal, the argument goes, for 1776. The case against: the fires died down decades before the war, plenty of patriot leaders were never awakened, and both movements may just come from the same restless individualism. I don't think that's settled.

Maya: So the answer is "argue both sides." Very AP of them.

Marcus: About as AP as it gets.

Maya: So the Awakening caused the Revolution. I'm locking that in.

Marcus: [firm] Not exactly. The trap answer is "the Awakening caused the Revolution." It's a live debate, not a fact.

Maya: No goodbyes yet.

Maya: Four boxes, let's land them. One: Whitefield. Cold churches, the 1662 halfway compromise, and a twenty-something Englishman preaching to fields in 1739. The sound check said thirty thousand could hear him. Two: Edwards. Enfield, July 1741, the spider over the fire, read nearly flat. The argument got him fired in 1750. Three: Franklin's counterweight. The Almanack from 1732, the Junto in 1727, the clockmaker God, reason answering feeling. Four: the splits. The Old Lights were the revival fans, the New Lights held the line, and the organizing taught colonists to defy authority.

Marcus: One correction.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Which did I flip?

Marcus: The lights. The New Lights were the revival fans. The Old Lights held the line.

Maya: [sheepish] I did flip it. New Lights, the revival. Old Lights, the old guard. Landing all four.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it.

Maya: One. The stimulus is Edwards, 1741, and these are his actual words: "The God that holds you over the pit of hell, much as one holds a spider, or some loathsome insect, over the fire." Who's preaching, and what's the point of this passage?

[15-second pause]

Maya: Jonathan Edwards, at Enfield, Connecticut, in July 1741 — the spider image, the pit of hell, the famous sermon title. The point is the method, not the theology: terror as conversion, the Awakening's emotional style doing what the cold, formal churches couldn't.

Maya: Two. Why did the Awakening split churches into New Lights and Old Lights?

[15-second pause]

Maya: The split was a fight over authority — itinerant preachers drew crowds without invitations, and converts claimed their own experience outranked the old ministers. Once feeling counts as proof, the old guard's rules stop working, and the church divides.

Maya: Three. How far did the Great Awakening go toward causing the American Revolution?

[20-second pause]

Maya: Argue both sides. For: colonists learned to organize and defy the established church, in a shared experience from New England to Georgia. Against: the fires burned out decades before 1776, and plenty of patriot leaders were never awakened. The graders want the weighing, not the verdict.

Maya: One more, fast. The Awakening and the Enlightenment both challenged authority. Which one worked through the heart, and which through the head?

[5-second pause]

Maya: The Awakening through the heart, the Enlightenment through the head.

Maya: [confident tone] Check your four boxes. The colonies learned to argue with authority —

Marcus: [professional broadcast tone] with the heart, and with the head.

Maya: [intrigued] Next time: colonial self-government. The assemblies learned to rule themselves while London looked the other way. And a printer named Zenger who put press freedom on trial.
