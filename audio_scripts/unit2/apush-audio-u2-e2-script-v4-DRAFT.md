Maya: Last time: four empires racing for a continent: Spain's missions, France's rivers, Dutch trade, and England's settlers, the ones who stayed. This time the settlers build a labor machine called indentured servitude, and watch it explode into Bacon's Rebellion. Three boxes: indentured servitude, Bacon's Rebellion, and the slave codes of 1705. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes, and you'll land all three.

Marcus: Start with the math of tobacco. One crop, endless work, and colonists who'd rather starve than swing a hoe. The fix: indentured servants. A poor English kid signs away four to seven years for a boat ticket. The planter gets cheap labor, and the colony sweetened it: fifty acres of land for every person a planter imported. The headright. Importing workers literally bought you land.

Marcus: And the pitch to the servant was freedom dues. Finish the contract, collect some corn, some clothes, maybe a little land — then join the landowning class yourself. That was the promise of Virginia: work a while, then start over as a freeholder.

Maya: What did the years actually feel like? The contract sounds almost fair on paper.

Marcus: The paper lied. Your contract could be bought and sold like the boat ticket it paid for. Run away and get caught, and the court added years to your term. Marry without your master's permission, and a woman servant got extra time tacked on. The work broke bodies, and the law backed the master.

Maya: I signed a phone contract at sixteen and thought I'd died. Four years felt like forever. So why didn't the planters just keep that deal going?

Marcus: Because the deal worked too well. Servants stopped dying. The seasoning fevers eased, and the population started growing on its own. So more freed servants survived every year, and every one of them wanted the land they'd been promised. But the good river land was already taken. That left a growing crowd of young, poor, landless, armed men. Berkeley, a governor in his seventies, counted them (six parts of seven, he wrote) and panicked.

Maya: So the servants snapped. A labor strike, but with muskets?

Marcus: Servants were in it, yes. But so were poor farmers, enslaved Black people, and even some rich planters. That's the terrifying part: everybody angry at once, crossing every line that usually kept them apart. The spark was land: after raids by the Doegs and Susquehannocks on the frontier, settlers wanted a war, and Berkeley said no. He liked the fur trade and didn't want a costly war, so he built forts instead. Then Nathaniel Bacon, a twenty-nine-year-old planter hungry for land, demanded a commission, got refused, and raised his own army anyway. He even issued a Declaration of the People, listing his grievances like a manifesto — unjust taxes, a rigged court, the beaver-trade monopoly.

Maya: Hold on. The governor says no, and Bacon just goes to war without permission?

Marcus: Berkeley called him a rebel. Bacon marched on Jamestown and burned it that September. About a month later, dysentery killed him. The rebellion's head died of a stomach bug, and the whole thing fell apart. Berkeley came back and hanged about two dozen of the leaders. The image the planters never forgot: poor whites and enslaved Blacks, fighting side by side.

Maya: Did London cheer him on? He put the rebellion down.

Marcus: London was horrified. The king recalled Berkeley, and the line attributed to Charles the Second was that the old fool had hanged more men in that naked country than the king had for the murder of his father.

Maya: So was Berkeley actually scared, or was that letter just him performing for London?

Marcus: Scared enough. He wrote it reporting back to London, and the arithmetic was real. Whether he meant every word... I don't think that's settled. But the planters' answer wasn't about Berkeley. It was about never again.

Marcus: That was the lesson of 1676: never let the poor unite across race. The fix was a workforce split by race from birth: enslaved African labor. Lifetime. Hereditary, so the children belonged to the planter too. And visibly distinct, so no poor white man would ever mistake himself for one of them. The supply helped: late in the century the English slave-trade monopoly broke, enslaved Africans got cheaper, and English servants got scarcer.

Maya: And the legal template was imported, right? They didn't invent the code from scratch.

Marcus: Imported from Barbados. In 1661 the sugar island passed the first comprehensive slave code in English America: people defined as property, punishments written down as law. Chesapeake planters traded with the West Indies constantly, and they brought the Barbados idea home with them. Don't improvise the system. Codify it.

Maya: So it was just a math problem? Cheaper labor wins?

Marcus: That's the economics half. Edmund Morgan, the historian, said the bigger half was political. The planters were terrified of another Bacon: armed, poor, united. Enslaved labor solved both problems at once, cheap for life and divided by race so they'd never stand with poor whites again. He called it an instinctive attempt to subdue class conflict by racism. He spent a whole book on the paradox: American Slavery, American Freedom. Other historians push back and say the racism was already there. Which drove which? Nobody's fully sure. But the codes are the fact.

Maya: The codes. 1705 wasn't the start, right?

Marcus: Not the first laws. Virginia had been passing one-off laws for decades. A 1662 law said a child's status followed the mother. A 1667 law said baptism didn't free anybody. And before any of that, there was a warning in 1640: three servants ran away together, two white, one Black. Caught, hauled back. The two white men got extra years tacked on. John Punch, the Black man, got life.

Maya: Same crime, different sentence.

Marcus: Exactly. So 1705 wasn't an invention, it was a consolidation. The assembly gathered it all into one code: slavery for life, hereditary, with white servants and enslaved Black people under different law. People as property — that's what chattel means. Race, written into statute. And the code spelled out what custom never could. It classified enslaved people as real estate, so they passed to your heirs like land. It said a master who killed an enslaved worker during punishment faced no prosecution. Custom had done it piecemeal. The code made it permanent and inheritable. And Virginia wasn't alone; every colony drew some version of this line. The box: the 1705 slave codes.

Maya: So for the test, if they ask "most significant cause" of the shift, I say the rebellion?

Marcus: You say the chain: rebellion to codes. Economics set the table; the rebellion decided the seating. Lead with Bacon; land on 1705.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. Box one: indentured servitude. Four to seven years for a boat ticket, fifty acres a head for the planter who imported you: the headright. The system broke when servants survived, wanted land, and found the good land gone. Checking that one off.

Maya: Box two: Bacon's Rebellion, 1676. Frontier settlers wanted a war after the raids, Berkeley refused, Bacon went anyway, burned Jamestown, died of dysentery. Then Berkeley hanged — what, a hundred rebels?

Marcus: About two dozen.

Maya: Two dozen, then. Checking that one off.

Maya: Box three: the 1705 slave codes. Virginia folded decades of smaller laws — and the John Punch warning from 1640 — into one code: slavery for life, hereditary, different law for Black and white. So racism caused the codes —

Marcus: Flip it. Morgan's point: the codes helped cause race. Slavery came first; the law drew the line.

Maya: Codes first, race after — Morgan's flip. Checking that one off. Three for three.

Maya: Two questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One: Berkeley's own letter, 1676: he governs "a people where six parts of seven at least are poor, indebted, discontented and armed." What's he afraid of, and what did planters change after the rebellion so it couldn't happen again?

[5-second pause]

Maya: He's afraid of the united poor, servants, freedmen, enslaved people, all armed. The change: a workforce split by race, lifetime, hereditary, enslaved, and the slave codes to keep it that way.

Maya: Two: explain the chain from Bacon's Rebellion to the 1705 codes in two sentences.

[5-second pause]

Maya: The rebellion showed planters that poor whites and enslaved Blacks could unite, which was their worst fear. Their answer: a divided labor force, written into law in 1705, with race as the dividing line.

Maya: Check your three boxes. Episode two in the books.

Maya: Next time: New England. The Puritans came to build a city upon a hill, and started banishing their own almost immediately.

Marcus: The rebellion united the poor —

Maya: so the law divided them by race.
