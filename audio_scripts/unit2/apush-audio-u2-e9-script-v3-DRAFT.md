Maya: Last time: one 22-year-old's shot in the rain, a war gone global, a bill of roughly 130 million pounds. London looked back at its empire and decided to finally start running it. This time: the whole unit, 1607 to 1763, in one episode. Ten questions, three boxes on your sheet: empires and the labor machine, regions and the money, the deal runs out. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes, all ten, then you test yourself.

Marcus: Question one. Match the model, one sentence each: Spain, France, Holland, England.

Maya: Spain: crown-run, souls and silver: missions, mines, and the casta ladder ranking your grandparents. France: a fur-trade empire, beaver pelts for felt hats, thin on the map: a fur economy never needed farmers. Holland: a company colony, patroons on the Hudson, New Amsterdam surrendered in 1664. England: company money, settler families, farms that filled the land and pushed Native peoples off it.

Marcus: Clean sweep, four for four. Question two: why did Jamestown survive when it should have died?

Maya: It nearly didn't. The Starving Time, 1609 to 1610, left about sixty of five hundred alive. Then John Rolfe's tobacco, around 1614, gave the colony something England would buy. The headright promised fifty acres a head to whoever imported you, and his marriage to Pocahontas bought a few years of peace with the Powhatans.

Marcus: And the Pocahontas story itself?

Maya: The cartoon version? Smith claimed she saved him, but it comes from his own pen, years later. Historians argue about it.

Marcus: The record won't fully settle it. The marriage and the tobacco are the facts.

Marcus: Question three: Bacon's Rebellion to the slave codes. Walk the chain.

Maya: 1676: frontier settlers want war after the raids, Berkeley says no, Bacon raises an army, burns Jamestown, dies of dysentery. The planters' lesson: never let the poor unite across race, so the 1705 codes drew the line that kept them apart.

Marcus: So the codes caused the rebellion?

Maya: No — wait. Flip it. The rebellion caused the codes. Economics set the table, the rebellion picked the seating.

Marcus: And the line got redrawn in 1740. The year before, enslaved Africans rose at Stono River, marching for Spanish Florida — and South Carolina answered with the Negro Act, one of the harshest slave codes in British North America.

Marcus: Never again.

Maya: Never again.

Marcus: Box one, down.

Marcus: Box two: regions and the money. Question four: why did the Puritans come, and how did covenant shape their towns?

Maya: They came to purify the Church of England, not leave it. Leaving was the Pilgrims, 1620 — the Mayflower Compact, majority rule signed aboard ship before they came ashore. The Great Migration, 1630s, around twenty thousand people, and nearly half were women. Covenant ran everything: churches, towns, the meetinghouse at the center. Dedham signed one in 1636 about taxes and disputes. A city upon a hill, with every eye watching: aspiration and warning both.

Marcus: Question five: what made the Middle Colonies the diversity lab?

Maya: Penn's Holy Experiment, 1681: religious liberty as a sales pitch, because empty land pays nothing. The Germans answered: Pennsylvania Dutch, really Deutsch, about a third of the colony by the Revolution. Scots-Irish on the frontier, Dutch, Swedes, Welsh already there. My school cafeteria has a table for every language: the German club, the Punjabi table. Same energy as those docks.

Marcus: And the limits of the experiment?

Maya: Plenty: enslaved people in a Quaker colony, the Walking Purchase defrauding the Lenape in 1737, Catholics distrusted. Pluralist, not tolerant.

Marcus: Question six: mercantilism in one paragraph: theory, rules, and the dodge.

Maya: Theory: the world's wealth is fixed, so stack the gold and silver. The colonies ship raw materials cheap and buy finished goods dear. Rules: the Navigation Acts (1651, 1660, 1663): enumerated goods like tobacco sail to England only, on English ships. The dodge: smuggling as a career. The Molasses Act of 1733 went unenforced; New England's distillers kept buying French molasses and bribing the customs men.

Marcus: And historians split on whether the whole bargain helped or caged the colonies.

Maya: Protected markets and the Navy's sea lanes on one side, higher prices and boxed-in trade on the other.

Marcus: Box three: the deal runs out. Question seven: the Great Awakening's political aftershock.

Maya: Heart and head, both challenging authority. Whitefield in the fields, mid-twenties, crowds in the thousands. Franklin measured his voice at thirty thousand. Edwards at Enfield, July 1741, the spider over the fire, read almost flat while the room came apart. The aftershock: New Lights versus Old Lights, church splits that taught ordinary people to organize and defy. Some historians call it the colonies' first shared experience. Others call it a stretch.

Marcus: Question eight: how did assemblies get powerful while London wasn't looking?

Maya: The purse. They voted the taxes and, in a lot of colonies, the governor's salary. The king's man went hat in hand to the planters. Walpole's sleeping dogs: the neglect was real even if the exact quote is shaky — and decades of looking away turned a habit into something like a right. Plus the Zenger trial, 1735: a jury that wouldn't convict a printer for defying a governor.

Marcus: Question nine: the French and Indian War. Why did Britain win, and what did victory cost?

Maya: They won because — bigger army? More ships?

Marcus: Numbers helped, but the answer is strategy. 1757: Pitt the Elder. Subsidize Prussia, pin France in Europe, pour everything into North America. Quebec, 1759, both commanders dead, New France finished.

Maya: "I will conquer America in Germany," that's the actual quote?

Marcus: Attributed. The wording's debated; the strategy isn't.

Maya: And the cost: roughly 130 million pounds of debt. Pontiac's Rebellion the moment the French left. The Proclamation Line in October 1763, no settlement past the Appalachians. And the Albany Plan showed the colonies couldn't unite even when they needed to.

Marcus: Last one. Question ten: one sentence: why did 1763 change everything?

Maya: Growth made the colonies valuable, diverse, and self-governing, and therefore ungovernable on Britain's old terms.

Marcus: There it is. That's the thesis. Keep it.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: empires and the labor machine. Four models: Spain's souls and silver, the missions until the Pueblos answered in 1680, France's fur, Holland's company, England's settlers. Jamestown lived because tobacco paid. Bacon's Rebellion taught the planters never again, and the 1705 codes drew the line.

Marcus: Landed.

Maya: Two: regions and the money. Puritans, covenant towns, the city on a hill, and the 1675–76 war that burned a dozen towns and broke Native power in New England. The Middle Colonies: diversity lab, pluralist not tolerant. Further south: rice and indigo, worked by enslaved labor. Mercantilism: fixed pie, Navigation Acts, the triangle trade's messy reality, and a whole coast that treated the laws as suggestions.

Marcus: Box two, down.

Maya: Three: the deal runs out. The Awakening's aftershock: heart and head, both defying authority. Assemblies with the purse, Zenger's jury, and the sleeping dogs. The war: Pitt's gamble, the 1763 bill. The debt was sixty million, right?

Marcus: The war added about sixty. The total landed near one-thirty.

Maya: War added sixty, total one-thirty. Pontiac, the Proclamation Line, and London deciding to finally run the empire. Three boxes, all landed.

Maya: Two questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One: a 1735 courtroom speech: "not the cause of one poor printer... it is the cause of liberty." Name the trial, the lawyer, and the bigger idea.

[5-second pause]

Maya: The Zenger trial. The lawyer's Andrew Hamilton, not the musical. The bigger idea... the truth defense? No — wait. The judge wouldn't even let Hamilton prove it. The bigger idea is the jury: ordinary colonists looking at raw power and saying no anyway. Press freedom as a British subject's right, forty years before independence.

Maya: Two: Grenville does the math in 1763. Name two things the empire does next, and the era they end.

[5-second pause]

Maya: Enforce the trade laws and levy new taxes on the colonies, and they end salutary neglect.
