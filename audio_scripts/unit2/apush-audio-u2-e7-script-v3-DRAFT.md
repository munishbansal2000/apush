Maya: Last time: the Great Awakening: Whitefield and Edwards setting the colonies on fire, Old Lights glaring at New Lights. This time: a quieter conversion. How the colonies learned to govern themselves while London wasn't looking, and why that made 1763 feel like a betrayal. Three boxes on my sheet: power of the purse, the Zenger trial, and Anglicization. Circle the ones you couldn't explain cold right now. Eight minutes, let's land all three.

Marcus: Start with the weirdest power flip in colonial America. The royal governor arrives with the king's commission, a wig, and his orders. But the assembly sitting in Williamsburg holds the one thing his commission doesn't include: money.

Maya: Because they vote the taxes.

Marcus: They vote the taxes, they vote the budget. In a lot of colonies, they vote the governor's own salary too. So the king's representative goes hat in hand to the planters for his paycheck.

Maya: No boss does that. More like an employee review. My school's student council once held the bake-sale money hostage until the principal gave us spirit week. Same energy, bigger wigs.

Marcus: Same logic, definitely. And London mostly let it happen. Early 1700s, a prime minister named Walpole decides the trade laws aren't worth enforcing. The ocean's wide, England has other wars. Nobody votes for neglect. It just settles in. The Navigation Acts stay on the books; smuggling stays on the water. Decade after decade, the assemblies practice the same move: we hold the purse, so we hold the power.

Maya: Okay, wait. If London wasn't enforcing anything and the assemblies were running the show, weren't the colonies basically already independent? Like, independent-independent?

Marcus: Historians are still fighting about that one. Some say yes: by the mid-1700s the colonies governed themselves in everything but name. Others say that's hindsight: the colonists still called themselves loyal British subjects, still petitioned the king, still wanted his blessing. The power was real. The break wasn't. Not yet.

Marcus: Though the crown reached back in when it wanted men. British press gangs grabbed colonial sailors off the docks to crew warships. Boston rioted over it for three days in 1747. Loyal, yes. Cuddly, no.

Maya: Loyal with a riot clause.

Maya: One box down. Checking that one.

Marcus: Box two is a courtroom. New York, 1735. A German immigrant printer named John Peter Zenger runs a paper called the New-York Weekly Journal, and for about a year he roasts the royal governor, William Cosby, in print.

Maya: Roasting. In 1734.

Marcus: Relentless. Cosby had fired a judge who ruled against him in a salary fight, and Zenger's paper printed the whole ugly story. So Cosby does what cornered governors do: he has Zenger arrested for seditious libel. November 1734. Zenger sits in a jail cell for the better part of a year. His wife Anna keeps the paper printing without him.

Maya: Hold on — Hamilton? Like the musical?

Marcus: Same last name, wrong decade. Andrew Hamilton: Scottish-born, Philadelphia's most famous lawyer, old, sick, supposedly retired. He takes the case, and his argument is the dangerous one: if what Zenger printed was true, it's not libel. Truth is the defense.

Marcus: The judge, Cosby's handpicked man, says no. Under the law, the words are the crime; truth doesn't matter. So Hamilton turns to the jury and gives the speech: "it is not the cause of one poor printer, nor of New-York alone, which you are now trying... It is the best cause. It is the cause of liberty."

Maya: Chills. So the jury —

Marcus: Acquits. And I want to be straight with you about this part: the judge had ruled the other way. No new law was written that day. It was a jury looking at raw power and saying no anyway.

Maya: So Zenger walks free, but the law doesn't actually change?

Marcus: Not that day. On the books, truth was still no defense. What changed was the idea. A colonial jury had decided a printer could criticize a governor and live. Forty years before independence, the colonies are already rehearsing the Revolution's favorite argument.

Marcus: Box three is the paradox. Historians call it Anglicization. Big word, simple idea. By the 1700s, colonists are reading English papers, drinking English tea, building English-style houses, quoting English law.

Maya: Trying really hard to be British.

Marcus: And the harder they tried, the sharper the insult got. The more British they felt, the less they'd accept being treated as second-class British. A Virginia planter reads the same papers as a London merchant, drinks the same tea. Then he gets told his assembly's laws can be vetoed by men who've never seen Virginia.

Maya: So why not just stop being British, then? Pick a side.

Marcus: Because being British was the side. As British subjects — that was the claim. Not "we're different, leave us alone." It was "we're Englishmen. Englishmen get assemblies, and trials, and presses, and Englishmen don't get pushed around by a Parliament they never elected." Their Britishness was the weapon.

Maya: There's the bridge to 1763.

Marcus: There's the bridge. A century of voting their own budgets, printing their own papers, thinking of themselves as full Englishmen. Then the war ends and London wants its empire back. New taxes, new rules, redcoats staying put. It doesn't land like policy. It lands like a promise broken.

Maya: And if an SAQ ever asks "to what extent" the colonies governed themselves: these three boxes are your answer.

Maya: So the spring is loaded. A century of self-rule about to meet a Parliament that wants its empire back. Three boxes, let's land them.

Maya: One: power of the purse. Assemblies like the House of Burgesses in Williamsburg voted the taxes, and in a lot of colonies the governor's salary, so the king's man answered to their budget. London looked the other way for decades, and the habit hardened into something like a right. And when London did reach back in for sailors, Boston rioted for three days.

Marcus: "Something like" is doing a lot of work there. The right was never written down. It was assumed.

Maya: Two: the Zenger trial, 1735. Governor Cosby of New York jails printer John Peter Zenger for criticizing him. Hamilton proves the stories were true, so the jury throws the case out.

Marcus: Careful — Hamilton never got to prove it. The judge barred the truth evidence. The jury acquitted anyway, choosing liberty over the law as written. That was the whole point.

Maya: Right. Jury says no to power; the law on the books stays ugly. Three: Anglicization. The more British the colonists felt, the harder they insisted on being treated as full British subjects — assemblies, presses, rights and all. Some historians say they were basically running themselves by the 1760s; others say that's hindsight, because they still felt loyal.

Marcus: Both things were true. The power was real, and so was the loyalty. That collision is what loads the spring.

Maya: All three landed.

Maya: Two questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One: a governor writes to London, furious: the assembly won't vote his salary unless he signs their tax bill. What does this source show about who really holds power in the colony?

[5-second pause]

Maya: It shows the purse is the power. The governor holds the king's commission; the assembly holds the money. The man sent to rule the colony ends up answering to it.

Maya: Two: a 1735 courtroom speech says the Zenger case "is not the cause of one poor printer, nor of New-York alone... it is the cause of liberty." What's the point of this source? What's the bigger idea the speaker is selling?

[5-second pause]

Maya: That press freedom isn't about one printer. It's a right of British subjects, and a jury defending it defends every colonist. The Revolution's pamphlet wars, forty years early.

Maya: Check your three boxes.
