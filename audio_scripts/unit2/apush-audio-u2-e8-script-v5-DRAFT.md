# Pronunciation: zhoo-mohn-VEEL (Jumonville), tan-uh-kuh-RIH-sun (Tanacharison), doo-KAYN (Duquesne), muh-nahn-guh-HEE-luh (Monongahela), mohn-KAHM (Montcalm), PAHN-tee-ak (Pontiac), GREN-vil (Grenville), lah-sah-see-NAH (l'assassinat).

Maya: Last time: the power of the purse, the Zenger trial, and Anglicization. This time: a twenty-two-year-old fires a shot in the Ohio country in 1754, and it sets the world on fire. Four boxes: the shot, Albany and Braddock, the fall of Quebec, and the aftermath: a line on a map, an enormous debt, and the end of London looking the other way. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Twelve minutes.

Marcus: May 1754. The Ohio country, before dawn, in the rain. Washington, twenty-two, a Virginia militia officer, has about forty men around a French camp in a rocky hollow called Jumonville Glen. Somebody fires. Then the French surrender. Around ten dead, including their commander, the Sieur de Jumonville. About twenty captured. One man slips away into the woods.

Maya: And France says that commander was carrying a diplomatic message, not attack orders.

Marcus: France's version: Jumonville was an envoy on a peaceful mission. Washington says his men were hiding in a ravine, soldiers not diplomats. A patrol had every right to fire. Nobody's fully sure. The two sides tell the same death two ways.

Maya: So whose truth does the exam want?

Marcus: The exam doesn't ask you to pick. It asks what the killing did: France called it murder, and used it to justify war. Tanacharison, the Seneca leader the English called the Half-King, was standing beside Washington with his warriors. During the interrogation of the captives, Tanacharison tomahawked Jumonville to death. Nine more French captives were scalped before Washington could stop it. The brains detail is legend — reported, never proven — but France didn't need it. They had a dead officer.

Maya: So Washington's troops killed a French... wait, he wasn't an ambassador, was he?

Marcus: Good catch. Not an ambassador — an ensign, a junior officer. And it wasn't Washington's men who did the killing. But France's charge held anyway: in peacetime, you'd ambushed and killed a French officer. That's a massacre in Paris, a skirmish in London.

Maya: Peacetime — I keep forgetting Britain and France weren't even at war.

Marcus: Right. Not officially. Which is why what happens five weeks later matters so much. Washington falls back and throws up a stockade in a meadow: Fort Necessity. July third, 1754. More rain. One day of fighting, and Washington is beaten. He signs the surrender terms. In French. Which he can't read.

Maya: Your turn. The paper's in French, which you can't read. Your men are exhausted, and the French want your signature. What's the danger in signing blind?

[10-second pause]

Maya: They can write whatever they want.

Marcus: And they did. Buried in the surrender terms was a French word: l'assassinat. Assassination.

Maya: Washington signed a paper admitting he'd assassinated Jumonville.

Marcus: The French carried it home like a confession. And that document helped light the fuse. It was also, for the record, Washington's only surrender in his entire military career.

Maya: If the exam hands you that surrender document, don't argue whether Jumonville was murdered. The point is what the paper did: it gave France its casus belli, its reason to fight.

Maya: And the common mistake: don't write that Jumonville was a French general. He was an ensign, a junior officer. France's fury wasn't about his rank. It was about a killing in peacetime.

Maya: Albany, next.

Marcus: Still 1754. Seven colonies send delegates to Albany, New York, to coordinate against the French. Franklin puts a plan on the table: one united colonial government, a council, a president appointed by the king. The Albany Plan of Union.

Marcus: The colonies say no first. They'd rather keep their own power than share it. Then London says no too. Franklin's big idea dies twice.

Maya: On Albany, the exam always asks a why-not. Don't write that Parliament vetoed the plan. The colonies rejected it to protect their own sovereignty. If a stimulus shows colonial governments squabbling, the answer is sovereignty, not incompetence.

Maya: Wait, is "incompetence" the trap?

Marcus: It's the trap a lot of students fall into — "the colonies were just disorganized." They weren't disorganized; they were guarding their own power.

Maya: Common mistake, same box: don't date the Albany Plan after Braddock. It's 1754, before the real army arrives.

Marcus: Then comes the real army. July 9, 1755. General Edward Braddock marches on Fort Duquesne with nearly fifteen hundred British regulars, the best-drilled army on earth. A smaller French and Native force hits the column near the Monongahela. In the woods.

Maya: My uncle does search-and-rescue in the Cascades. He says the people who get lost are the ones who trust their instincts instead of the map. Braddock trusted the map of Europe in the woods of Pennsylvania.

Marcus: He trusted it, and it killed him. The British regulars stand in their lines, and the French and their Native allies shoot from the tree line. Nearly a thousand British killed or wounded. Braddock is wounded and dies four days later. Washington is there too, an aide on Braddock's staff. He walks out alive, and writes home about it. His actual words, from his July 1755 letter to his brother: "four bullets through my coat, and two horses shot under me." Four holes in the coat. Two horses shot under him. He rides home unhurt.

Maya: "Shot heard 'round the world" — that's this, right? The shot that started the world war?

Marcus: Nice instinct, wrong war. Emerson's poem. Lexington and Concord, 1775. This shot is the one nobody remembers, and it started the world war anyway.

Maya: Two boxes down. That one's earned.

Marcus: Three years of losing. Then 1757: William Pitt takes charge of Britain's war. His line, or the line attributed to him: conquer America in Germany. The wording's debated. The strategy isn't.

Maya: Your turn. You're Pitt, 1757. Britain's losing in America, France is fighting Prussia in Europe. Where do you spend?

[8-second pause]

Maya: Keep France tied down in Europe, and spend the real money where the war gets decided: North America.

Marcus: Exactly. Twenty-five thousand redcoats across the Atlantic, London even paying the colonial militias' expenses.

Marcus: The payoff is September 13, 1759: Quebec. Wolfe lands before dawn and scales the cliffs in the dark. By morning he's lined up on the Plains of Abraham. Montcalm marches out to meet him instead of waiting. The battle that decides North America lasts under an hour. By the end, both commanders are dying: Wolfe on the field, Montcalm by morning. Quebec falls. Montreal goes the year after.

Maya: Both generals die and Britain still wins. Only in APUSH.

Marcus: February 10, 1763. The Treaty of Paris. France surrenders nearly its whole North American empire: Canada and everything east of the Mississippi go to Britain. Spain gets Louisiana, including New Orleans, as compensation for giving up Florida to Britain.

Maya: And France keeps the sugar islands?

Marcus: Guadeloupe, Martinique. To the merchants in Paris, Caribbean sugar was worth more than all of Canada. France traded a continent for sugar.

Maya: And here's the trap the exam sets with those sugar islands. If it asks why France walked away from Canada so easily, the answer is the sugar islands. For Paris, sugar profits outweighed Canadian land.

Marcus: Same box, one more trap: don't write that France "lost everything in North America." It kept the Caribbean sugar colonies.

Maya: The war's won. Now the bill.

Marcus: Then the bill. And the line. Britain has just won a continent — and run up an enormous debt.

Maya: But the French are gone. Who's left to fight over it?

Marcus: Everybody who actually lived there. Spring 1763. The French are barely gone, and an Ottawa leader named Pontiac organizes a rising. The French had been trade partners: gifts, diplomacy, ceremonies. The British ended the gift diplomacy and treated the Ohio peoples as a beaten enemy. Pontiac's warriors besiege Fort Detroit, then British forts fall across the Great Lakes and the Ohio Valley. Hundreds of soldiers and thousands of colonists killed or captured, and it takes the British more than a year to break it.

Maya: Okay, the bill I get. What's the line?

Marcus: London panics. October 7, 1763: the Proclamation. No settlement west of the Appalachians: to stop paying for frontier wars, while keeping the fur trade flowing. The colonists who'd just fought for that land walk right over the line.

Maya: Including the speculators.

Marcus: Including Washington. Years spent fighting for two hundred thousand acres of Ohio bounty land promised his Virginia Regiment. The Proclamation froze the grants. The kid who started the war couldn't cash in on winning it.

Maya: One more trap, on the Proclamation. The exam wants the motive. Don't write "to punish the colonists." Follow the money: Britain wanted to stop paying for frontier wars. The treasury, not revenge.

Marcus: Careful with the Proclamation's weight, though: don't say the Proclamation caused the Revolution. It angered speculators and settlers. The tax fight is a whole other episode. The line was one grievance among many.

Maya: Four boxes, let's land them. One: Jumonville Glen, May 1754. Washington, Tanacharison, about fifteen minutes in the rain. France calls it murder, and his signed paper becomes France's confession — the only surrender of Washington's career.

Marcus: Correct. And l'assassinat.

Maya: Two: Albany and Braddock. Franklin's plan for united colonies dies because nobody gives up power. Then Braddock's regulars learn European lines don't work in the woods, July 1755, the Monongahela. Washington rides out with four bullets through his coat.

Marcus: His actual words.

Maya: Three: Pitt bets everything on North America. Quebec, September 1759: under an hour, both generals dead, and France trades a continent for sugar in the Treaty of Paris, February 1763.

Marcus: Four?

Maya: Four: Pontiac's Rebellion, the Proclamation line along... the Mississippi? No. The Appalachians. London draws a line. The colonists ignore it. An enormous debt, and a century of London looking the other way is over.

Marcus: The Appalachians. Mississippi was the treaty's line. And that century of neglect has a name. The programs that ended it are next episode's story.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. On the long essay: this war is your turning-point paragraph. 1763 changes everything. New land, an enormous debt, and a bill somebody has to pay.

Maya: One. The stimulus is the surrender document Washington signed at Fort Necessity: a French text containing the word l'assassinat. What does this source prove about the war's start?

[15-second pause]

Maya: It proves the incident became France's justification for war, not that Washington was guilty: Washington couldn't read French; the paper said "assassination." The document mattered as propaganda. France carried home a confession, real or not, and used it to demand revenge.

Maya: Two. Braddock's army outnumbered the French and their Native allies at the Monongahela. So why did Britain lose?

[20-second pause]

Maya: European tactics failed in American woods: British regulars held formation and fired volleys while French and Native fighters fired from the tree line; nearly a thousand British killed or wounded. Discipline designed for open fields became a target in the forest, and the war exposed the limits of an empire's way of fighting.

Maya: Three. The stimulus is a 1763 map: everything east of the Mississippi is British, Louisiana and New Orleans are Spanish, Florida is British. What does the map show about who won what?

[15-second pause]

Maya: Britain won the continent; France kept the sugar: Canada and the east go to Britain, but France keeps Guadeloupe and Martinique, and Spain trades Florida for Louisiana. The map shows France chose sugar profits over land, and Britain chose the land it would now have to pay for.

Maya: One more, fast. Why did London draw the Proclamation line — revenge, or the treasury?

[5-second pause]

Maya: The treasury. London wanted the frontier wars off its books.

Maya: Check your four boxes.

Maya: Next time: the bill lands. George Grenville does the math, and the colonies learn what an empire costs.

Marcus: A continent won —

Maya: and London sends the bill.
