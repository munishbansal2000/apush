# Pronunciation: MEHR-kun-tih-lizm (mercantilism), BOOL-yun-izm (bullionism), eh-NOO-muh-ray-ted (enumerated), SAL-yoo-teh-ree (salutary), WAHL-pohl (Walpole), moh-LAS-iz (molasses)

Maya: Last time: Penn's holy experiment, the middle colonies as a diversity lab. This time: the money underneath it all. Britain ran its empire on a theory that said the world's wealth was fixed, so grab the gold and strangle everyone else's trade. Three boxes: mercantilism, the Navigation Acts, and salutary neglect. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes: the theory, the rules, and the great colonial dodge.

Marcus: The theory first. Mercantilism said a nation's wealth was the pile of gold and silver it sat on. Bullion, they called it. And the world's pile was fixed, so one country's gain was another country's loss. The winning move: sell more than you buy. The favorable balance of trade.

Maya: I had a piggy bank shaped like a cannon. Counted the coins every Sunday and refused to spend a single one, because the pile WAS the wealth. Ten-year-old mercantilist.

Marcus: Which is the whole theory, honestly. Now give it an empire. The colonies got two jobs: ship raw materials to England cheap, and buy England's finished goods at full price. And never, ever trade with the rival if London could help it.

Maya: So gold-rush economics. Whoever digs up the most gold wins.

Marcus: Not digging. Stacking. Mercantilists didn't care who dug it up. They cared who ended up sitting on the pile.

Maya: Mercantilism. Wealth is the pile. The pie is fixed. And the colonies are the farm and the customer. Checking that one.

Marcus: The theory needed teeth, and Parliament obliged: the Navigation Acts of 1651, 1660, and 1663. Start with the valuable goods — the enumerated list, tobacco chief among them. Those could only be shipped to England.

Maya: So a Virginia planter can't sell his tobacco to the Dutch, even if the Dutch pay more.

Marcus: Not legally. And the ships carrying it had to be English, crewed mostly by English sailors. Then the last turn of the screw: anything the colonies bought from Europe had to pass through an English port first.

Maya: French cloth and Dutch tools, all taking the long way through London.

Marcus: And London takes its cut at every stop. Tobacco sails to London, gets taxed, gets re-exported. The middleman always wins, and the middleman is always London.

Maya: And the colonists just agreed to this? People who crossed an ocean to get rich don't strike me as rule-followers.

Marcus: They didn't agree. They dodged. Smuggling became a way of life up and down the coast. Customs officials took bribes — some just took their cut and looked away. And the famous example is John Hancock: the big-signature guy, whose fortune had a lot to do with dodging the customs house.

Maya: Wait. "Put your John Hancock here." The signature's a whole idiom. Was he actually guilty, though? Like, convicted?

Marcus: Accused, never convicted. The customs men seized his ship, the Liberty, in 1768, and the case fell apart. Whether he was guilty on every count, historians still argue about that. That his business ran on dodging the duties? That's the version you'll find in most textbooks.

Maya: So the rules said England, and the ships went wherever paid.

Marcus: That was the tension. Then 1733: Parliament tried to close one loophole with the Molasses Act. New England's rum distillers were buying cheap French molasses, so Parliament taxed foreign molasses hard, trying to force them onto British molasses.

Maya: And New England just... paid it?

Marcus: New England ignored it. Bribed officials, forged paperwork, midnight landings. The duty was rarely paid. One act of Parliament, and a whole region treated London's trade laws as optional.

Maya: The laws were real. The obedience was optional.

Marcus: Now the picture everyone draws wrong: the triangular trade. Textbooks love the neat triangle. English goods to Africa, enslaved Africans to the colonies, sugar and tobacco back to England. Clean arrows, three sides.

Maya: I labeled that diagram in eighth grade. With colored pencils.

Marcus: Keep the colored pencils, but add a footnote. Real trade was messier. Plenty of ships ran two legs and never completed the triangle. New England rum went to Africa, sure, but plenty sailed straight to the Caribbean. Most voyages were bilateral deals, not grand circuits. The triangle names a pattern, not the average voyage.

Maya: So if the triangle's mostly diagram, what's it actually telling us?

Marcus: Dependency. New England's distilleries needed Caribbean sugar. Caribbean sugar plantations needed enslaved labor. That chain is why whole islands went all-in on one crop, and why the demand for enslaved workers kept climbing whether or not any single ship closed the loop.

Maya: The arrows lied. The chain didn't.

Marcus: Helped along because nobody in London enforced the rules. The 1720s and 30s: Robert Walpole runs Britain, and his supposed line on the colonies was "let sleeping dogs lie."

Maya: Is that a real quote? It sounds like something people invented and put on a mug.

Marcus: The exact wording comes to us secondhand, so nobody's fully sure he said those words. But the neglect was real. Customs offices left understaffed. Governors looking the other way. For decades, the empire ran on winks.

Maya: And it worked?

Marcus: That's the paradox of the whole episode. The system was built to keep the colonies subordinate: suppliers and customers, never rivals. Left unenforced, colonial merchants got rich inside it.

Maya: So the world's most successful economic policy was not having one?

Marcus: Having one and napping through it. Which is where historians split — not the neglect, the whole mercantilist bargain. One side says it was a decent deal: protected markets, steady buyers, the Royal Navy keeping the sea lanes open. The other side says it was a cage: higher prices on European goods, trade boxed onto England's terms, profits draining toward London.

Maya: And the exam move is "evaluate the extent": you don't pick a side, you weigh both.

Marcus: And one thread to carry forward: this prosperity, under neglect, is why 1763 feels like a betrayal. London looks at rich, confident colonies and decides to finally run the empire properly. Enforcement. New taxes. The sleeping dogs wake up.

Maya: Rich under a system built to keep them poor. Three boxes, let's land them.

Maya: One: mercantilism. Wealth is the pile of gold. The pie is fixed. The colonies ship the raw stuff and buy the finished stuff. And the argument: protected markets and the Navy's sea lanes on one side, higher prices and boxed-in trade on the other. Box one, landed.

Maya: Two: the Navigation Acts. 1651, 1660, 1663. Enumerated goods — tobacco's the big one — sail to England only, on English ships; European goods come through England first. Its shadow: smuggling as a career, Hancock's fortune, and a customs service that ran on bribes. Plus the Molasses Act of 1733, which New England obeyed to the letter —

Marcus: Ignored to the letter.

Maya: Right. Ignored. Box two, landed.

Maya: Three: salutary neglect. Walpole's sleeping dogs — maybe his words, definitely the neglect. The laws stay on the books while nobody enforces them, and merchants get rich in the gaps. The triangle trade: neat diagram, messier reality. Box three, landed. All three landed.
