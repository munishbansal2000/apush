Maya: Last time: four empires, four ways to want a continent — Spain's silver machine, France's beaver trade, the Dutch patroons, England's families and farms. This time: the labor that worked England's tobacco fields. Servants first. Then a rebellion burns the capital, and the planters rebuild the whole system on race. Three boxes: indentured servitude, Bacon's Rebellion, and the slave codes of 1705. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Thirteen minutes.

Marcus: Start with the deal. A poor English kid signs away four to seven years of labor for a boat ticket. Call it the indenture: paid up front with the passage. The planter gets cheap labor, and the headright you met last episode sweetened it: fifty acres to whoever paid the passage. The boss, not the boat ride.

Maya: And the payoff at the end? Freedom dues, right?

Marcus: Freedom dues: finish the contract and collect a little corn, some clothes, maybe some land. Then start over as a freeholder. That was Virginia's promise.

Maya: What did the years actually feel like?

Marcus: The paper lied more than it told. Your contract could be bought and sold like the ticket it paid for. Run away and get caught, and the court tacked years onto your term. Marry without your master's say-so, and a woman servant got extra time tacked on.

Maya: I detasseled corn one summer in Iowa. I lasted a week. Four to seven years of tobacco? I can't even picture it.

Marcus: The work broke bodies, and the law backed the master. Then the deal worked too well. The seasoning fevers eased, and more servants survived their terms. Every one of them wanted land. But the good river land was already taken. So a crowd of young, poor, landless men kept growing, and they were armed. Berkeley, the governor, wrote home in 1676 of "a people where six parts of seven at least are poor, indebted, discontented and armed."

Maya: Six of seven. He counted them.

Marcus: He counted them, and he was scared. Your turn. You're freed. No land on the rivers. Past the fall line — where ships stop — into piedmont country, the hilly land west of the rivers. But it's already fought-over ground. What do you do?

[10-second pause]

Maya: You push west anyway. Into the piedmont, into the frontier fights.

Marcus: Into the frontier fights. Which is where this explodes. Exam warning for box one: the exam tests the headright as a trap. Follow the money, not the muscle. The land went to the planter who paid, never the servant who sailed.

Maya: Don't write that servants and enslaved Africans were the same system. Different system — term labor vs. lifetime slavery. They overlapped for decades; don't merge them.

Maya: Checking that one. Box one: indentured servitude.

Maya: So the frontier. Who's out there fighting whom?

Marcus: It started with raids on the frontier farms: the Doegs, and the Susquehannocks dragged into the violence after. The settlers wanted a war. Berkeley said no. The wealthy traders were doing good business with the tribes, and a war would cost more than it gained. His answer: forts on the frontier. Cheaper than war.

Maya: The farmers hear "forts" and think "human shields."

Marcus: Then Nathaniel Bacon steps in. Twenty-nine, a planter, a recent arrival from England. Wealthy enough to buy in, but the good coast land was already taken. He demanded a commission to raise a militia and fight. Berkeley refused.

Maya: Bacon just goes anyway.

Marcus: He raised his own army, four to five hundred men, and marched. And the part that ruins the hero story: his men didn't just hit the Susquehannocks. They attacked the Pamunkeys too — allies of the English.

Maya: So much for the freedom-fighter version.

Marcus: The frontier farmers had real grievances. But nobody's the good guy in this one. Bacon even put it in writing: the Declaration of the People, July 1676. Unjust taxes, favorites in high office, the fur-trade monopoly, a government that wouldn't protect the frontier.

Maya: A manifesto, then. A public list of grievances.

Marcus: Then Bacon marched on Jamestown and burned it in September 1676. About a month later, dysentery killed him. The rebellion fell apart without him. Berkeley came back and hanged twenty-three of the leaders. The king recalled Berkeley, and Charles the Second's attributed line: the old fool had hanged more men in that naked country than the king had for the murder of his own father.

Maya: Attributed. So we don't actually know he said it.

Marcus: We don't. It's the line everyone repeats. Treat it as the story, not the transcript.

Maya: One exam warning for box two: don't call this a slave revolt. Enslaved Black people fought in it, side by side with poor whites — against Berkeley. The sides were class, not color. That IS the point.

Maya: Your turn. You're a Virginia planter in 1677. Your labor system just tried to kill you. You still need workers, but you'll never again trust a workforce that can unite across race. What do you build?

[10-second pause]

Maya: A workforce that can't unite. Divided by race, for life.

Marcus: Lifetime, hereditary (the children belong to the planter too), and visibly distinct, so no poor white man would mistake himself for one of them. That was the pivot. And the supply helped: the Dutch monopoly on the slave trade broke in 1682, and enslaved Africans got cheaper, just as English servants got scarcer.

Maya: The new system didn't arrive as one big law, though. It arrived in pieces, right?

Marcus: The code arrived in pieces. In 1640, three servants ran away together: two white, one Black. Caught and hauled back. The two white men got four more years on their terms. John Punch, the Black man, was condemned to slavery for life.

Maya: Same crime, different sentence.

Marcus: In 1662, Virginia made it law: a child's status followed the mother. English common law had said it followed the father. The colony flipped it, and slavery became hereditary. In 1667: baptism didn't free anybody.

Maya: So by 1705 there's not much left to invent — they just copied Barbados?

Marcus: Not copied — consolidated. Virginia wrote its own code out of decades of its own law. But the legal template did come from outside. Barbados, 1661: one of the first comprehensive slave codes in English America. People defined as property, punishments written down as law. Chesapeake planters traded with the sugar islands constantly, and they carried the Barbados idea home.

Maya: And Carolina too. The Carolina planters came from Barbados.

Marcus: They did. Wealthy plantation owners migrated from Barbados and built Carolina's southern economy to resemble the island's. The code traveled with the planters.

Maya: So 1705 gathers everything into one code. What did it actually say?

Marcus: It redefined who counted as a slave: any servant imported who wasn't Christian in their native country. It held enslaved people as real estate. Real estate is the word: land-like property, so they passed to your heirs like land. And it said a master who killed an enslaved worker during punishment faced no prosecution. As if it had never happened.

Maya: Did other colonies copy it?

Marcus: Virginia's code became the model.

Maya: Exam tip: if a stimulus hands you the words "real estate," that's the code talking. The word is the tell.

Maya: And the common mistake: don't write that 1705 started slavery. 1640, 1662, 1667. The code gathered decades of law into one book.

Marcus: Now the historian's fight. Edmund Morgan argued the planters made a cold political calculation: a workforce divided by race could never unite the way Bacon's rebels had. Racism as the tool, slavery as the machine. Other historians say the racism was already there, that the prejudice made the enslavement possible, not the other way around.

Maya: So which caused which?

Marcus: Nobody's fully settled it. The codes are the fact. The motive is the argument. If the essay asks, give both sides. The graders reward that.

Maya: Three boxes in the book. Let's see if they hold.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: indentured servitude. Four to seven years for a boat ticket, the headright paying the planter who paid, not the servant who sailed. The system broke when the servants lived, and wanted land that wasn't there.

Maya: Two: Bacon's Rebellion, 1676. Frontier raids, Berkeley's forts, Bacon's army, the Declaration of the People in July, Jamestown burned in September, and Bacon died of… dysentery? That October?

Marcus: Dysentery. About a month after the burning.

Maya: Right. Then Berkeley hanged twenty-three, London recalled him, and the planters learned their lesson: never again a united poor. Box two, checked. Three: the slave codes. 1640's warning, 1662's mother rule, Barbados 1661 as the template, all of it gathered into Virginia's 1705 code. Slavery for life, hereditary, people as property. So race caused the codes —

Marcus: Flip it. Morgan's point: the codes helped make race.

Maya: The codes are the fact; the motive is the argument. Box three, checked. Three for three.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it.

Maya: One. Berkeley's own report to London, 1676: he governed "a people where six parts of seven at least are poor, indebted, discontented and armed." A historian pairs that line with the burning of Jamestown. What's the argument?

[20-second pause]

Maya: Berkeley feared the united, armed poor: six of seven poor and armed, and the rebellion proved it, with servants and freedmen and enslaved Black people fighting together. The giveaway: the fear was class unity, not race. The planters spent the next thirty years engineering it away.

Maya: Two. After 1676, Virginia's planters pivoted from servants to enslaved Africans. Give both reasons.

[15-second pause]

Maya: Politics and price: the rebellion showed landowners that landless young white men were dangerous, and the Dutch trade monopoly broke in 1682, so enslaved Africans got cheaper. A workforce for life, divided by race, solved both problems at once.

Maya: Three. The exam hands you a line from the 1705 code: "All Negro, mulatto and Indian slaves within this dominion… shall be held to be real estate." What changed between 1640 and 1705?

[20-second pause]

Maya: Custom hardened into law: in 1640, one court gave John Punch a life sentence while his white partners got four more years. By 1705, the assembly wrote it down for everyone: people as property, for life, hereditary. One-off punishments became a system.

Maya: One more, fast. The slave code Virginia copied: which island, which year?

[5-second pause]

Maya: Barbados, 1661.

Maya: On the long essay: this whole episode is a change-over-time machine. Name what changed: servants to enslaved Africans. Name what stayed: tobacco's hunger for land and labor. Check your three boxes. Next time: the colonies sort into regions. New England towns, the middle colonies, and the plantation South.

Marcus: One rebellion showed the planters the poor could fight together —

Maya: so the codes made sure they'd never fight together again.
