# Pronunciation: LEH-nee leh-NAH-pay (Lenni Lenape), shack-uh-MAX-un (Shackamaxon), PAH-luh-tyne (Palatine), doytch (Deutsch), sus-kwuh-HA-nuh (Susquehanna), UL-ster (Ulster), "sour" (Saur)

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: covenant towns, banished dissenters, King Philip's War. New England, held together by fear of the wrong belief. This time: the colony that ran the opposite experiment. One colony advertised in German. Its founder had been jailed in England for his religion. And by the Revolution, arguably the most diverse colony in British North America. Three boxes: the Holy Experiment, the Pennsylvania Dutch, and the limits of tolerance. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Twelve minutes, and you'll land all three.

Marcus: Start with the man. William Penn, the admiral's son. He became a Quaker around twenty-two, in an England that jailed Quakers for it. The Tower of London. And the crown owed his family money. In 1681 Charles the Second settled it with land: twenty-five thousand square miles west of the Delaware.

Maya: A colony for a debt. Some way to collect.

Marcus: The king named it Pennsylvania, for Penn's father, the admiral. Penn was embarrassed by it. And Penn called the whole project his Holy Experiment: a colony built on Quaker peace and equality, where nobody got jailed for their religion.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Quakers were the ones who wouldn't bow to anybody, right?

Marcus: [measured] Wouldn't bow, wouldn't doff the hat to their betters, wouldn't swear oaths. In a world of titles and curtsies, they shook hands. Radical manners, radical politics. In 1682 Penn wrote the Frame of Government: an elected assembly, trial by jury, and liberty of conscience for anyone who acknowledged one Almighty God.

Maya: So not everyone, exactly. One God gets you in the door.

Marcus: One God. The fine print matters. But students miss the second half: the liberty was also a business plan. Empty land pays nothing. Penn needed tens of thousands of buyers.

Maya: Skip the theology. Give me the sales pitch.

Marcus: In 1681 Penn published a pamphlet advertising Pennsylvania across Europe: cheap land, religious freedom. The pamphlet went out in German as well as English, and the land started selling. Penn's own recruitment drive, and it made Pennsylvania one of the fastest-growing colonies in English America.

Maya: A colony founded by advertising. I once drove forty minutes to a garage sale because of a flyer with clip art. Same energy, smaller stakes.

Marcus: [measured] The middle colonies are four: New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Delaware. New York you know: the Dutch built it, England seized it in 1664 and renamed it for the Duke of York. New Jersey: the Duke's friends got it, sold it to Quaker investors, split it east and west. Delaware: Penn's lower counties on the bay. They got their own assembly in 1704 and drifted off into their own colony.

Marcus: [measured] The famous scene. Penn bought land from the Lenape instead of seizing it, and he met their leaders under an elm at Shackamaxon. Tradition says, anyway. The paintings came ninety years later. I don't think anyone's sure the meeting happened the way the paintings show.

Maya: The Lenni Lenape: the name means something like "original people."

Marcus: Something like that. Now, exam tip for box one: watch the dates. The charter is 1681. The Frame of Government is 1682. Philadelphia was laid out in 1682 too. Students swap them, and the exam notices.

Maya: So it was all charity, then. Penn's gift to the persecuted.

Marcus: [firm] Not quite. It was a debt settlement and a land business. The liberty was real. It was also the pitch.

Maya: [confident tone] Box one's checked. The Holy Experiment: a debt paid in land, a Frame with an elected assembly. Liberty was the reason to buy.

Maya: [intrigued] But a pamphlet doesn't pay anyone's passage.

Marcus: Checked. Your turn. Penn's pamphlets are circulating in the Rhineland. German families are reading them. Who can actually afford the crossing, and what does that decide about who shows up?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The rich ones come free. Everyone else comes in debt.

Marcus: Box two: the Pennsylvania Dutch. First, the name. Not Holland. Deutsch. German. Two years of German class, finally paying rent.

Marcus: The Palatines, from the Rhineland, fled wars and bad harvests and came in whole families. By the Revolution, roughly a third of Pennsylvania was German. And something like half of them arrived as redemptioners.

Maya: Redemptioners. Box-two word, right there.

Marcus: [measured] The mechanism: a sea captain carried you across, and you promised to pay once you landed, either by borrowing from relatives already there or by contracting years of your labor. Indentured servants negotiated their terms back in Europe. Redemptioners negotiated on the dock, sick and broke, with the ship behind them. Guess who got the worse deal.

Marcus: Franklin's 1751 essay fretted that the Germans would Germanize Pennsylvania instead of Pennsylvania Anglicizing them. Think about what that sentence proves: the colony had gotten so German that even Franklin was nervous.

Maya: Lancaster County. The Amish are still there — saw a buggy once and thought I'd time-traveled.

Marcus: Same people, different century. By the late 1730s the Germans had their own press — Christopher Saur's German-language paper out of Germantown. An entire press in their own language. Then the Scots-Irish: Ulster Presbyterians, squeezed by high rents and religious pressure at home. James Logan, Penn's agent, reckoned something like six thousand landed in a single year in the late 1720s, most of them Scots-Irish. The good land near Philadelphia was taken, so they went west, up the Susquehanna, onto the frontier. By the Revolution, something like a fourth of Pennsylvania.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And the Quakers were fine with that? Pacifists putting fighters on the border?

Marcus: Logan encouraged it. The frontier needed bodies, and the Scots-Irish knew how to fight. Plus the earlier arrivals were already there: Dutch, Swedes, Welsh Quakers.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Nobody up here is growing tobacco or sugar. So what were all these people actually growing?

Marcus: [measured] Wheat and corn. The Middle Colonies were the breadbasket: grain instead of tobacco, small farms and family labor instead of plantations, towns full of millers and merchants. Pennsylvania flour sailed south to feed the Caribbean sugar islands, which grew too little food of their own, and across the Atlantic too. One immigrant's letter called Pennsylvania the best poor man's country.

Maya: Best poor man's country — if you survived the crossing to claim it.

Marcus: Exam tip for box two, and it's a classic: Pennsylvania Dutch doesn't mean Holland. Deutsch. The exam will hand you the word "Dutch" and wait.

Maya: Right, so redemptioners were basically the German indentured servants, same deal.

Marcus: [firm] Common mix-up. Not the same deal. One negotiated in Europe, the other on the dock. The exam knows the difference.

Maya: That's the Pennsylvania Dutch: Germans, not Dutch, roughly a third of the colony, half of them in debt for the crossing, and wheat where the Chesapeake grew tobacco.

Maya: [intrigued] But what happens to the experiment when Penn's gone?

Marcus: Penn died in 1718, back in England. He hadn't set foot in the colony since 1701. Your turn. His sons hold a deed, unsigned, for as much land as a man can walk in a day and a half. The Lenape expect a normal walk, maybe forty miles. The colony is hungry for land. What do the sons do with it?

[10-second pause]

Maya: They don't walk it. They run it.

Marcus: [measured] Box three: the limits of tolerance. 1737. Thomas and John Penn, with James Logan, pulled the walk: a cleared path through the woods, the colony's fastest runners. Edward Marshall covered something like sixty-odd miles, and the colony walked away with something like a million acres, roughly the size of Rhode Island. The Lenape were pushed west.

Maya: So the Holy Experiment's peace lasted exactly as long as Penn did.

Marcus: I'd call that the honest version. And Maya, the harder correction: the Quakers weren't the anti-slavery people yet. Not then.

Maya: [incredulous] Wait. Quakers, no slaves. I thought that was the whole brand.

Marcus: [measured] Penn himself held enslaved people. At least a dozen, by the usual count. And in 1688, four German Quakers in Germantown signed the first protest against slavery in the English colonies. It went up through the meetings, and the Yearly Meeting declined to take a stand. The paper sat in the archives for a century and a half.

Maya: The first anti-slavery protest in English America, and the Quakers filed it away.

Marcus: Slavery ran through the whole region. In New York, it was central to the economy, even under the Dutch. Catholics were distrusted — especially when England was at war with France, the usual account goes. By the Revolution, something like six thousand enslaved Black people lived in Pennsylvania.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So was it tolerant, or just crowded?

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] Historians argue that one, and nobody's fully sure on this one. Some say Pennsylvania wasn't tolerant, just pluralist: lots of groups living side by side, each one suspicious of the others. The people were there. They weren't equal.

Maya: And on the test, that's the middle column. New England, Middle, Chesapeake: three different answers to who belongs.

Marcus: One exam tip for box three: if a stimulus hands you Franklin's "Germanize us" line, the answer is about anxiety over numbers. Read what he's afraid of.

Maya: So Pennsylvania had no slavery. A Quaker colony, case closed.

Marcus: [firm] Not exactly. Penn held people himself. Thousands were enslaved there.

Maya: Checking the last one. And the limits: the Walking Purchase, 1737, the deed Penn's sons turned into a footrace. Enslaved people in a Quaker colony. The 1688 protest that went nowhere. Plenty of peoples, unequal footing.

Maya: [sheepish] Did it stick? Three boxes, let's land them. One: the Holy Experiment. Charter 1681, Frame 1682. An elected assembly, liberty of conscience, and liberty as the sales pitch — because acres without settlers earn nothing. Two: the Pennsylvania Dutch. The German third of the colony, many of them redemptioners, a German-language press by the late 1730s, the Scots-Irish on the frontier, wheat instead of tobacco. Three: the limits. The peace died with Penn in... 1718? And the Walk came in 1737?

Marcus: 1718, and 1737. Nineteen years after.

Maya: Right. Pluralist, not necessarily tolerant. All three landed.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it.

Maya: One. A 1751 essay warns: "Why should Pennsylvania, founded by the English, become a Colony of Aliens, who will shortly be so numerous as to Germanize us instead of our Anglifying them?" What's the point of this source?

[10-second pause]

Maya: By 1751, German immigration had grown so large it alarmed even a Pennsylvanian — "so numerous as to Germanize us." The fear of the numbers proves the scale: the Germans weren't a footnote; they were roughly a third of the colony.

Maya: Two. Penn advertised religious liberty across Europe in 1681. Was the liberty the point, or the pitch?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The pitch paid for the point — the pamphlet went out in German, and the land started selling, making Pennsylvania one of the fastest-growing colonies. The colony needed paying settlers; liberty recruited the buyers the business needed.

Maya: Three. A historian writes that Pennsylvania was pluralist rather than tolerant. Using one limit from this episode, explain what she means.

[10-second pause]

Maya: Many peoples, one hierarchy: pick yours. The Walking Purchase cheated the Lenape out of a Rhode Island-sized piece of land. The 1688 anti-slavery protest was filed away. The founder himself held enslaved people. The groups were present; they weren't equal, and that gap is the difference between pluralism and tolerance.

Maya: One more, fast: Penn's liberty was also a business plan — in one phrase, why did he need the buyers?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The colony was a debt repayment — and paper promises don't sell themselves. He had to sell land to make the debt pay.

Maya: [firm] On the long essay: this episode is your comparison paragraph. Three regions, three answers to who belongs. New England's covenant, Pennsylvania's crowded marketplace, the Chesapeake's slave codes. Name all three.

Maya: [intrigued] Check your three boxes. Next time: the empire's money theory. Mercantilism, the Navigation Acts, and the great colonial dodge.

Marcus: [professional broadcast tone] Pennsylvania proved a colony could hold every faith —

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] and still not hold them equally.
