Marcus: May 1754. The Ohio Valley, before dawn, in the rain. A 22-year-old Virginia militia officer named George Washington has his men surround a French camp in a rocky hollow called Jumonville Glen. The order comes. Muskets fire in the dark. When it's over, the French commander, the Sieur de Jumonville, is dead. And some accounts say he carried a diplomatic message, not attack orders.

Marcus: Washington has just started a world war. He still doesn't know that yet.

Marcus: And he didn't fire alone. Beside him stood Tanacharison, a Seneca leader the English called the Half-King, with his warriors. Some accounts say Tanacharison sank his tomahawk into Jumonville's head and washed his hands in the Frenchman's brains, sealing the alliance in blood. Washington watched his Native ally turn a skirmish into a war neither empire could walk away from.

Marcus: Here's the thesis for the next eight minutes. Britain is about to win the biggest war of the century, take a continent, and go broke doing it. Victory and debt. That double legacy breaks the empire, and every grievance of the Revolution starts here.

Marcus: The setup first. Two empires want the Ohio country. Virginia's governor claims it. The French are already building forts on it. Washington marches in with a few hundred men to push them out, and walks into a diplomatic incident with gunpowder.

Marcus: Five weeks after the Glen, the French come looking for him. Washington throws up a stockade in a meadow. Fort Necessity, the name tells you everything. He surrenders it in early July. The surrender terms are in French, and they use the word l'assassinat. Assassination. Washington signs. He says later he hadn't understood the translation.

Marcus: He signed a confession he couldn't read. The French take it home and call it murder. London and Paris are already circling each other, and this skirmish in a rainy hollow lights the fuse.

Marcus: A year later, Britain sends a real army. General Edward Braddock lands with redcoats trained for European battlefields: march in formation, fire in volleys, hold the line. In July 1755 he marches that army through the Pennsylvania woods toward the French stronghold at Fort Duquesne.

Marcus: Near the Monongahela River, French and Native fighters hit the column from the tree line. The formation means nothing in the woods. The column collapses. Braddock is mortally wounded. His men break and run.

Marcus: Washington rides out alive. He writes home with the kind of line you don't forget: "four bullets through my coat, and two horses shot under me." Four bullets through the coat. Two horses dead beneath him. He walks away.

Marcus: And the war goes everywhere. Not just the Ohio country. Europe, India, the Caribbean, even Manila in the Philippines. Some historians call this the first world war, and they're not being cute about it. Armies and navies on four continents, all fighting over who gets to own North America.

Marcus: In India, Robert Clive wins at Plassey in 1757, and the East India Company starts turning from a trading firm into an empire. In the Caribbean, Britain seizes Havana in 1762, the richest sugar port in the Americas. In Europe, Frederick the Great's Prussia survives against France, Austria, and Russia combined, paid for with British gold.

Marcus: And through it all, the Native nations aren't scenery. They're players. The Iroquois Confederacy had spent decades playing France against Britain, selling their neutrality to the highest bidder. The Ohio peoples, the Shawnee, the Delaware, the Miami, chose sides for their own reasons: which empire would leave them their land. Everybody in this war is using everybody else.

Marcus: Meanwhile the colonies try the obvious thing, once. June 1754, Albany: seven colonies send delegates to coordinate their defense, and Benjamin Franklin puts a plan on the table. One united colonial government. One army, one treasury, one council. The Albany Plan of Union.

Marcus: Every assembly says no. London says no too, but the colonies say it first. Nobody wants to pay for it, and nobody wants to give up power. The moment they most need to act together, they can't. Remember that. It matters later.

Marcus: The war goes badly for Britain for three years. Then 1757: William Pitt the Elder takes charge and flips the board. His line, or the line attributed to him: "I will conquer America in Germany." The wording is debated. The strategy isn't.

Marcus: Pitt pays Prussia, subsidies to Frederick the Great, to keep France bleeding in Europe, and pours Britain's money and men into North America. Hold France with Prussian bayonets that Britain paid for. Win across the Atlantic.

Marcus: The payoff comes September 13th, 1759, before dawn. Wolfe had spent the whole summer sick in his tent, running a siege that went nowhere, writing that he'd rather die than fail. So he gambled everything on one night: General James Wolfe lands his men below Quebec, scales the cliffs in the dark, and lines up on the Plains of Abraham. The French commander, Montcalm, marches out to meet him instead of waiting behind the walls.

Marcus: The battle that decides North America lasts about fifteen minutes. By the end of it both commanders are dying: Wolfe on the field, Montcalm by the next morning. Quebec falls. Montreal goes the year after. New France is finished.

Marcus: Fifteen minutes to end an empire. February 1763, the Treaty of Paris makes it official. France gives up Canada and nearly everything east of the Mississippi. Spain gives up Florida. Britain's North American empire roughly doubles in a day.

Marcus: Notice what France kept: the sugar islands. Guadeloupe, Martinique. To the merchants in Paris, Caribbean sugar was worth more than all of Canada. There's a line attributed to Voltaire, that France traded away a few acres of snow. Whether he said it or not, plenty of Frenchmen agreed with him.

Marcus: And one secret deal: France had already handed Louisiana to Spain in 1762, compensation for Spain's losses. So Spain loses Florida to Britain, gains Louisiana from France, and the map of North America gets redrawn in back rooms.

Marcus: And the bill roughly doubles too. The war piles something like sixty million pounds onto what Britain already owed, and the national debt lands near 130 million. London now owns a continent it cannot afford.

Marcus: But the treaty forgot the people who actually lived there. Spring 1763, the French barely gone, and the Ohio country explodes. An Ottawa leader named Pontiac organizes a rising: first the siege of Detroit, then British forts falling across the Great Lakes and the Ohio Valley.

Marcus: Here's why. The French had been trade partners: gifts, diplomacy, marriages, a whole system of alliance. The British ended the gift diplomacy, raised prices, and treated the Ohio peoples as a defeated enemy. They weren't defeated. Pontiac's Rebellion burns through 1763, and London panics.

Marcus: The panic has a name. October 1763: the Proclamation Line. No colonial settlement west of the Appalachians. London draws a line on the map to stop the fighting and, just as much, to stop paying for frontier wars. The colonists who just fought for that land are furious. The land speculators most of all.

Marcus: Including Washington. The surveyor turned speculator holds claims all over the Ohio country, and the Proclamation freezes them. The kid who started the war can't cash in on winning it. One more crack between London and the colonies.

Marcus: One more legacy, and it's human. The war put British regulars and colonial militias in the same camps, and they despised each other. British officers called the provincials cowards and wouldn't give them real commissions. The colonials watched redcoats lose battles in woods the militias knew by heart. Both sides walked away from the war respecting each other less than when it started. That contempt ferments for a full decade, and then it explodes.

Marcus: So the double legacy. Victory: France out, Spain pushed back, Britain owns everything east of the Mississippi. Debt: 130 million pounds, an empire twice the size, and no plan to pay for it except the colonies that just got rich.

Marcus: And the historians' question, the one that changes whose story this is. Was it a European war that happened to be fought in America, or an American war that went global? The answer you pick decides whether this is Britain's triumph or America's origin story. I'd say it was both, and let the historians fight.

Marcus: The one thing to carry forward. A century of growth made the colonies valuable, diverse, and self-governing. This war made them expensive. And when London looks at the bill, it doesn't see a partner that just bled with Britain. It sees a treasury to tap. Next: Grenville does the math, and the long nap ends with a bill.
