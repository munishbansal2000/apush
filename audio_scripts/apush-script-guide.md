# Maya + Marcus Script Guide (APUSH audio)

How E1 got here: v5 was rejected by a high schooler as "ai slop… can't compete
with heimler." Eleven versions later, v11 works. Every rule below was paid for
with a specific mistake from that rewrite. Follow this and the next episode
starts where v11 ended, not where v5 did.

## The format

Maya (host, student-surrogate) + Marcus (expert). ~8 minutes, hard cap 180 WPM,
target 1150–1350 words (under ~1150 reads thin for the slot). Maya tracks
N boxes on her episode sheet; each box gets checked off **in her voice** as
the episode earns it. Box picks: named terms a student could plausibly see as
an MCQ stem or SAQ prompt, spread across the episode's topics. Default 3
boxes; boxes ≤ topics — fold extra topics under a box, never leave a topic
unboxed. A box owns its sub-topics in the recap: if headright lives under
the tobacco box, the recap says so. Cold open sells the episode in 30 seconds. Topics play as
conversation, never lecture. Close = recap (Maya drives, fumbles
welcome) → self-test (question, `[3-second pause]`, Maya gives the model answer
after the pause) → one shared tagline, once.

## Voice

**Maya**
- Student-surrogate, not a cue machine. No more than ~60% of her lines end in `?`.
- Gets things messily wrong: confident, specific, human. Not textbook-perfect
  misconceptions every time; sometimes she says something that goes nowhere.
  Two different tools: the mid-episode wrong beat (a wrong guess or shaky
  claim Marcus corrects in flow, e.g. "a hundred guys who'd never held a
  plow") and the recap fumble (memory-check or content-fumble while landing
  the boxes). Use both, not two of one.
- Has irrelevant human moments (got lost in St. Louis once; gets queasy on the
  ferry to Catalina). Calibration: a concrete personal image that colors her
  reaction to the topic — not a random non sequitur, not a second anecdote
  competing with the first. One per episode is plenty.
- Knows something Marcus doesn't at least once per episode, OR asks something
  he can't fully answer ("Nobody's fully sure"). The most natural source is
  pop culture / student-world knowledge (pirate treasure, fourth-grade eye
  patches) — not textbook facts she mysteriously memorized.
- Says the checkoffs herself ("Checking that one.").
- Never echoes Marcus's point back at him. New thought or silence. Exception:
  a shared single-phrase beat (both voices landing the same 1–3 words, e.g.
  "Supposed to." / "Supposed to.") reads as a duet, not an echo — allowed
  once per episode max.

**Marcus**
- Expert, teacherly, crisp. Explains; doesn't lecture. No monologue runs past
  ~120 words without Maya breaking in.
- Reactions vary every time. Never "Great question!" twice.
- Admits uncertainty ("I don't think that's settled").
- Never announces lists ("Three examples. One… Two… Three…").

## The banned list (mechanical — the gates enforce these)

1. Sentence starters `That's` / `Here's` / `Here is`: max 2 per episode.
   The tic is the habit, not the word — one or two is human, three-plus
   is the AI rhythm.
2. Spoken curriculum labels: "Objective one," "LO1," "causal chain,"
   "historiography," "learning objective." Objectives are tracked silently; the
   episode-sheet boxes are the student-facing version. Plain-language
   equivalents ("cause and effect," "the chain from X to Y") are fine — the
   ban is on the jargon term, not the idea.
3. Exam-pitch clichés: "the exam counts/loves…". One exam mention per episode
   max, varied wording. The budget covers *pitches*, not the ritual
   self-test framing ("Two questions, AP-shaped") or a genuine student
   question ("So for the test — 1588, why does it matter?").
4. Balanced triples and parallel closers ("Different X, different Y, different
   Z"). One earned chain per episode max, and it must carry real logic
   (necessity, not decoration).
5. Feed lines that exist only to trigger Marcus's next paragraph ("Prove it.
   Walk me through them one by one." → "Okay, so how does that actually
   break down?").
6. Verbatim repeated taglines within an episode. Vary it or split it between
   the two voices. Cross-episode repetition is governed by Series grammar
   below, not by this rule.
7. Button-word loops ("Check it." ×3).
8. Aphoristic closers ("That's your whole comparison answer.").
9. Modern analogies (genetic engineering, highways).
10. Standalone micro-turns (a whole turn under 5 words): max 8 per episode.
    Checkoffs, recap labels, and the tagline landing count toward this and
    routinely sum to ~7. Mid-turn fragments ("Slowly. But it held for
    centuries.") — max 2–3.
11. Uncontracted stiffness (it is, that is, do not) — TTS reads it as a robot.
    Emphatic negation (were not, was not) is exempt. W1 never applies
    inside quotation marks — historical quotes stay verbatim ("he that
    will not work, shall not eat").
12. Twist pivots: "there's a twist," "then a twist," "but here's the twist."
    Same tic family as the banned starters — hard gate G10, no exceptions.
13. Em dash = a beat/pause in TTS: safe for interruptions ("Wait — pieces
    of eight?") and mid-sentence asides. For the closing tagline's dash,
    add a production note (held breath — "do not rush it"), because the
    pause carries the landing. Hard gate G8: max 10 in dialogue. If nearly
    every paragraph has one, cut two-thirds.
14. "Not X, just Y" antitheses ("Exaggerated, not invented"): max 2 per
    episode, hard gate G9. They read as punchlines; three-plus is a routine.
15. Stock transitions ("Which brings us to," "Enter X," "On this much,
    historians agree"): retire after one use across the series. The
    fully-retired list ("that's the last of it," "here's the nuance,"
    "now the box that matters most," "that's what makes it tricky," twist
    pivots) is hard gate G10 — any occurrence fails.

## Episode structure

1. Cold open (Maya): stakes + boxes, ≤30 sec. Shape: one-line continuity nod
   ("Last time: …") + collision/stakes line + the boxes + "circle the ones
   you couldn't explain" + time promise. Callback density: era + key names
   ("Drake and Roanoke and the Armada"), not a plot summary. The cold open's promises must be
   delivered by the episode (if you say "gold, God, and glory," all three
   get a beat).
2. Topics (Marcus leads, Maya interrupts): each topic earns one box.
3. Transition tease: one line, Maya's. Never say goodbye before the recap.
   The forward tease belongs to the closer, not the transition — one "next
   time" per episode.
4. Recap (Maya drives; Marcus corrects; fumbles stay in). Ritual opener:
   "Three boxes, let's land them." — keep the ritual, vary the tissue
   around it. Two welcome fumble kinds: the memory-check ("the crown
   took... twenty percent?") and the content-fumble ("Stuff goes both
   ways, except the bad stuff mostly goes one way?"). Marcus corrects
   crisply; never lectures the correction.
5. Self-test: question → `[5-second pause]` → Maya's model answer. Ritual
   framing ("Two questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it.")
   stays consistent across episodes. Make at least one question
   stimulus-style (a source description → "what's the point of this
   source?"), not just recall of what Marcus said 30 seconds earlier.
   Test the boxes, not the trivia.
6. Closer: the tagline, shared or varied, in new words every episode.
   Exactly once.

## Series grammar (cross-episode repetition)

Repetition across episodes is intentional when it's ritual, accidental when
it's tissue:
- RITUAL (keep consistent): the cold-open box circle ("Circle the ones you
  couldn't explain right now"), the recap opener ("Three boxes, let's
  land them."), the self-test framing. These are the show's grammar —
  students should feel the shape repeat.
- TISSUE (vary every episode): the tagline closer, transition lines,
  topic-level language, Maya's asides. Never let two episodes share a full
  sentence outside the ritual lines. (E1 and E2 both wrote "That's the last
  of it" independently — that sentence now belongs to neither; find a new
  transition each time.)
- CHECK LAYERS (don't double-count): the recap ("Three boxes, let's land
  them") is the check layer, plus the closing "Check your three boxes."
  Mid-episode check-ins are optional — one "one down" beat max. Never check
  every box mid-episode AND run a full recap.
- Continuity: the cold open's "Last time:" line is the only required
  callback. Deeper callbacks (a Plains-horses nod, a tagline echo) are
  welcome but must earn their place — one per episode max.

## Fact standards (AP audience = fail-closed)

- Population/date claims: ranges, not pins ("Around 1100," "10,000 to 20,000,"
  "rivaled or exceeded").
- Causal claims: necessity vs sufficiency. "No maize, no Cahokia" (necessity)
  is fine; "The whole thing ran on corn" (sufficiency) gets softened to
  "Maize made the scale possible."
- "Most of" beats "all" ("while most of Europe was still run by kings").
- Debated claims get attributed in-dialogue ("Some historians think so.
  Others say it's overstated.").
- Don't invent second-order detail (French explorers "saw the mounds and
  recorded them" — not what they asked the locals).
- Voice the hedge on a ladder, matched to the uncertainty: "about" (mild) →
  "roughly" → "something like" → "the leading theory" / "nobody's fully
  sure" (strongest). Don't pin what sources don't pin.
- Every draft gets a dedicated fact pass BEFORE the read-aloud pass, checked
  against sources, not memory.

## Process (script-first, always)

1. Write draft.
2. Run `apush-script-gates.py` — fix every FAIL.
3. Read every WARN; keep or fix with intent.
4. Human read-aloud (or TTS skim) for rhythm.
5. User approves the script.
6. Only then render. **Never render an unapproved script.**

## Production (TTS)

- Strip before TTS: the `#` header, the read note, and every `[N-second
  pause]` tag. Convert pause tags to real silence — raw bracket text will
  be read aloud if it reaches the voice.
- Pronunciation: put a `# Pronunciation:` line in the header with
  phonetic spellings for every risky name/term (Potosí, de Bry, mita…).
  Test the riskiest three in the voice before full render.
- Length: time a test render. ~1150–1350 words at ≤180 WPM plus pauses
  should land ≈8–8.5 min; if it doesn't, the word count is wrong, not
  the estimate.
- School-safe vocabulary (hard gate G11): no profanity in dialogue, however
  historical the nickname. Find the polite translation and wink at it
  ("fire-pooper," and yes, that's the polite translation).

## What the gates can't catch

- Whether Maya feels like a person (read it aloud).
- Whether the facts are right (separate pass, every time).
- Whether it's actually fun (the high-schooler test: one "ai slop" = fail).
