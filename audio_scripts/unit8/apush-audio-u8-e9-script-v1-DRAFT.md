# APUSH Unit 8, Episode 9: Vietnam
# Script v1 DRAFT — Debate (Maya moderates, Marcus + guest)
# Read note: Debate format. Maya frames the question and moderates; Marcus argues the hawk case exactly as it was made at the time; Linh, a Vietnamese-American voice, gives the war as it looked from the other side. Guest dignity rule: Linh appears in this debate only — a serious, knowledgeable voice, never a caricature. Closer is Maya's verdict beat plus the next-episode tease (no boxes in debates). [5-second pause] marks are production notes for real silence — they never go to the voice. Strip this header, the read note, and all pause tags before TTS; convert pause tags to silence. Intended duration ~8 min at ≤180 WPM.
# Pronunciation: Ngo Dinh Diem (noh din zee-EM), Ho Chi Minh (hoh chee MIN), Viet Cong (vee-et KONG), Tet, Saigon (sy-GON), Dien Bien Phu (dyen byen FOO), Cronkite (KRON-kite)

Maya: Last time: the Great Society, what it built and what buried it. This time: the thing that buried it. The question: was Vietnam a tragic necessity or a catastrophic mistake? Two cases, full strength. Marcus makes the hawk case the way it was actually made in the sixties — containment, credibility, the dominoes. Linh's family lived the war from the Vietnamese side, and she'll tell you what it looked like from a country that had already been at war for a decade before America arrived. About eight hard minutes. Nobody gets a caricature here; everybody gets their best argument.

Marcus: Opening statement. The hawk case, as made. After Korea, American policymakers believed communism was a single global tide, and Vietnam was where the tide would be stopped. Eisenhower's domino theory: if Vietnam falls, the rest of Southeast Asia follows, country by country. Then credibility: America had promised to defend free peoples everywhere — the Truman Doctrine's blank check. If Washington lets an ally fall, every ally on earth recalculates. So the logic ran: fight them in Vietnam, or fight them later somewhere worse. Containment had worked in Korea and Berlin. Vietnam was supposed to be the next proof. So they argued. Sincere, bipartisan, and wrong about almost everything downstream.

Linh: Opening statement. Before it was America's war, it was our war. Vietnam fought the French for independence for nearly a decade and beat them at Dien Bien Phu in nineteen fifty-four after a fifty-six-day siege. The Geneva agreement split the country at the seventeenth parallel and promised elections to reunify it. The elections never happened. The American-backed leader in the South refused them, because Ho Chi Minh would have won. So the war for independence kept going, except now the foreign soldiers were American. From a Vietnamese village, the question wasn't dominoes or credibility. It was: how many more decades do we bury our children? America lost fifty-eight thousand. Vietnam lost millions. Remember the ratio when you judge the decisions.

Maya: First clash: the slide. How does a country go from advisors to half a million troops? Marcus, walk the chain.

Marcus: Five presidents, one trap, each inheriting the last one's commitments. The 'who lost China' politics of the fifties made quitting unthinkable for all of them. Truman funds the French. Eisenhower sends the first advisors and gives the domino speech. Kennedy raises it to sixteen thousand advisors and backs the coup that kills South Vietnam's president Diem in November of nineteen sixty-three. Then Johnson.

Maya: And Johnson turns it into a real war. Huh.

Marcus: August nineteen sixty-four: the Gulf of Tonkin. North Vietnamese boats reportedly attack American destroyers — the second attack almost certainly never happened — but Johnson gets the Tonkin Resolution, a near-unanimous blank check from Congress, and starts the bombing. By the end of nineteen sixty-five there are nearly two hundred thousand Americans in Vietnam. By nineteen sixty-eight, over half a million. Nobody voted for a war. They voted for resolutions, and resolutions became a war.

Linh: Each escalation had a Vietnamese logic nobody in Washington heard. General Westmoreland's strategy was attrition: kill more of them than they can replace. It assumed the enemy would run out. The enemy was the population. The Viet Cong weren't an invading army; they were southerners, living in the villages, supplied down the Ho Chi Minh Trail. American strategy — search and destroy, body counts as the scoreboard — meant the war was fought in peasant hamlets with napalm and defoliants. From the ground: a foreign army burning villages to save them from an enemy that lived in the villages. Every escalation recruited for the other side. The Americans measured progress in bodies. The Vietnamese measured it in endurance. Endurance won.

Maya: Second clash: Tet, and nineteen sixty-eight. Marcus, the offensive America won and lost at the same time.

Marcus: January nineteen sixty-eight. The North launches the Tet Offensive: surprise attacks on a hundred cities at once, including the American embassy in Saigon, where commandos breached the walls. Militarily, it's a disaster for the attackers — the Viet Cong are essentially destroyed as a fighting force. But politically, it's an earthquake. For years the generals had promised light at the end of the tunnel. Tet proves the enemy can strike anywhere, anytime. A month later Walter Cronkite, America's most trusted newsman, looks into the camera and says the war is mired in stalemate. Johnson reportedly says: if I've lost Cronkite, I've lost middle America. In March, Johnson announces he won't run again. Tet was the moment the living-room war broke the presidency.

Linh: The living-room war. The phrase still fits, because Vietnam was the first war on television, beamed into dinner hours. Americans saw the embassy under attack and stopped believing the briefings. But notice what Tet means from the Vietnamese side: the North sacrificed an entire generation of its southern fighters for a political effect in America. It worked. And that tells you the war's real battlefield was never the jungle. It was American public opinion. The Vietnamese leadership understood that before Washington did.

Maya: And the credibility gap. The phrase itself comes from this war. What broke?

Marcus: The gap between what presidents said and what the cameras showed. Johnson promised progress; television showed Tet. Nixon promised peace with honor, but the Pentagon Papers, leaked in nineteen seventy-one, proved Kennedy, Johnson, and Nixon had all misled the public about how the war was going. By nineteen seventy-one, a majority of Americans called the war a mistake. Then My Lai: American soldiers massacred hundreds of Vietnamese civilians in nineteen sixty-eight, covered it up, and the story broke in sixty-nine. Each revelation widened the gap. By the end, a majority of Americans didn't just oppose the war — they didn't believe their government about anything.

Maya: Third clash: the war comes home. The counterculture, the campuses, the generation gap. Linh, you first: what did Vietnam do to the generation that had to fight it?

Linh: It split the country by age and by draft number. The draft lottery meant an eighteen-year-old could be conscripted to die in a war he couldn't vote against, until the voting age was lowered to eighteen in nineteen seventy-one. The draft fell hardest on the poor and on Black Americans, who died in disproportionate numbers. Muhammad Ali refused induction in nineteen sixty-seven and lost his title for it.

Maya: And then the campuses explode, right?

Linh: Students for a Democratic Society organized the marches. In nineteen seventy, after Nixon expanded into Cambodia, students protested at Kent State, the National Guard opened fire, and four were killed. And underneath the protests, the counterculture: the music, the communes, Woodstock's four hundred thousand. A generation decided its parents' war was immoral and its parents' values were the disease. The generation gap wasn't about hair. It was about Vietnam, always.

Marcus: And the hawks' answer, honestly stated: the protests told Hanoi to hold on. North Vietnam's strategy counted on American division, and they said so openly. Every campus march was beamed back as proof that America would quit. The tragedy has no clean moral: the dissent was sincere and democratic, and it also became the enemy's strategy. Both things were true, and the country tore itself apart over which truth mattered more.

Maya: Closing statements. Linh first.

Linh: Vietnam's lesson, from the side that buried millions: foreign powers keep arriving with theories, dominoes, credibility, containment, and the theories keep missing the country underneath. Vietnam wanted independence, from France, from Japan, from America. It got it, at a price no theory can ever justify. When you debate this war, start with the Vietnamese dead. Everything else is commentary.

Marcus: America's lesson: containment was built for Europe, for lines on maps and armies in the open. In Vietnam it met guerrillas, jungles, and nationalism older than the Cold War, and the doctrine broke. The war cost fifty-eight thousand American lives, over a trillion in today's dollars, and the public's trust for a generation. The hawk case was sincere. Sincerity isn't strategy.

Maya: My verdict. Vietnam structures every American foreign-policy debate after it. Every president since has had to answer the Vietnam question before sending troops anywhere, and the country still fights about what the lesson is. The 'Vietnam syndrome', the reluctance to commit troops, shaped them all. The concrete legacy: the War Powers Resolution of nineteen seventy-three, Congress clawing back the war-making power Vietnam had handed to presidents, requiring consultation within forty-eight hours and withdrawal after sixty days without approval. The deeper legacy: the credibility gap never fully closed. Tet taught Americans to distrust the briefing; Watergate, next episode's story, taught them to distrust the briefer. Next time: a river catches fire, and a movement decides the planet needs a lawyer.
