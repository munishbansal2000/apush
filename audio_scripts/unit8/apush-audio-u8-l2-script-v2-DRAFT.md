# Pronunciation: Gorbachev (GOR-buh-choff), glasnost (GLAHZ-nost), perestroika (pair-ih-STROY-kuh), Yeltsin (YELT-sin), Schabowski (shah-BOF-skee)

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: Reagan's revolution — Reaganomics, the coalition, the verdict. But Reagan also promised to face down the Soviet Union, and he left office with the whole thing cracking. This time: the man who tried to rescue the Soviet Union and ended it instead, the year the map flipped, and the fight over who gets the credit. Three boxes: Gorbachev's reforms, the eighty-nine cascade, the verdict. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. About twelve minutes, pauses and all.

Marcus: [measured] Start with the room Gorbachev walked into in eighty-five. The Soviet economy had been flat for years. The war in Afghanistan was eating men and money. And in eighty-six, the Chernobyl reactor would blow, and the government's first instinct would be to hide it: the classic move of a system that feared its own people more than the fallout.

Maya: My uncle tried to "restructure" his restaurant once. New menu, new staff, same leaking roof. Six months later he owned neither the old place nor the new one. [chuckle] I'm already worried for this guy.

Marcus: You should be. Gorbachev was fifty-four, young for the job, and he diagnosed the disease correctly: the economy was frozen and the truth was illegal. His treatment had two drugs, and the exam wants each one matched to its job.

Maya: Two reforms, and I'm already mixing them up. Which one's which?

Marcus: Glasnost means openness. The premium book calls it the dismantling of the repressive apparatus: censorship loosened, newspapers could print what was actually happening, critics could speak. Perestroika means restructuring: market elements slipped into the planned economy, managers got real choices, some private business allowed.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So one opens the mouth, the other opens the wallet?

Marcus: The pairing that ended an empire: without glasnost, no free press. Without a free press, nobody says the shelves are empty. Without that, the system survives.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Your turn, listener. You've just handed your people a free press for the first time in living memory, and the economy is a wreck. What's the first thing that press does with its freedom?

[9-second pause]

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] It reports the wreck. Every failure glasnost was supposed to help fix became a headline, and every headline fed the protests that would tear the system down.

Maya: [incredulous] Wait — so was he secretly anti-communist? Like, he knew this would kill the whole thing and did it anyway?

Marcus: [firm] No, and don't write that on the exam. Gorbachev was a believer trying to save the system. The reforms were meant to make communism work, not end it. He rescued nothing, because the rescue was the thing that killed it.

Maya: [sheepish] I just turned him into a secret saboteur. Classic.

Marcus: Now watch the diplomacy, because Reagan and Gorbachev met four times, and the near-miss matters more than the handshake. First, Geneva, eighty-five. No arms deal. But the two men discovered they could actually talk, and the ice broke.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And the second one is the famous one?

Marcus: [measured] Reykjavik, Iceland, October eighty-six. This is the closest the world has ever come to erasing nuclear weapons. Over two days, the two leaders talked themselves nearly all the way to eliminating their entire nuclear stockpiles by 2000. Then it died on one demand: Gorbachev would only sign if Reagan kept SDI research in the laboratory, with no testing in space. Reagan refused. He walked away from the table over Star Wars.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Your turn. You're Reagan. You're one handshake from deleting every nuke on Earth, and the price is your missile-defense dream. What do you do?

[9-second pause]

Marcus: He keeps the dream. SDI was the one thing Reagan wouldn't trade. And the failure made the next success possible, because Gorbachev came back the following year willing to deal without touching it.

Maya: [incredulous] So close to zero nukes and they walked away over Star Wars? Tell me something actually got signed.

Marcus: [confident tone] The INF Treaty, signed in Washington in December eighty-seven. Both sides agreed to destroy their land-based missiles in the middle range, the ones aimed across Europe, and to let inspectors from the other side watch the destruction happen. Every earlier treaty just capped how many weapons each side could build. This one abolished a whole category.

Maya: Exam tip: match each reform to its job. Glasnost opens the press, perestroika rewires the economy. Swap them and you've answered a question nobody asked.

Marcus: And one mistake to kill for this box: don't write that Gorbachev set out to end communism. He set out to rescue it.

Maya: One box down. Gorbachev's reforms, landing.

Marcus: Box two is eighty-nine, the year the map flipped. Start in Poland, where the Solidarity labor movement forced the Communists from power. Then Hungary, which started opening its border with the West. The key point — and the books are explicit here: it was clear Gorbachev wouldn't send the tanks in. In fifty-six and sixty-eight, Moscow crushed revolts. In eighty-nine, the tanks stayed home, and every regime that had ruled by fear stood alone.

Maya: I know this photo. Two million people holding hands across three whole countries. My history teacher put it up on the smartboard and nobody talked for a minute.

Marcus: [warm tone] The Baltic Way. August twenty-third, eighty-nine. Roughly two million people formed a human chain through Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania, three republics the Soviet Union had swallowed in World War Two. Fifty years to the day after the pact that carved them up. No guns, no barricades. Just hands.

Maya: And then the Wall.

Marcus: [dramatic] November ninth, eighty-nine. And here's the part the textbooks flatten: the Wall didn't fall on orders. It fell on a mistake, live on television.

Maya: [surprised] I know that clip! My teacher played it. Some East German spokesman reading new travel rules on live TV, and he clearly hadn't read them himself. A reporter asks when it starts, and he just says —

Marcus: "Immediately, without delay." The spokesman was Günter Schabowski, handed the new rules minutes before the cameras rolled. West German TV ran with "the border is open," East Berliners flooded the checkpoints, and the guards, with no orders and a crowd they couldn't hold, gave up. By midnight the Wall was a souvenir stand.

Maya: So the most famous night of the decade happened because a guy didn't read his memo?

Marcus: The pressure was volcanic; it would have blown that week or the next. But yes: the spark was a guy who never got the memo. By the end of eighty-nine, every Communist government in Eastern Europe had fallen or transformed.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And the Soviet Union itself?

Marcus: [serious tone] Two years later, it followed. In August of ninety-one, hardliners tried a coup. They locked Gorbachev up at his vacation house in Crimea and rolled tanks into Moscow. And the image the world remembers: Boris Yeltsin, president of the Russian republic, climbing onto a tank in front of the parliament building and facing the coup down. The coup collapsed in three days. Gorbachev came back with nothing left to govern, and that December the Soviet Union dissolved.

Maya: The trap here: don't assume the Wall fell while Reagan was still president. He left office in January of eighty-nine; the Wall opened that November, ten months later, announced by a man reading rules he'd barely seen. And the why-eighty-nine answer is the tanks. Gorbachev kept them home.

Maya: Okay, box three. The fight. Did Reagan's pressure end the Cold War, or was the Soviet Union already rotting from the inside?

Marcus: Teach both, because the books teach both. The premium book's line is the whole debate: yes, the accelerated arms race taxed the Soviet economy more than it taxed America's. But you also have to look at the internal dynamics, the economic stagnation, the political dissatisfaction. And the Princeton book says it: some historians credit the arms race with bankrupting the Soviet Union, others credit Gorbachev.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So which side does the exam want?

Marcus: [firm] The exam wants the debate, not the slogan. Reagan's pressure was real. Reagan's supporters say SDI forced Moscow to spend money it didn't have, chasing a shield it couldn't build. But pressure only works on something already fragile. Without the rot, the arms race is just expensive. Without the arms race, the rot keeps eating the system from the inside — slower, maybe. That's the internal-rot case, and the books give it real weight.

Maya: So the trap: don't write "Reagan won the Cold War" and stop. And the tip —

Marcus: If the SAQ asks what ended it, never hand it one name. Hand it the argument: external pressure on a system that was already failing from within.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: Gorbachev's reforms.

Maya: [sheepish] Glasnost was the economic restructuring, perestroika was the — wait. No. Glasnost is openness, the press thing. Perestroika is restructuring, the economy thing. I had it.

Marcus: You caught it, and that's exactly the one students swap. Glasnost loosens the press, perestroika rewires the economy.

Maya: [confident tone] Two: the eighty-nine cascade. Solidarity forces the Communists out in Poland, Hungary opens its border, the Baltic Way chains roughly two million people across three republics, the Wall opens November ninth by accident on live TV, and after the failed ninety-one coup, the Soviet Union dissolves that December. Checking that one.

Marcus: Landed.

Maya: [confident tone] Three: the verdict. Reagan's pressure, the arms race, SDI, taxed a Soviet economy that was already stagnating, and Gorbachev's own reforms finished the job. Nobody gets the whole credit alone. Checking that one.

Marcus: All three landed.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it.

[5-second pause]

Maya: One. A Soviet cartoon from eighty-seven shows Gorbachev unlocking a filing cabinet labeled "the truth about Chernobyl," while cracks spread through the office wall behind him. What's the cartoonist arguing about glasnost?

[17-second pause]

Maya: The artist's point is that openness was supposed to be a repair job. Unlock one cabinet, fix one disaster, save the system. But the evidence in the drawing is the spreading cracks: once the press could report one failure honestly, every failure followed — empty shelves, dead factories, the whole rot. The rescue became the wrecking ball. Glasnost didn't save Soviet communism; it published the evidence that finished it.

[5-second pause]

Maya: Two. Communist regimes survived uprisings in Hungary in fifty-six and Prague in sixty-eight. An SAQ asks why the regimes fell in eighty-nine instead. What's the one-word answer, and the sentence that earns the point?

[15-second pause]

Maya: The word is Gorbachev, or non-intervention. The sentence: in every earlier crackdown, Soviet tanks did the regime-saving, but in eighty-nine the tanks stayed home, so regimes that had ruled by fear stood alone — and fell.

[5-second pause]

Maya: Three. Two historians answer "what ended the Cold War?" One points to Reagan's military buildup and SDI, the other to Soviet economic stagnation and political rot. The SAQ asks you to take a side. What's the AP answer?

[15-second pause]

Maya: Take both sides, because the books do. The arms race taxed the Soviet economy far more than it taxed America's, so Reagan's pressure was real. But pressure only breaks something already fragile: the stagnation and dissatisfaction inside the system are what made it un-reformable. External pressure on internal rot. The exam rewards the argument, never the slogan.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Check your boxes. The reforms, the cascade, the verdict. Next time: the nineties — globalization, the tech boom, and an economy that goes worldwide.

Marcus: The Cold War didn't end with a battle.

Maya: It ended with an unlocked press, an open border, and a memo nobody read.
