Maya: [professional broadcast tone] You're pumping gas into a car you can barely afford, in a country that can't stop apologizing. Last time: the seventies unraveled, stagflation and the hostage crisis, and the hostages came home the day Reagan took the oath, January twentieth, eighty-one. This time: a former actor promises to make America stop flinching, and the country buys it. Three boxes on your sheet: Reaganomics, the coalition that carried it, the verdict. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. About twelve minutes, and you'll check all three off.

Marcus: Box one starts with a theory that fits on a curve. An economist named Arthur Laffer argued that cutting tax rates could bring in MORE government revenue. Lower rates give people more reason to work and invest, so the economy grows, and a bigger economy means more to tax even at lower rates. Call it supply-side economics: feed the producers, and prosperity works its way down to everyone.

Maya: [incredulous] Wait, "trickle-down." Was that his pitch?

Marcus: [firm] His critics' label. During the nineteen eighty primaries his own rival, George Bush, called it voodoo economics. Reagan called it supply-side. The name tells you whose side you're on.

Maya: [sheepish] Okay. I just quoted his enemies at him.

Marcus: Congress bought the theory. The nineteen eighty-one tax law cut income tax rates by about a quarter, phased in over three years. The top rate fell from about seventy percent to fifty. The theory's promise was that lower rates would bring in MORE revenue through growth. But plenty of Reagan's allies had a second logic: cut the government's paycheck, and the beast has to shrink.

Maya: Your turn, listener. You've cut the government's paycheck. You've also handed the military a much bigger budget. Social Security and Medicare are untouchable. What happens to the deficit?

[10-second pause]

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] It explodes. Cut taxes, raise the military budget, leave the big programs alone, and the deficit has nowhere to go but up. The national debt went from about nine hundred billion in nineteen eighty to over two trillion by eighty-six. Doubled in six years.

Maya: [surprised] So the beast went into debt to cover the difference.

Marcus: Reagan also deregulated: banking, cable television, long-distance phone service. Looser rules for business. And in eighty-one he fired every striking air traffic controller, which told organized labor the old rules were gone. By the mid-eighties inflation and unemployment both fell. The economy grew.

Maya: But somebody paid for the recovery.

Marcus: [serious tone] They did. The cuts helped people who paid a lot of income tax, which meant the wealthy. The poor pay little income tax, so rate cuts meant nothing to them, while housing aid and education money got cut. And the gap between rich and poor widened.

Maya: [confident tone] So the supply-side moral is: the cuts paid for themselves. Famous conservative success story.

Marcus: [firm] That was the theory's promise: lower rates would pay for themselves through growth. The result was a doubled debt, from about nine hundred billion to over two trillion by eighty-six.

Marcus: That's the first box. Supply-side cuts, deregulation, a busted union, falling inflation, and a deficit the theory swore wouldn't happen.

Maya: [playful] My dad says that "government is the problem" line at dinner whenever a bill arrives. I thought he invented it.

Marcus: He borrowed it from Reagan's first inaugural, January twentieth, eighty-one, the same day the hostages came home. Reagan said, "In this present crisis, government is not the solution to our problem, government is the problem."

Maya: So who cheers for that line? The coalition, right? Box two?

Marcus: Box two. The seed was planted in nineteen sixty-four, when Barry Goldwater ran for president on small-government, states'-rights conservatism and lost in a landslide. His campaign proved the audience existed. The movement spent the seventies organizing.

Maya: What's in the coalition by nineteen eighty?

Marcus: The money people came first: pro-business conservatives who wanted lower taxes and deregulation, the Reaganomics crowd. Then the morals people: in seventy-nine the preacher Jerry Falwell founded the Moral Majority, mobilizing evangelical Christians into a new political force. School prayer, abortion, the culture. And then the map: the Sunbelt, California, Texas, Arizona, Nevada, Florida, booming since World War Two and leaning conservative. Plus the think tanks. Groups like the Heritage Foundation, founded in seventy-three, spent the decade turning conservative ideas into ready-made policy, so a Reagan White House could govern from day one.

Maya: And the Reagan Democrats? I hear that phrase everywhere.

Marcus: [serious tone] The most argued-over part of the coalition. Working-class voters, often union members, often Catholic, in the industrial Midwest and Northeast, who'd voted Democratic since Franklin Roosevelt. In eighty and eighty-four a lot of them crossed over. Why? The economy, for one. The values message, for another. And running underneath both, historians are blunt about this, a racial undertow. Nobody agrees on the mix. Some say economics did the work, others say race did. The honest exam answer holds all three at once.

Maya: Which is where the coded-language stuff lives.

Maya: [confident tone] Two coded-language beats, Neshoba and the welfare queen. So the exam move is picking the right reading: coded signal or standard stump line?

Marcus: [measured] Both readings, at once. The exam grades the debate itself. First beat: August of nineteen eighty. Reagan speaks at the Neshoba County Fair in Mississippi and says, "I believe in states' rights." The fairgrounds sit near the county where three civil rights workers were murdered in nineteen sixty-four. Critics heard a signal: states' rights was the old language of resistance to integration. Defenders, including people who worked for Reagan, say it was a standard small-government stump line about returning federal programs to the states, and the fair was just a big crowd in an election year.

Maya: [incredulous] A campaign picks that county by accident?

Marcus: That's the whole fight. Some historians say no campaign picks a stage like that by accident. Others say reading a racial message into a federalism speech proves too much. [beat] The AP answer carries both meanings at once, because both audiences heard the one they wanted.

Maya: And the welfare queen?

Marcus: Same shape. Starting in seventy-six, Reagan told crowds about a Chicago woman gaming the welfare system. He said eighty aliases, a hundred fifty thousand a year in tax-free cash. The real woman existed. She was convicted in seventy-seven of fraud worth about eight thousand dollars. Real story, wildly exaggerated telling. And the same debate: he never named her race, and his defenders call it a story about government waste. His critics say the audience knew exactly who he meant.

Maya: Two boxes down. One to go. And you haven't let me mention the Cold War once.

Marcus: Just one beat here. The end of the Cold War is next lesson's story. This lesson gets the posture. In eighty-three Reagan called the Soviet Union an "evil empire," launched the Strategic Defense Initiative, the missile shield critics nicknamed Star Wars, and poured money into the military to close what he called a window of vulnerability. The rhetoric heated up, and so did the spending and the pressure. Whether that pressure ended the Cold War is a fight historians are still having.

Maya: He's the "tear down this wall" president, though. That clip is everywhere.

Marcus: Eighty-seven, at the Berlin Wall. Park it for now.

Maya: Fine. Box three: the verdict. How big was eighty-four?

Marcus: Enormous. Walter Mondale, Carter's vice president, ran with Congresswoman Geraldine Ferraro, the first woman on a major party's national ticket. Mondale attacked supply-side and said he'd raise taxes. Reagan ran on "morning again in America." He took almost sixty percent of the popular vote and every electoral vote except Minnesota and the District of Columbia. Forty-nine states.

Maya: [confident tone] Verdict box, easy one: he shrank the government. Am I wrong?

Marcus: The paradox is where the lesson lands. What changed: conservatism became the country's default setting. Reagan redefined the terms of political debate. After him, even Democrats argued on his turf about taxes and spending. Anti-government went from a fringe complaint to common sense.

Maya: So the beliefs changed, but the machine itself? Did it actually shrink?

Marcus: [firm] It got redirected. The military grew, the deficit grew, the debt doubled. The gap between rich and poor widened. The AIDS crisis began in eighty-one, and the administration's slow response became its own indictment. Homelessness rose in the cities while social services were cut. The verdict holds both sides: Reagan changed what Americans believed government was for, and the machine itself got redirected.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: Reaganomics.

Marcus: The theory said lower rates would bring in more revenue. It didn't. The eighty-one law cut income taxes about a quarter over three years, and the eighty-six law dropped the top rate to twenty-eight percent. The debt doubled anyway.

Maya: Two: the coalition. Goldwater's sixty-four campaign planted it. The money people, the Moral Majority, the Sunbelt — and the think tanks, right? Heritage was seventy-three?

Marcus: Seventy-three. Ready-made policy, so a Reagan White House could govern from day one.

Maya: Right. The Reagan Democrats crossed over on the economy, the values, and the undertow. Neshoba and the welfare queen are the coded-language beats, and the exam wants the debate.

Marcus: Carry both meanings at once.

Maya: Three: the verdict. Forty-nine states in eighty-four, everybody but Minnesota and D.C., on "morning again in America." He made anti-government the new common sense. But the debt doubled, the gap widened, and the government got redirected. Checking that one.

Marcus: All three landed.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it.

[10-second pause]

Maya: One. A textbook quotes Reagan at the nineteen eighty Neshoba County Fair: "I believe in states' rights." An SAQ asks why historians still argue about the line. What's the debate?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The phrase carried two meanings at once. The fairgrounds sat near the county where three civil rights workers were murdered in sixty-four, and "states' rights" was the old language of resistance to integration, so critics heard a coded racial signal. The other side says the speech was a standard small-government stump line about returning federal programs to the states. The debate is the answer: both audiences heard the meaning they wanted.

[10-second pause]

Maya: Two. Supply-side theory promised the tax cuts would pay for themselves through growth. Why did the deficit explode instead?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Only one side of the ledger moved. The tax cut shrank revenue while the military budget grew and the big programs stayed untouchable. Cut the income, raise the outgo, and the deficit has nowhere to go but up. By eighty-six the national debt had gone from about nine hundred billion to over two trillion.

[10-second pause]

Maya: Three. Working-class voters had backed Democrats since the New Deal. Why did so many cross over to Reagan?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Three forces at once. Stagflation wrecked the Democrats' credibility on the economy. The Moral Majority and the culture fights offered a new political home. And underneath both, a racial undertow historians still argue about. Hold all three, and don't flatten it to one.

[10-second pause]

Maya: One more, fast. True or false: Reagan's tax cuts paid for themselves.

[10-second pause]

Maya: False. That was the promise. The result was a national debt that climbed from about nine hundred billion to over two trillion in six years.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Check your boxes. Reaganomics, the coalition, the verdict. Next time: the Cold War ends, and the fight over whether Reagan's pressure ended it.

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] He didn't shrink the government.

Maya: He just changed what it was for.
