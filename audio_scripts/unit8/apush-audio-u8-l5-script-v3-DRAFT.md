# Pronunciation: subprime (SUB-prime), securitization (seh-kyoor-ih-tih-ZAY-shun), Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP), Glass-Steagall (GLASS STEE-gull), Keynes (KAYNZ), Dodd-Frank

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: 9/11, the wars, the deficit. This time the money breaks. One Monday morning in September 2008, a famous bank dies, and the whole economy seizes up with it. Three boxes: what actually broke, in plain English. What Washington did about it. Why it didn't become another Great Depression. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eleven minutes.

Marcus: [dramatic] September 15, 2008. Lehman Brothers, one of Wall Street's biggest banks, collapses. All weekend, officials had scrambled to find a buyer, and the talks failed. So Washington made a choice — let it fall. Monday morning, the markets went off a cliff. Banks stopped trusting each other. The machine that moves money through the economy started seizing up.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] The machine that moves money: what actually broke inside it?

Marcus: [measured] Box one: the machine. It started with mortgages. In the 2000s, lenders gave home loans to borrowers with shaky credit: subprime mortgages, high interest rates, bad terms. By 2008, almost three in ten mortgages were subprime. Then the lenders sold those loans to Wall Street banks, which bundled thousands of them into securities and sold the bundles to pension funds, foreign banks, anyone with money. The fancy word: securitization.

Maya: [incredulous] Wait, so a risky loan in Nevada ends up inside a German bank's pension fund?

Marcus: [conversational] Exactly what happened. And back in our 1990s episode, the seed was planted: Congress repealed most of Glass-Steagall in 1999, and the bundling business exploded. But the whole thing leaned on one bet: that home prices would keep rising.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Who stamped these bundles "safe"?

Marcus: [firm] The rating agencies: Moody's, Standard and Poor's, Fitch. The banks paid them to rate the bundles, and they stamped mountains of them triple-A, the top safety grade. Pension funds were only allowed to buy safe-rated investments, so those stamps opened the floodgates. The government's later inquiry called the agencies key enablers of the whole meltdown.

Maya: [conversational] So basically people lied on mortgage applications and it all fell apart.

Marcus: [firm] Some fraud happened, but the machine was mostly legal. The real poison was the assumption: "home prices always rise." When prices started falling in 2007, borrowers owed more than their homes were worth. The books call that "underwater," and millions of borrowers were. Defaults spiked. The bundles turned toxic, which is the industry's own word for it.

Maya: [sheepish] Okay, the fraud story is too simple. The bet itself was the machine. Checking box one.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] But why would a lender make a loan they knew was bad?

Marcus: [measured] Because the lender collected the fee and sold the loan the next day. The risk belonged to somebody else. When you never have to live with the loan, you stop checking whether it's good.

Marcus: [measured] Box two: the cascade. One line of context first: the wars and the tax cuts had already pushed the deficit deep into the red, so Washington walked into this crisis broke. In 2007, the cracks showed: one bank had to shut down two hedge funds stuffed with these bundles. Then March 2008: Bear Stearns, another giant, got swallowed by JPMorgan with the Federal Reserve backstopping the deal. That was the warning shot. Washington rescued Bear, and then, in September, let Lehman die.

Maya: [confident tone] I know this part from The Big Short. The guys who bought insurance on bonds they didn't even own.

Marcus: [measured] Credit default swaps. They were insurance policies on mortgage bundles, except you could buy one without owning the bundle, a bet on somebody else's house burning down. The giant insurer AIG had sold billions of these bets. When the bundles died, AIG owed fortunes it didn't have. The day after Lehman fell, the Federal Reserve rescued AIG with an $85 billion loan.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So why rescue Bear in March and let Lehman die in September?

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] Moral hazard. The idea that rescuing risk-takers just teaches them to take bigger risks. Washington wanted to draw a line. The line lasted about twenty-four hours: the next day, the fallout was so bad they rescued AIG. The debate over that weekend never really ended.

Maya: [serious tone] So here's a real decision point. It's that September weekend. Lehman's dying. You run the government. Do you save the bank, or let it fall? Your turn.
[10-second pause]
Maya: They let it fall.

Marcus: [serious tone] And Monday showed why the choice was so heavy. The stock market cratered. Banks stopped lending to each other because nobody knew who was holding the losses. Then Washington moved. Congress passed TARP: seven hundred billion dollars to steady the banks. President Bush signed it in October 2008. It was wildly unpopular, and critics said the money came with no new rules attached. And don't confuse TARP with the stimulus. Different president, different year, different law.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Banks freezing each other out. How does a regular person feel that?

Marcus: [conversational] A small business can't borrow to make payroll. A family can't get a car loan. Credit is the economy's plumbing: when it freezes, every room in the house goes cold.

Maya: [incredulous] Seven hundred billion for the banks. That must have gone over great.

Marcus: [speaking slowly] It didn't. And the Fed went further than it ever had. In December 2008, it cut interest rates to zero (technically zero to a quarter percent), and then started buying bonds by the trillion. Newly created money, cheaper borrowing, desperate medicine.

Maya: [playful] So the Fed just shops.

Marcus: [confident tone] Basically, yes. Buying bonds pushes their prices up and interest rates down across the economy. Then came President Obama's turn: the 2009 stimulus, almost eight hundred billion in spending and tax cuts, straight out of the Keynes playbook. When private spending collapses, the government spends to take up the slack.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Did the stimulus actually work, though?

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] Economists still argue about it. Unemployment was at ten percent in October 2009 and under five when Obama left office in 2017, but how much of that drop came from the stimulus, nobody can pin down.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And the car companies got one too. GM, Chrysler?

Marcus: [conversational] Both were dying in 2009, and Washington loaned them tens of billions. Bush started it with TARP money in December 2008, and Obama continued it. The industry survived and paid back most of the loans. So did it work? By the middle of 2009, the panic had stopped and the economy was growing again, slowly. But here's the unfairness that stuck: the banks got rescued while foreclosures piled up across the country and unemployment hit ten percent.

Maya: [warm tone] My street had three for-sale signs in 2007. By 2009, every one of them was a bank sign. The houses were still there. The people weren't.

Marcus: [serious tone] And your street wasn't unusual. By the fall of 2008, the stock market's main index had lost half its value, and nearly three million workers had lost their jobs that year alone. The human cost was the part no bailout could undo.

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] Box three: the verdict. Why no second Depression? Look at 1929. Back then, the Fed had spent the late 1920s raising rates to fight stock speculation, and after the crash, Washington chased a balanced budget while the banks collapsed around it. In 2008, Washington did the opposite. It spent. The Fed cut rates to zero. And this time there were guardrails the thirties never had: insured bank deposits, unemployment checks. Most economists say that's what kept 2008 from becoming the thirties.

Maya: [incredulous] So the whole lesson was just... do the opposite of 1929?

Marcus: [confident tone] More or less, and the safety net caught millions of falling families. Then came the cleanup. In 2010, Dodd-Frank, the biggest financial reform since Glass-Steagall, told banks to hold more capital and take less risk, and tried to fix the "too big to fail" problem.

Maya: [confident tone] So that problem's solved. Too-big-to-fail is over.

Marcus: [measured] That was the aim back in 2010: the law was designed to address the problem. But the biggest banks are bigger than ever, and nobody's fully sure the system is safe. That question is still open. And the inequality aftershock: from 1979 to 2007, the top one percent's incomes more than tripled while the middle sixty percent gained under forty percent. The crisis poured fuel on that gap.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] One thing I never got: did anybody go to jail for this?

Marcus: [serious tone] Almost nobody at the top. No senior bankers did prison time, and that's one reason the anger outlived the crisis: the banks got rescued, and nobody got punished.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Okay, but the anger had to go somewhere. Where did it land?

Marcus: [conversational] It did. On the right, the Tea Party said government was the problem: smaller government, lower taxes. On the left, Occupy Wall Street said in 2011 that the whole economy was rigged for the rich.

Maya: [conversational] The machine broke in a morning. The trust never quite came back.

Maya: [sheepish] Three boxes, let's land them. Box one: the machine, subprime loans, bundled, sold worldwide, stamped triple-A, built on rising prices. Checking that one. Box two: the rescue, TARP was Obama, right? Seven hundred billion?

Marcus: [firm] Bush. October 2008. Obama's was the stimulus, 2009. Classic mix-up — keep them straight.

Maya: [conversational] Right. TARP steadied the banks, the Fed cut rates to zero and bought bonds, the stimulus jolted demand, the autos survived. It stopped the panic, and it didn't feel fair. Box three: the verdict, Washington did the opposite of 1929, spent instead of tightening, and the safety net caught people. Then Dodd-Frank, the inequality gap, the Tea Party, Occupy. Checking all three.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer out loud before I do. One: a historian hands you a chart of American home prices from 2000 to 2010, up, up, up, then off a cliff. What's the chart actually proving about 2008?
[10-second pause]
That housing was the first domino. Prices fell by roughly a third, and everything built on rising prices fell with them. The mechanism matters more than the chart.

Maya: Two: compare Washington's response in 1929 with 2008. What changed?
[10-second pause]
In 1929, the Fed had hiked rates before the crash to fight speculation, and afterward Washington chased balanced budgets — Hoover even raised taxes in 1932 — while the banks collapsed. In 2008 it did the opposite. Washington spent freely. The Fed took rates to zero. And a safety net the thirties never had caught the families who fell anyway. That's the core of the "no second Depression" argument. If the question asks "why," start with the medicine. The medicine is the answer.

Maya: Three: TARP steadied the banks while foreclosures piled up and unemployment hit ten percent. What political fallout did that unfairness feed?
[10-second pause]
The Tea Party on the right, Occupy Wall Street on the left, both were answers to the same anger — the banks got rescued, and everyone else kept struggling.

Maya: Check your three boxes.

Marcus: Next time: the presidencies, and the polarization this crisis helped build.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] The banks got saved in a weekend. The trust took a decade to come due. [production note: held breath — do not rush the landing]
