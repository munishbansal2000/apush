Maya: Picture this: it's nineteen seventy-nine, and you're sitting in a line of cars that stretches around the block, waiting to buy gasoline. Your license plate ends in an odd number, so today you can buy. Tomorrow you can't. Last time: Watergate, the cover-up that ate a presidency. This time: the decade when America ran out of cheap everything, and started doubting itself. Four boxes on your sheet: stagflation, the OPEC energy weapon, détente, the malaise. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes, and you'll check all four off.

Maya: Wait, we skipped Ford. He was president for like two years, right?

Marcus: Right. Ford takes over when Nixon resigns, pardons him, and declares the long national nightmare over. Then he loses to Jimmy Carter in seventy-six, a peanut farmer promising he'll never lie. Ford's the bridge between the scandal and the malaise, the only president never elected to either office.

Marcus: Start with the word that broke economics. Stagflation. Stagnation plus inflation, two things that weren't supposed to happen together. The old rule said you got one or the other: a hot economy with rising prices, or a cold one with rising unemployment. In the seventies America got both. Prices climbing, jobs vanishing, at the same time. Economists had no playbook, because the playbook said it was impossible.

Maya: So what do the presidents actually do about it?

Marcus: Flail, mostly. Nixon freezes wages and prices by decree in nineteen seventy-one. It works for a year, then collapses. Ford wears a WIN button, Whip Inflation Now, as if a slogan could fix it. By nineteen eighty, inflation tops thirteen percent and unemployment tops seven. They invent the misery index, inflation plus unemployment. It becomes the number that ends presidencies.

Maya: Huh. So the experts' models just broke. What broke them?

Marcus: Oil. That's box two, and it's the hammer. In October of nineteen seventy-three, Egypt and Syria attack Israel in the Yom Kippur War. America backs Israel. The Arab members of OPEC, the Organization of Petroleum Exporting Countries, answer with an embargo: no oil for the United States. The price of a barrel quadruples in months. Gas stations run dry. And America learns, all at once, how much of its life was built on cheap imported oil.

Maya: And the lines? The odd-even days?

Marcus: Nineteen seventy-nine, the second shock, after the Iranian Revolution cuts supply again. Odd-even rationing: your plate ends in an odd number, you buy on odd days. The national speed limit drops to fifty-five to save fuel. Thermostats go down. And the humiliation is the point: the most powerful country on earth, waiting in line for gasoline because a cartel on the other side of the planet said so. Energy becomes a weapon, and America is on the wrong end of it.

Maya: And there's a nuclear footnote in seventy-nine, right? Three Mile Island?

Marcus: Right. March of nineteen seventy-nine, a reactor in Pennsylvania partially melts down. Nobody dies, but it kills new nuclear orders for decades. So America wants energy independence and fears every path to it: oil from a cartel, nuclear from its own backyard. The embargo floods oil states with petrodollars, billions recycled through American banks. The whole global money map gets redrawn around a fuel pump.

Maya: And Carter's answer to all this? Cardigans?

Marcus: Cardigans and cold thermostats. He puts solar panels on the White House, preaches conservation, and gets mocked. The country wants a hero, and he hands them a thermostat and a cardigan. That gap is exactly what the next election explodes.

Maya: Okay, so the economy's on fire and the gas tank's empty. Where's the Cold War in all this? Because détente sounds like the opposite of a crisis.

Marcus: Good instinct. Détente is French for relaxation, and it means the superpowers trying to cool the Cold War down. Nixon and his advisor Henry Kissinger play the triangle: in February of nineteen seventy-two, Nixon visits China, the first American president to do it, and the footage of him shaking Zhou Enlai's hand stuns the world, because China had been the enemy for twenty years. Three months later, in May, Nixon goes to Moscow and signs SALT One, the first treaty limiting nuclear weapons.

Maya: The idea being what, exactly? Trade and handshakes instead of threats?

Marcus: The idea: trade, talks, and arms control instead of brinkmanship. Kissinger flies between Middle East capitals, shuttle diplomacy, and wins a Nobel Peace Prize for it. In nineteen seventy-five the Helsinki Accords recognize Europe's borders, with human-rights promises nobody expects Moscow to keep.

Maya: But not everybody loved détente, right? I remember Reagan hating it.

Marcus: He called it a one-way street: America gives, the Soviets take. The conservative movement decides the Cold War isn't something to manage. It's something to win. Détente's enemies are already writing its obituary while it's still alive.

Maya: So does it work? It sounds like it works.

Marcus: For a while. American grain flows to the Soviet Union, hotlines stay hot, and the arms race at least gets a speed limit. But détente was always a truce, not a friendship. Then December of nineteen seventy-nine: the Soviet Union invades Afghanistan. Carter calls it the greatest threat to peace since World War Two, boycotts the Moscow Olympics, and détente dies. The Cold War freezes right back over.

Maya: Which brings us to the word on the box: malaise. And I have a feeling about this one.

Marcus: You should. Here's the confusion to correct: Jimmy Carter never said the word malaise. Not once. On July fifteenth, nineteen seventy-nine, with gas lines and inflation strangling the country, Carter gives a televised speech about a crisis of confidence. He'd retreated to Camp David for ten days beforehand, polling ordinary Americans like a patient. He says America has lost its way, its unity of purpose, its faith in the future. It's a sermon, not a plan. His opponents slap the label malaise on it, and the label sticks so hard that people remember Carter saying a word he never said.

Maya: Huh. So the malaise speech isn't the malaise speech.

Marcus: Exactly. And the crisis gets a face four months later. November fourth, nineteen seventy-nine: Iranian revolutionaries storm the American embassy in Tehran and take fifty-two Americans hostage.

Maya: Fifty-two people. For over a year.

Marcus: Four hundred forty-four days. Every night the news counts: day one hundred, day two hundred. A rescue mission, Operation Eagle Claw, dies in the Iranian desert when helicopters collide. Eight servicemen dead. Carter looks helpless. The hostages become a symbol of everything: the economy, the energy, the doubt. America held hostage, literally and figuratively. They're freed the day Reagan takes office, as if to underline the point.

Maya: So the seventies end with the country doubting itself. Both at once, right? The economy and the confidence.

Marcus: Both at once. Stagflation broke the economic models. OPEC broke the illusion of energy independence. Détente broke down and the Cold War came back. And the malaise, the crisis of confidence, was the feeling underneath all of it: that the problems were bigger than the presidency. Which is exactly what the next president will run against.

Maya: Four boxes, let's land them. One: stagflation.

Marcus: Stagnation plus inflation, the impossible combo. Oil shocks broke the old economic rules, and nobody had new ones.

Maya: Two: the OPEC energy weapon.

Marcus: The nineteen seventy-three embargo quadruples oil prices. Gas lines, odd-even rationing, fifty-five miles per hour. Energy as a weapon.

Maya: Three: détente.

Marcus: Nixon to China in February seventy-two, SALT One in Moscow that May. Relaxation, arms control, trade. Dead by Afghanistan, December seventy-nine.

Maya: Four: the malaise. Checking that one.

Marcus: Carter's crisis-of-confidence speech, July fifteenth, seventy-nine. He never said malaise; his opponents named it for him. Then the hostages, fifty-two Americans, four hundred forty-four days.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it, two seconds each.

Maya: One: stagflation to OPEC, what's the chain, in one sentence?

[2-second pause]

Maya: The oil embargo quadrupled energy prices, which drove inflation up while the economy stalled, breaking the old rules.

[2-second pause]

Maya: Two: what was détente, and what killed it?

[2-second pause]

Maya: The seventies relaxation of Cold War tensions, Nixon to China, SALT One. The Soviet invasion of Afghanistan killed it.

[2-second pause]

Maya: Three: why is malaise the wrong word for Carter's speech, and why does it stick anyway?

[2-second pause]

Maya: He said crisis of confidence, never malaise. It sticks because it named the feeling: a country doubting itself.

Marcus: The seventies ran out of cheap oil —
