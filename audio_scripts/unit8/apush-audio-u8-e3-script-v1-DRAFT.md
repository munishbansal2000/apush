# APUSH Unit 8, Episode 3: The Korean War
# Script v1 DRAFT — Story Mode (Marcus solo)
# Read note: Story Mode — Marcus narrates the whole episode. Thesis opens the piece, numbered proof beats carry the war's phases, one confusion-correction beat lands mid-story, and the closer is "the one thing to carry forward" plus a three-question self-test. [5-second pause] marks are production notes for real silence — they never go to the voice. Strip this header, the read note, and all pause tags before TTS; convert pause tags to silence. Intended duration ~8 min at ≤180 WPM.
# Pronunciation: Pusan (poo-SAHN), Inchon (in-CHON), Chosin (choh-SIN), Yalu (yah-LOO), Syngman Rhee (SING-man REE), Panmunjom (pahn-moon-JUHM), MacArthur (muhk-AHR-thur)

Marcus: Last time: containment went from an idea to an arsenal: the Truman Doctrine, the Marshall Plan, NSC-68. This time the arsenal gets used. Thesis first: the Korean War was the moment containment stopped being a memo and started being a war, and it invented a brand-new American way of fighting: the limited war. Follow the phases and you'll see the invention happen in real time.

Marcus: Phase one. Dawn, June twenty-fifth, nineteen fifty. Artillery opens up along the thirty-eighth parallel, the line that split Korea in half after World War Two, communist North above it, American-backed South below it. Ninety thousand North Korean soldiers pour across, tanks in the lead. Nobody in Washington expected a war that summer. The South Korean army folds in days. Seoul, the capital, falls within the week.

Marcus: Truman moves fast. He doesn't ask Congress for a declaration of war. He calls it a mere police action, and the word choice matters later. He goes to the UN instead, and here's the lucky break: the Soviets are boycotting the Security Council over China's seat, so there's nobody to veto. The UN authorizes the defense of South Korea. The troops are overwhelmingly American, but the flag says United Nations officially.

Marcus: The first Americans on the ground are Task Force Smith, about five hundred lightly armed soldiers flown in from occupation duty in Japan. On July fifth, outside a town called Osan, they meet North Korean tanks with weapons that can't stop tanks. They're overrun in hours. The North keeps rolling south, and by August the Americans and South Koreans are squeezed into a tiny pocket around the port of Pusan, in the southeast corner of the peninsula. It all comes down to this. If Pusan falls, Korea falls.

Marcus: And Pusan holds. Barely, but it holds. Reinforcements pour in through the port, and the perimeter line bends but doesn't break. This is the darkest hour of the war. It's also the setup for the boldest gamble in American military history.

Marcus: Phase two. Inchon. General Douglas MacArthur, the hero of the Pacific in World War Two, looks at a map and sees what nobody else sees. The North Koreans have stretched their supply lines hundreds of miles south to Pusan. Their rear is exposed at Inchon, a port on Korea's west coast, far behind their lines. MacArthur wants to land an invasion force there, cut the North's army in half, and retake Seoul in a month.

Marcus: Every other general in the room says no. Inchon has some of the most extreme tides on earth. At low tide the harbor is a mudflat, and the whole landing has to happen in a window of a few hours. There are seawalls to climb. The channel into the port is narrow enough that one sunk ship blocks the whole fleet. MacArthur hears it all, then does what legends always do: he insists. September fifteenth, nineteen fifty: the landing goes in, and it works. Within two weeks the Marines retake Seoul, and MacArthur personally hands the capital back to Syngman Rhee. The North Korean army south of the line disintegrates: cut off, surrounded, destroyed. In one month, the war reverses completely.

Marcus: And this is where victory starts to rot. MacArthur doesn't stop at the thirty-eighth parallel. He pushes north, deep into North Korea, driving toward the Yalu River on the Chinese border. The troops are told they'll be home by Christmas. Washington is nervous, because China has been warning, loudly, that it will not tolerate an American army on its border. MacArthur's intelligence says the Chinese won't come. MacArthur's intelligence is wrong.

Marcus: Phase three. Late October, the first Chinese soldiers are spotted. Then, the night of November twenty-fifth, the trap springs: roughly three hundred thousand Chinese troops slam into the overextended Americans in the mountains of North Korea. What follows is one of the longest retreats in American military history, through snow, through minus-thirty cold, fighting all the way. The First Marine Division gets surrounded at the Chosin Reservoir. It fights its way to the sea, carrying its wounded. The war that was almost won is suddenly being lost. By the spring of nineteen fifty-one, the front has frozen roughly where it started, near the thirty-eighth parallel, and here's the thing: it will barely move again for two years.

Marcus: Which brings us to the moment this war is really remembered for. Not a battle. A firing.

Marcus: MacArthur, humiliated by the Chinese offensive, starts publicly demanding a bigger war: blockade China's coast, bomb its bases, unleash the Nationalist Chinese from Taiwan. He writes letters to congressmen criticizing Truman's policy. Truman has told him, twice, to stop freelancing foreign policy in public. Then MacArthur does it again. On April eleventh, nineteen fifty-one, Truman fires the most famous general in America — in the middle of a war.

Marcus: The country explodes. MacArthur comes home to ticker-tape parades. Congress invites him to address a joint session, and he gives the speech of his life, the one that ends "old soldiers never die; they just fade away." MacArthur testifies to the Senate: in war, there is no substitute for victory. Half the country thinks Truman fired a hero for trying to win.

Marcus: Now the confusion I hear most about Korea, so let's correct it. People hear that Truman fired a general for wanting to win. They conclude Truman didn't want to win. Completely backwards, see? Truman fired MacArthur because MacArthur wanted a different war than the one America had chosen. Truman's war was a limited war: stop the invasion, restore the line, don't start World War Three with China and the Soviet Union. MacArthur wanted total victory over communism in Asia, whatever the cost. Firing him settled something enormous: in America, civilians control the military. Not the generals. Not even the legends. The president decides what the war is for, and the generals fight the war they're given. That principle held, and it's been tested ever since.

Marcus: Phase four is the long ending. Peace talks open in the summer of nineteen fifty-one at Panmunjom, and they drag on for two full years, while the fighting grinds on and men keep dying over hills that change hands and change back. The sticking point, strangely, isn't territory. It's prisoners: the communists want every captured soldier sent back; thousands of North Korean and Chinese prisoners refuse to go back, and the UN won't force them. The armistice finally comes on July twenty-seventh, nineteen fifty-three, a month after a new president, Dwight Eisenhower, elected promising he'd go to Korea, takes the pressure up. The line ends up almost exactly where it started. Three years, and the map barely moved.

Marcus: The bill: more than thirty-six thousand Americans dead, hundreds of thousands of South Koreans, and well over a million North Koreans and Chinese. Korea stayed divided. It still is. American troops are still there. And the veterans came home to something strange: no parades, no national moment. The war fell between the big one and Vietnam. Too small to be glorious, too inconclusive to celebrate. They started calling it the Forgotten War. Washington didn't even build the national memorial until nineteen ninety-five.

Marcus: One thing to carry forward. Korea invented the limited war, the new American way of war. Before Korea, America fought wars to win them completely. Unconditional surrender, ticker-tape parades. After Korea, the pattern is fight to a line, negotiate, garrison, and stay. Containment's first hot test proved the doctrine could hold a country, and it set the template Vietnam would follow, with all the same frustrations. When you hear "limited war," think Korea first. It was the first draft.

Marcus: Three questions. Say your answer before I do.

[5-second pause]

Marcus: One: why was Korea a turning point in how America fights wars — what changed about the goal?

[5-second pause]

Marcus: The goal stopped being total victory outright. Truman chose a limited war instead: restore the thirty-eighth parallel line, avoid a wider war with China and the Soviet Union, and he enforced it by firing MacArthur.

[5-second pause]

Marcus: Two: the war ended where it started. So did containment work in Korea?

[5-second pause]

Marcus: By containment's own logic, yes: the invasion was stopped and South Korea stayed non-communist. The confusion is judging a limited war by total-war standards.

[5-second pause]

Marcus: Three: why is Korea called the Forgotten War?

[5-second pause]

Marcus: Because it fell between the big one and Vietnam: too small to be glorious, too inconclusive to celebrate, too strange to parade. Korea didn't conquer the North; it was never supposed to.

Marcus: Dawn, June twenty-fifth. Artillery across the parallel, a rout, a gamble, a firing. Three years later the guns stop near the line where they started, and the American way of war is never the same.
