# APUSH Audio — Unit 8, Lesson 4: 9/11 & the War on Terror (Interview) — DRAFT v2

# Pronunciation: al-Qaeda (ahl KAY-duh), Taliban (TAH-lih-bahn), Shanksville (SHANKS-vil), Guantanamo (gwahn-tah-NAH-moh), Abu Ghraib (ah-BOO grayb), Hamdan (ham-DAHN), Khalid Sheikh Mohammed (hah-LEED shayk moh-HAH-med), Shia (SHEE-uh), Sunni (SOO-nee)

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: Fukuyama's holiday from history, the nineties, when America declared the big argument over. This time: the morning it ended. Four boxes: the September 11th attacks, the war in Afghanistan, the war in Iraq, and the home front, the security state, and the long verdict. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. About twelve minutes, pauses and all.

Marcus: [serious tone] Start with the morning itself, because the facts deserve precision. September 11, 2001: nineteen hijackers, working for the terrorist network al-Qaeda, seize four American airliners. Two crash into the Twin Towers of the World Trade Center in New York. One hits the Pentagon. The fourth goes down in a field near Shanksville, Pennsylvania, after the passengers fight back. By day's end both towers have collapsed. Almost three thousand people are dead. [beat]

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] My mom still keeps the paper from September 12th in a kitchen drawer. She won't throw it away. What I never got, how does a network most Americans had never heard of pull that off?

Marcus: [measured] The network was al-Qaeda, "the base," led by Osama bin Laden, a Saudi exile. He was living under the Taliban's protection in Afghanistan. The Taliban were the militant movement running the country. Nine days after the attacks, President Bush addressed a joint session of Congress and declared a War on Terror. The target wasn't one country. It was a tactic. That choice of words matters. It echoes through everything after.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Okay, so the fourth plane, Flight 93. That was the one headed for the Capitol, right? I feel like every documentary says the Capitol.

Marcus: [firm] Common mix-up, and an honest one, because nobody's fully sure. The most common reading is Washington, the Capitol or the White House. What the record does establish: the passengers learned what was happening, fought the hijackers, and the plane went down in Pennsylvania short of any target. One mistake to kill early: don't write that all four planes hit something. Three reached their targets. The fourth never did.

Maya: [sheepish] Fixing that in my notes. So the country just unites? I can't picture that now.

Marcus: [confident tone] For a while, yes. A French newspaper's next-day headline said, in translation, "We are all Americans." Bush stood on the rubble at Ground Zero with a bullhorn. And Washington moved fast. Six weeks later, in October 2001, Congress passed the Patriot Act, giving intelligence agencies much wider power to investigate suspected terrorists. That same October, Bush created an Office of Homeland Security, and late the next year Congress created a full cabinet department, the Department of Homeland Security, with a new agency, the TSA, taking over airport screening.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Can I add one thing I actually know? Springsteen put out a whole album about this, The Rising. My dad plays it every September. It's basically the country's grief with guitars.

Marcus: [conversational] I didn't know that. And that's the honest version of the unity. It was real, and it was grief-shaped. But grief doesn't set war aims. The next question did: what do you do about the people who planned it?

Marcus: [measured] Start with the demand. Bush told the Taliban: hand over bin Laden and al-Qaeda. They refused. So that fall, the United States and its allies invaded Afghanistan, Operation Enduring Freedom. Within weeks the Taliban government collapsed, and a fragile pro-Western government took shape in Kabul. The stated aim was counterterrorism, destroy al-Qaeda. But the administration also believed the region's problems would be solved by introducing democracy. The mission started as counterterrorism, then grew into something bigger, building a new Afghan state.

Maya: Hold on, prediction time. Your turn. The Taliban just refused to hand over bin Laden. You're advising the president. What's the move, and what do you tell the public the goal is? [8-second pause]

Maya: You go in. The goal's getting al-Qaeda, the people who did this.

Marcus: [confident tone] The stated aim, then. And the exam move is to watch the aim. It starts as punishment and prevention, and it slides into remaking a country. That slide is the whole story of box two.

Marcus: [serious tone] The initial victory was fast. The war wasn't. American forces stayed for twenty years, the longest war in American history. In 2011, a Navy SEAL team killed bin Laden. But the Taliban regrouped, and the 2021 American withdrawal was chaotic, and the Taliban walked back into power. Twenty years, and the ending rhymed with the beginning. One mistake to kill: don't write that the war ended when the Taliban fell. The fall took weeks. The war took two decades.

Maya: [conversational] Two down. Checking that one. But the same logic, punish, then remake, shows up again, right? Bigger this time.

Marcus: [serious tone] Bigger, and further from the original target. Iraq had no operational link to 9/11. Its dictator, Saddam Hussein, had even used poison gas on his own people years earlier, part of why Washington distrusted him. In January 2002 Bush named Iraq part of an "Axis of Evil." The CIA assured the president that Iraq possessed weapons of mass destruction. And the administration argued Iraq might hand such weapons to terrorists.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] But did anyone check? Like, inspectors, the UN, somebody with a clipboard?

Marcus: [measured] They did. The administration worked with the UN Security Council to resume weapons inspections, and Congress passed the Iraq Resolution in October 2002 authorizing force. The inspectors went back in. They kept finding no stockpiles, and the hawks kept saying Saddam was hiding them.

Maya: Your turn again. Inspectors say they've found nothing. The administration says wait and he'll hide it, invade and he'll use it. What do you do? [8-second pause]

Maya: I'd keep the inspectors in. Inspections are cheap. Wars aren't.

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] The debate split the country, and split America from some of its oldest allies. In March 2003 the United States, Britain, and their coalition partners invaded anyway.

Marcus: [firm] Baghdad fell in weeks. Saddam was captured. And the weapons of mass destruction were never found. One textbook puts it plainly: the rumors were later found to be false. The claim that Saddam helped orchestrate 9/11? The same book calls those allegations dubious, and other textbooks note that critics disputed the administration's whole view of Iraq. So the trap to avoid: don't write the WMD claim as fact. The claim is the story; the failure to find the weapons is the history.

Maya: [incredulous] Did the UN ever actually vote yes on the invasion, though? I always hear it both ways.

Marcus: [measured] No new Security Council resolution authorized it. The administration went in on Congress's authorization and the coalition's, which is exactly why the invasion's legality is still argued about. The war then became what the textbooks describe. Iraqis divided, Shia and Sunni Muslims, Kurds and Arabs, fighting each other, while an insurgency of Saddam loyalists and al-Qaeda-affiliated extremists bled the occupation for years.

Maya: [conversational] Quick exam habit, since this is where students slip: check the years before you write a word. Afghanistan, 2001. Iraq, 2003. Students write the Iraq invasion as the immediate answer to 9/11. It's two years and a whole argument later. That gap is the argument.

Marcus: [serious tone] Now the home front, the box most students underwrite. October 2001: the Patriot Act, giving intelligence agencies much wider powers, including the FBI's National Security Letters, which could pull phone, email, and financial records without a court order. Late 2002: the Department of Homeland Security becomes a cabinet department, absorbing agencies like immigration, with the TSA screening airports.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And that's where the torture debate starts?

Marcus: [serious tone] It goes public here. In 2002 the military opened a detention camp at Guantanamo Bay, Cuba. Detainees were interrogated hard. The CIA later admitted waterboarding three prisoners, including the man the books call the mastermind of the 9/11 attacks, Khalid Sheikh Mohammed. In 2004, photographs surfaced of American soldiers humiliating prisoners at Abu Ghraib prison in Iraq. Critics said the techniques amounted to torture. The Supreme Court pushed back in 2006, Hamdan v. Rumsfeld, ruling detainees couldn't be held indefinitely without due process.

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] Now hold both sides, because the box demands it. The supporters' case: no second attack on the scale of 9/11 followed, and the tools, surveillance, detention, interrogation, got the credit. The critics' case: the tools corroded the liberties they were meant to protect. And Barack Obama, elected partly on these concerns, kept many of the same policies anyway. The honest answer is that historians still argue about where the right line was. That's the debate this box asks you to hold.

Maya: [thoughtful tone] So both sides get to claim the same twenty years.

Marcus: [measured] Then the ledger. Roughly seven thousand American service members died across both wars. Civilian counts run into the hundreds of thousands, though the exact figure is fiercely disputed. The money runs into the trillions once veterans' care is counted. And the trust: the WMD intelligence failure and the Abu Ghraib photos cost America credibility it hasn't fully won back. Iraq's combat mission ended in 2011, but limited troops returned to fight ISIS. Afghanistan ended in 2021 with the Taliban back in charge.

Maya: [conversational] So twenty years of war end with the Taliban back in charge and the bills still coming due.

Maya: [conversational] Four boxes, let's land them. Box one: September 11th, 2001, nineteen hijackers, al-Qaeda, four planes. Three reached their targets, the Towers and the Pentagon, and the fourth went down in Pennsylvania after the passengers fought back. Nearly three thousand dead. Box two: Afghanistan. The Taliban sheltered bin Laden, refused to hand him over, and fell within weeks of the invasion that fall. But the mission slid from counterterrorism into nation-building, and the war ran twenty years to the chaotic 2021 exit.

Maya: [sheepish] Box three: Iraq, 2003. The WMD claim that collapsed, the al-Qaeda tie the books call dubious, no new UN resolution. And the dates I always flip: the Patriot Act was October 2001, and Homeland Security was late 2002? Tell me I finally have that right.

Marcus: [conversational] You have it right. Patriot Act, October 2001. Homeland Security, late 2002.

Maya: [confident tone] Then box four lands itself: the home front, wider surveillance powers, the new security department and the TSA, Guantanamo and the interrogation fight, and the verdict, thousands of American dead, trillions spent, and Afghanistan back under the Taliban. Checking all four.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it.

Maya: One: a textbook sidebar excerpts President Bush's September 20, 2001 address to Congress, the speech where he declares a War on Terror. Why does the textbook include it? What's the exam point of that source? [15-second pause]

Maya: The point is the framing. He doesn't declare war on a country. He declares war on a tactic, terrorism itself. The evidence is the open-ended language: a war on a tactic has no borders and no finish line. So the mission could slide from al-Qaeda in Afghanistan to regime change in Iraq. The source explains the next twenty years in one speech.

Maya: Two: what made the 2003 invasion of Iraq controversial? [15-second pause]

Maya: The premises collapsed. The administration said Iraq had weapons of mass destruction. The CIA assured the president, and the weapons were never found. It suggested ties between Saddam and al-Qaeda. The books call those allegations dubious, and critics disputed the whole view. So the controversy isn't just the war itself. It's that the stated reasons didn't survive the war.

Maya: Three: explain how American war aims changed between the fall of 2001 and the spring of 2003. [15-second pause]

Maya: In 2001 the aim was counterterrorism with a clear target: destroy al-Qaeda and topple the Taliban sheltering it. By 2003 the aim had become preventing a hypothetical threat and remaking a region, invading Iraq over WMDs that were never found and a 9/11 link that didn't hold. The evidence is the two invasions themselves. One answered an attack, the other answered an intelligence estimate. That's the shift, from punishment to prevention, and it turned short wars into long ones.

Maya: One more, fast. Name two home-front changes after 9/11.

Maya: The Patriot Act's expanded surveillance powers, and the new Department of Homeland Security, with the TSA taking over airport screening. Fast enough.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Check your boxes. Next time: the money breaks, 2008, and the financial crisis. The holiday ended in a single morning. The bill took a decade to come due.
