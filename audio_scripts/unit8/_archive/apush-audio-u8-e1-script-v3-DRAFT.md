# APUSH Unit 8, Episode 1: Cold War Origins
# Script v3 DRAFT — Debate (Maya moderates, Marcus + Volkov)
# Read note: Maya moderates a debate. Marcus makes the orthodox case (the Soviet Union broke the peace). Dr. Elena Volkov, a historian of Soviet foreign policy, makes Moscow's case (the Soviet Union was defending itself). Maya tracks three boxes on her episode sheet: Soviet buffer states, the Cold War going global, and who started it — and checks each off as the episode earns it. [5-second pause] tags are production notes for real silence in the self-test; they never go to the voice. The em dash in Marcus's closing setup line is a held breath — do not rush it. Strip this header and the read note before TTS.
# Pronunciation: Yalta (YAWL-tuh), Kennan (KEN-nen), Gaddis (GAD-iss), Bandung (bahn-DOONG), Volkov (VAWL-kov)

Maya: Last time: the bomb ended the war and left America holding the only one on earth. This time the alliance cracks and the world splits, and historians still fight about who broke it first. Three boxes on your sheet. One: Soviet buffer states, why Moscow wanted Eastern Europe. Two: the Cold War going global, how collapsing empires turned a European fight into a world fight. Three: who started it, orthodox versus revisionist versus post-revisionist. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes, and you'll land all three.

Maya: The setup: Marcus argues the orthodox case, that the Soviet Union broke the peace. Dr. Volkov argues Moscow's case, that the Soviet Union was defending itself. I moderate, I push, and I keep score. Marcus, open.

Marcus: The orthodox case rests on broken promises. February 1945, Yalta: Roosevelt, Churchill, Stalin. Stalin signs on to free elections across Eastern Europe. Then Soviet troops, already sitting in those countries, install communist governments instead. Poland's 1947 election: the communists claim a landslide nobody outside Moscow believes. George Kennan, watching from the American embassy in Moscow, writes the Long Telegram in 1946. The Soviets respect only force, so America has to hold the line. That memo is containment's birth certificate.

Volkov: Moscow's case starts with the war the Soviets actually fought. Twenty-six, twenty-seven million dead. The exact number's debated, but the scale isn't. The Soviet Union had been invaded through Poland twice in thirty years. Of course Moscow wanted friendly governments on its border. No land grab — a country that just buried a generation making sure Germany can never march east a third time. And from Moscow's side, the threats looked American: the bomb Washington kept secret, the bases going up around the Soviet perimeter.

Maya: Two cases, both with receipts. Clash round one: the flashpoints, done concrete. Poland, Greece and Turkey, Berlin. Marcus, Poland is yours.

Marcus: Yalta promised free elections. Stalin delivered a one-party state. Washington didn't imagine the gap between those two things. It watched it happen, in public, in Poland first.

Volkov: And Moscow watched something else: the corridor every invader used, from Napoleon to Hitler. A friendly Poland was the price of never again. Even Truman's own Commerce Secretary, Henry Wallace, pointed out the hypocrisy — America ran its own sphere of influence across Latin America and called it security too.

Maya: Hold on, quick check. Yalta is the elections promise, right? Potsdam is the zones one?

Marcus: Half right — Yalta's the elections promise, and the zones were Yalta too. Potsdam was the colder sequel, new president, and the promise was already rotting by then.

Maya: Noted. Greece and Turkey, 1947. Walk me through it.

Marcus: Greece is in a civil war, Turkey is under Soviet pressure over the straits out of the Black Sea. Truman asks for four hundred million dollars and declares America will support free peoples resisting subjugation. He doesn't say Greece and Turkey. He says free peoples, anywhere. One speech, and containment goes from a memo to a promise with no borders.

Volkov: But Greece's communists weren't taking orders from Moscow. It was a local war. Truman sold it as Moscow versus freedom and bought himself a blank check. Notice what just happened: the threat gets defined as global, so the response gets defined as global. That framing did more work than any single fact about Greece.

Maya: Berlin, then. The blockade.

Marcus: June 1948. Stalin cuts every road and rail line into West Berlin. About two million people, no food coming in by land. Truman answers with the airlift, and at the peak a plane is landing or taking off every thirty seconds, around the clock, for almost a year. Stalin lifts the blockade in May 1949. But Germany is split for good, and NATO — the North Atlantic Treaty Organization — follows that April. Twelve countries, an attack on one is an attack on all.

Volkov: The blockade was a reaction. The West's new currency looked to Moscow like an economic takeover of the Soviet sector. As for NATO, listen to its own first secretary, Lord Ismay: to keep the Russians out, the Americans in, and the Germans down. From Moscow: no defensive alliance. Encirclement with a slogan.

Maya: Checking box one, Soviet buffer states. Moscow's fear was real. Washington's fear was real. Same facts, opposite readings — the security dilemma in one image.

Maya: Clash round two: the third player. While Europe split, the empires collapsed. Marcus.

Marcus: India, 1947. Britain leaves, two new nations are born. Over the next decades, roughly eighty new states appear on the world map. And both superpowers show up with gift baskets: aid, arms, alliances, all with strings attached. The Cold War stops being a European fight and becomes a global auction.

Volkov: The new nations didn't enjoy being auctioned. 1955, Bandung, Indonesia: twenty-nine African and Asian leaders meet and declare they won't be anybody's pawns. The Non-Aligned Movement is born. Some of them played both sides for aid, clever. Others got crushed between them. Angola, 1975: independence from Portugal, then a civil war where Washington and Moscow pick rival sides, and a local power struggle becomes a years-long bloodbath.

Maya: I actually know a piece of this. My film class did Oppenheimer, the whole "now I am become death" scene. Didn't Truman not even tell Stalin about the bomb first?

Marcus: At Potsdam he mentioned a new weapon, vaguely. Stalin already knew through spies. But the secrecy told Moscow everything about American intentions. Allies don't hide doomsday weapons from each other. And once the Soviets test their own bomb in 1949, every crisis after has an existential edge. The bomb doesn't just end one war, it poisons the next peace.

Volkov: Exactly. Washington read Soviet secrecy about its own program as proof of hostile intent. Both sides' secrecy fed the other's fear: the spiral in one image.

Maya: Clash round three: the who-started-it fight. Why does the answer matter?

Marcus: Three schools. The orthodox view, the traditional American case: Soviet expansionism broke the peace. Then the revisionists, led by William Appleman Williams in the sixties: flip the camera. America came out of the war with its factories intact and the only bomb on earth, plus a plan for open markets everywhere. The International Monetary Fund, the World Bank. By that reading, the Soviets were reacting to an American economic empire, not building their own.

Volkov: Then the post-revisionists, John Lewis Gaddis and others, call both stories half true. Both capitals, they argue, misread each other. The Soviets wanted security and pursued it in ways that looked like aggression. The Americans wanted open markets and pursued it in ways that looked like encirclement. Nobody's fully sure who started it, because both sides were answering fears the other never fully understood.

Maya: So the answer you pick rewrites everything after. Korea, Vietnam, the whole forty years.

Marcus: My closing verdict: Stalin had a choice in 1945, and he chose control over coexistence. The buffer could have been friendly without being chained. The broken promises are on the record, and no security fear erases them.

Volkov: My closing verdict: a country that buried twenty-seven million people doesn't gamble on trust. The West rearmed and ringed the Soviet Union with alliances, then called the Soviet reaction aggression. Security first. Everything else followed.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: Soviet buffer states. After Yalta's broken promise in Poland, Moscow built a wall of friendly governments, and Washington read invasion in it. Two: the Cold War going global. Eighty new nations, all twenty-nine leaders at Bandung saying not your pawns, Angola showing what happened when the superpowers picked sides. Three: who started it. Orthodox says Moscow expanded, revisionists say Washington built an empire, post-revisionists say both misread each other. My verdict: this argument never dies because every school of historians finds something real in the evidence. And was it inevitable? Every flashpoint was a choice. Poland, Greece, Berlin. Somebody chose, every single time. Checking all three.

Maya: Two questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One: a historian writes that the Cold War came from Soviet expansionism in violation of the Yalta agreements. Which school is that? [5-second pause] The orthodox view. The traditional American case, broken promises as the driver.

Maya: Two, stimulus-style: a 1955 photo shows twenty-nine African and Asian leaders meeting in Bandung, Indonesia, declaring they won't take sides. What's the point of this source? [5-second pause] That's the Non-Aligned Movement. New nations refusing to be Cold War pawns. It proves the Cold War had gone global, and the new nations knew it.

Maya: Check your three boxes. Episode 1 in the books. Next time: containment gets its instruments. Doctrine, dollars, alliance, and the memo that militarized everything.

Marcus: Two empires, one question —

Maya: and still no agreed answer.
