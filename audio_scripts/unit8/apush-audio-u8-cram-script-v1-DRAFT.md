# APUSH Audio — Unit 8 Cram Session: Maya + Jay — DRAFT v1
# Read note (strip before TTS): Cram format — no boxes. Ten rapid-fire questions, the period thesis, and the DBQ prediction. No pause marks appear in this script — self-test silence is added in production. Jay is the student: he retrieves, he guesses, he gets things wrong; Maya corrects and lands the memory hooks. Strip this header and the read note before TTS. Intended duration ~7 min at ≤180 WPM.
# Pronunciation: OPEC (OH-pek), détente (day-TAHNT), SALT (sawlt)

Maya: Last time: the seventies, stagflation, and the crisis of confidence. This time: no new material. Ten questions, the whole unit, rapid-fire. Nineteen forty-five to nineteen eighty, Cold War to malaise. Thirty-five years in ten questions. Answer all ten out loud and you're ready. About seven minutes. If you can answer all ten, you can write the LEQ. Go.

Jay: Ten questions, one unit, thirty-five years. Hit me.

Maya: Question one: who started the Cold War? Orthodox versus revisionist, one sentence each.

Jay: Orthodox: the Soviets did, Stalin broke his promises and grabbed Eastern Europe. Revisionist: America did, with the atomic bomb and the dollar, pushing into Moscow's backyard. Post-revisionist: it was both, each misreading the other.

Maya: Clean. Orthodox blames Soviet expansion, revisionist blames American economic power, post-revisionist blames the structure. This is the debate the textbooks fight about. Question one, landed.

Jay: Question two: Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, NATO. Which is military, which is economic, which is the alliance?

Maya: You take this one first.

Jay: Truman Doctrine is the military one, aid to fight communism. Marshall Plan is economic, rebuilding Europe's markets. NATO is the alliance, the actual treaty army.

Maya: Say it tighter: the doctrine is the promise, the plan is the money, NATO is the muscle. Nineteen forty-seven, forty-eight, forty-nine. Containment's the idea behind all three: stop communism everywhere, always.

Jay: Two down, eight to go. I'm keeping up.

Maya: Then keep up. Question two, landed.

Jay: Question three: Korea. Why did Truman fire MacArthur, and what did it settle?

Jay: MacArthur wanted to nuke China and Truman said no? And it settled that civilians control the military?

Maya: Close. MacArthur publicly defied the limited-war strategy, pushing to expand into China. Truman fired him in April of fifty-one. What it settled: the president commands, the general obeys, even a hero. Civilian control isn't a suggestion. MacArthur's farewell: old soldiers never die, they just fade away. Truman's reply, basically: you're fired. And Korea invented the limited war: fight to a line, negotiate, garrison, stay.

Jay: Question four: McCarthyism. One real spy case, and one way the scare went too far.

Maya: Real case first.

Jay: The Rosenbergs, Julius and Ethel, executed in fifty-three for atomic espionage. Real spies, real secrets.

Maya: And too far?

Jay: The Hollywood Ten, jailed for refusing to name names about their beliefs. Or the Lavender Scare, purging gay federal workers who'd done nothing.

Maya: Either one lands it. Real espionage, fake dragnet. Truman's loyalty program screened two million federal workers before McCarthy even started. Venona proved the spies were real in the nineties, but it proved nothing about McCarthy's lists. The distinction the exam wants. Question four, landed.

Jay: Question five: GI Bill to suburbs. The chain in thirty seconds.

Jay: Sixteen million veterans get college money and four-percent mortgages. They need houses. Levitt builds them assembly-line style: seventeen thousand houses, one every sixteen minutes, sixty-nine ninety, appliances included. Suburbs explode, Sunbelt tilts the map south and west.

Maya: And the fine print?

Jay: Redlining and deed clauses lock out Black families. Women get pushed back home. The boom had a color line. And the Sunbelt: air conditioning, interstates, defense dollars tilt the electoral map south and west by seventy-two.

Maya: Thirty seconds, fine print included. Question five, landed.

Jay: Question six: Brown to Selma. What did each step add?

Maya: Walk the chain.

Jay: Brown, fifty-four: segregated schools violate the Fourteenth Amendment, unanimous. Montgomery, fifty-five: the boycott, King, nonviolence works. Birmingham, sixty-three: brutality on TV forces the nation to watch. Selma, sixty-five: the vote, the Voting Rights Act.

Maya: And one movement that borrowed the playbook?

Jay: The women's movement. Or Chicano, or gay rights after Stonewall. Moral language, nonviolent confrontation, federal pressure, television.

Maya: Any of them. The playbook was open-source: moral claims, cameras, nonviolence, federal pressure. And here's the exam move: each step answers the last one's failure. Brown wins in court, but the South resists. Montgomery wins with boycotts, but the ballot's still blocked. Birmingham wins on TV, but voting's still denied. Selma wins the vote. Question six, landed.

Jay: Question seven: Cuban Missile Crisis. What was the actual deal?

Jay: Kennedy blockades Cuba, Khrushchev blinks, missiles out. Right?

Maya: Half right. The public deal: Soviets pull missiles from Cuba, America pledges not to invade. The secret deal: America quietly removes its Jupiter missiles from Turkey. Thirteen days, October sixty-two, resolved by a trade nobody was supposed to know about.

Jay: So toughness plus a secret trade. Got it.

Maya: The exam sentence, right there. Thirteen days in October, a naval quarantine, a secret Jupiter trade, and a hotline afterwards so it never happens again. Question seven, landed.

Jay: Question eight: Great Society. Two programs that survived, and the war that starved it.

Jay: Medicare and Medicaid survived. Head Start too. The war that starved it: Vietnam, guns versus butter.

Maya: And the number?

Jay: Poverty about nineteen percent in sixty-four, about twelve percent by sixty-nine. It worked, then the money ran out. Guns and butter: Vietnam ate the budget Johnson needed at home. The Moynihan Report warned about family breakdown in sixty-five, and Washington wasn't ready to hear it.

Maya: Question eight, landed.

Jay: Question nine: Tet plus Watergate. How did they combine to break trust?

Maya: Your turn first.

Jay: Tet, January sixty-eight: the enemy attacks everywhere, Cronkite says it's a stalemate, and the credibility gap opens. Watergate: the president covers up a burglary, lies about it, resigns. Together they teach America its leaders lie.

Maya: Say it like a thesis: Tet broke trust in what the government said about the war, Watergate broke trust in the presidency itself. The Pentagon Papers proved they lied on paper too. The Saturday Night Massacre, the smoking-gun tape, the resignation. The gap between the story and the truth, three times over.

Jay: Question ten: Silent Spring to EPA, then stagflation. And why did the seventies make Reagan possible?

Maya: Walk it.

Jay: Carson's Silent Spring, sixty-two, proves chemicals climb the food chain. Earth Day, seventy, twenty million march. Nixon creates the EPA that December. Then the seventies: OPEC embargo, gas lines, stagflation, the hostage crisis. Carter preaches limits, the country wants confidence.

Maya: And Reagan?

Jay: Morning in America. He promises the confidence Carter couldn't: tax cuts, military strength, optimism. The malaise elects its opposite. Stagflation discredited liberal economics, the misery index ended presidencies, the hostages discredited Carter, and Reagan promised both growth and strength.

Maya: Ten questions, landed. Now the period thesis, the one sentence that holds the whole unit. Say it with me.

Jay: From nineteen forty-five to nineteen eighty, the Cold War drove American power abroad while prosperity, civil rights, and cultural rebellion transformed life at home, until Vietnam, scandal, and stagflation cracked the consensus.

Maya: Say it again, slower. Your LEQ thesis, if the prompt asks about continuity and change.

Maya: The unit in one breath. Now for the prediction beat. Two Document-Based Questions I'd bet on. One: evaluate the extent to which the nineteen sixties represented a turning point in American history. Two: evaluate the effectiveness of containment as a Cold War policy, Korea through Vietnam.

Jay: The answers are sitting in these ten questions. The domino theory, the credibility gap, the movements.

Maya: Ten questions, one thesis, two predictions. The DBQ gives you documents; these ten give you the argument to hang them on. Unit eight, done. Next time: morning in America. Reagan, the conservative turn, and the Cold War's endgame.

Jay: Containment abroad —

Maya: And cracks at home.
