Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: morning in America, the Wall down, the towers fallen, the banks broken, and a country split — and still split. This time: no new material. Six lessons, six questions, 1980 to the present, in about fifteen minutes. The victory, the boom, the morning it ended, the money breaking, the divide. I'm firing the questions. Track the ones you couldn't explain right now — circle them.

Maya: One. 1981: an economist sketches a curve claiming tax cuts can raise government revenue. Congress buys it. Walk the chain from that curve to the national debt doubling in six years.

[10-second pause]

Marcus: The curve was Arthur Laffer's, and the theory was supply-side: feed the producers and prosperity works its way down. The 1981 tax law cut income tax rates by about a quarter over three years, the top rate falling from about seventy percent to fifty. Reagan also deregulated banking, cable, and long-distance phones. And in 1981 he fired every striking air traffic controller.

Maya: Give me the coalition behind it.

Marcus: Four strands. The money people: pro-business conservatives, lower taxes, deregulation. The morals people: Falwell's Moral Majority, founded in 1979, mobilizing evangelicals. The map: the Sunbelt, booming and leaning conservative. And the Reagan Democrats: working-class Roosevelt Democrats who crossed over on the economy, the values message, and a racial undertow historians still argue about.

Maya: [sheepish] And the eighty-six law, the Tax Revenue Act?

Marcus: [firm] Mix-up, and a real book trap. One review book prints "Tax Revenue Act." It's the Tax Reform Act of 1986, signed October 22nd, and it dropped the top rate to twenty-eight percent. Forty-nine states in eighty-four, remember.

Maya: [sheepish] With a landslide like that, the theory must have worked. The cuts paid for themselves.

Marcus: [firm] The debt went from about nine hundred billion in 1980 to over two trillion by 1986.

Maya: Two. November 1989: the Wall opens on live television because a spokesman didn't read his memo. Walk the chain from Gorbachev's reforms to that night.

[10-second pause]

Marcus: Gorbachev took over in 1985 with two reforms. Glasnost meant openness: the press could finally report honestly. Perestroika meant restructuring: market pieces slipped into the planned economy. But a free press reported the wreck, feeding the protests. In 1989 the cascade came: Solidarity pushed the Communists from power in Poland, Hungary opened its border, and in August roughly two million people formed a human chain across Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania.

Maya: The Baltic Way showed the scale, then came the Wall.

Marcus: [serious tone] November ninth. The East German spokesman Günter Schabowski announced new travel rules on live TV, and when a reporter asked when they took effect, he said, immediately, without delay. West German TV ran with "the border is open." The guards gave up. And the one word that explains why 1989 worked and 1956 didn't: the tanks stayed home. Moscow crushed earlier revolts. Gorbachev kept the tanks in.

Maya: [sheepish] And the INF Treaty was the one that cut both sides' nuclear stockpiles in half?

Marcus: [firm] Common mix-up. The 1987 INF Treaty didn't cut stockpiles in half. It abolished a whole category: land-based missiles in the middle range, aimed across Europe, with inspectors from both sides watching the destruction happen. First treaty to destroy a weapons category instead of just capping it. The year before, Reykjavik nearly erased them all, and died over SDI.

Maya: So who gets the credit for ending it?

Marcus: The winning SAQ answer is the argument: Reagan's pressure landed on a system already rotting from inside. The arms race taxed the Soviet economy far more than America's.

Maya: Three. NAFTA takes effect on New Year's Day, 1994. A review book says the Senate ratified it. Why is that sentence wrong?

[10-second pause]

Marcus: The mechanism was legislation: H.R. 3450, the House 234 to 200, the Senate 61 to 38, Clinton signing that December. Ratification takes two-thirds of the Senate; this cleared with a simple majority in both chambers.

Maya: [sheepish] And it was Clinton's deal?

Marcus: [firm] Not quite. Bush negotiated it; Clinton pushed it through Congress. Ross Perot ran against it in ninety-two, warning of a "giant sucking sound" of jobs heading to Mexico, and pulled nineteen percent of the vote, the best third-party showing in eighty years. The WTO opened in 1995. Congress gave China permanent normal trade relations in 2000. And the boom: one hundred twenty months of growth, unemployment under four percent, four straight budget surpluses.

Maya: [sheepish] So everybody won?

Marcus: Wages barely moved for most workers while union membership kept sliding. Stockholders won; wage earners stalled. Pair the surplus with the flat wages: the decade's exam point.

Maya: Four. September 2001: al-Qaeda hides in Afghanistan under the Taliban. By that October, America is at war. What was the aim, and where did it slide?

[10-second pause]

Marcus: [serious tone] Start with precision. September 11th, 2001: nineteen hijackers, working for al-Qaeda under Osama bin Laden, seized four airliners. Two crashed into the Twin Towers, one hit the Pentagon, and the fourth went down in a Pennsylvania field after the passengers fought back. Both towers collapsed. Almost three thousand were dead. [beat] Bush demanded the Taliban hand over bin Laden. They refused, and on October 7th, Operation Enduring Freedom began.

Maya: [sheepish] October? I thought November.

Marcus: [firm] Mix-up, and a book trap. One review book's summary says November; the book's own body text says October 7th. Teach the body's date. The stated aim was counterterrorism: destroy al-Qaeda. But it slid into nation-building, remaking Afghanistan. Twenty years later the exit was chaotic, and the Taliban walked back into power.

Maya: Iraq comes two years and a whole argument later.

Marcus: In 2003 the U.S. invaded over weapons of mass destruction that were never found, and a Saddam-to-9/11 link the books call dubious. No new UN resolution authorized it. At home: the Patriot Act, October 2001, wider surveillance powers; Homeland Security, late 2002. The debate: security versus liberty, and historians still argue where the line was.

Maya: Five. September 15, 2008: Lehman Brothers dies. Why did letting one bank fall freeze credit everywhere?

[10-second pause]

Marcus: Because nobody could tell who was holding the losses. The machine: subprime mortgages, almost three in ten by 2008, bundled into securities and sold worldwide. The rating agencies stamped the bundles triple-A. The whole thing leaned on one bet: home prices always rise. When prices fell, borrowers went underwater, defaults spiked, the bundles turned toxic. Lehman died, and banks stopped lending to each other.

Maya: [sheepish] And TARP, that was Obama's stimulus?

Marcus: [firm] That's the famous flip. TARP was Bush, October 2008: seven hundred billion dollars to steady the banks. Obama's was the stimulus, 2009: almost eight hundred billion in spending and tax cuts. Two different presidents, two different years, two different laws. The Fed cut rates to zero and bought bonds by the trillion, and in 2010 Dodd-Frank tried to rein the banks in.

Maya: So why no second Depression?

Marcus: Because Washington did the opposite of 1929. It spent. The Fed cut rates to zero. And a safety net the thirties never had, insured deposits, unemployment checks, caught the families that fell anyway. But the unfairness stuck: the banks got rescued while foreclosures piled up and unemployment hit ten percent. That anger fed the Tea Party on the right and Occupy Wall Street on the left.

Maya: Six. 2016: Clinton wins the popular vote by nearly three million, Trump wins the Electoral College 304 to 227. What does the split prove?

[10-second pause]

Marcus: That electors pick the president. Trump took 304 electors to Clinton's 227, while she won the popular vote by nearly three million. And 2016 didn't come from nowhere. In 2010 Obama signed the Affordable Care Act with zero Republican votes in either chamber. A landmark law on straight party lines: the preview of the parties stopping building things together. Then the outsider, the policy reversals, two impeachments, January 6th, and a second term after the 2024 win.

Maya: And the machine underneath the divide?

Marcus: Four gears. The news fragmented: the Fairness Doctrine ended in 1987, Fox and MSNBC launched in 1996, and the feed rewards outrage. The map sorted: journalist Bill Bishop argued Americans clustered with like-minded neighbors. The primaries: in a safe district the real fight is the primary, and primaries reward the fired-up wing. And the money: when the economy split in two, the politics followed. The gears feed each other.

Maya: [sheepish] So the whole split starts in 2016?

Marcus: [firm] The intensification traces back to the nineties. One of the books starts there.

Maya: Where do Biden and Dobbs fit?

Marcus: Biden took office with the COVID recovery still rocky, then the chaotic 2021 Afghanistan withdrawal, then Russia invading Ukraine in 2022. That same year, Dobbs v. Jackson sent abortion law back to the states. One more crack along the same fault line.

Maya: Six beats, one sentence underneath: America won the Cold War and spent the victory arguing with itself.

Marcus: Test it. Reagan's pressure helped crack the Soviet Union open. Globalization made the country richer, and the first argument was over who got the money: stockholders up, paychecks flat. September eleventh united the country, and Iraq broke the unity: was the war honest? The crash rescued the banks and left the foreclosed houses behind: who was the rescue for? And underneath it all, a machine that made sure the two sides never heard each other.

Maya: Won the big fight abroad. Spent the peace fighting itself. Say it in your sleep.

Maya: Ten questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. First: a 1992 debate clip — Ross Perot warning of a "giant sucking sound" of jobs heading to Mexico. What's the point of this source?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The line is the trade warning: NAFTA would drain factory jobs to Mexico, where wages were a fraction of ours. You can hear it in the phrasing — jobs "going south" — and his nineteen percent of the vote shows the fear was mainstream. The line stuck because it turns an abstract trade deal into a kitchen-table argument.

Maya: Second: a student writes that the 2003 invasion of Iraq was America's answer to 9/11. What's wrong?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The chronology and the premise. The answer to 9/11 was Afghanistan, October 2001: the Taliban sheltered al-Qaeda, refused the hand-over, and fell within weeks. Iraq came in March 2003, two years and a whole argument later, over weapons of mass destruction that were never found and a Saddam-to-9/11 link the books call dubious. The gap is the argument: one invasion answered an attack, the other answered an intelligence estimate.

Maya: Third: in one sentence, why did the 1998 to 2001 surpluses not erase the national debt?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Four straight surpluses, and the debt shrank. Four good years cut the hole smaller. The hole was decades deep.

Maya: Fourth: a student writes that the 1981 tax cuts paid for themselves. What's the verdict?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Nine hundred billion in 1980. Over two trillion by 1986. The supply-side verdict, in two numbers.

Maya: Fifth: a student writes that Reagan alone ended the Cold War. What does the exam want instead?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The argument has two authors: Reagan's pressure, and a Soviet system rotting from inside. The arms race taxed the Soviet economy far more than America's, and Gorbachev's reforms cracked open what stagnation had hollowed. The SAQ answer is the argument.

Maya: Sixth: a student writes that the Senate ratified NAFTA by a two-thirds vote. What's the correction?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The mechanism was legislation. H.R. 3450 passed the House 234 to 200 and the Senate 61 to 38, and Clinton signed it that December. Simple majorities, both chambers.

Maya: Seventh: a student writes that the 2001 Afghanistan invasion was about building a new Afghan government from day one. What slid?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The aim. The stated aim was counterterrorism: destroy al-Qaeda after the Taliban refused the hand-over, October 7th, 2001. The mission slid into nation-building, remaking Afghanistan, and twenty years later the exit was chaotic.

Maya: Eighth: a student mixes up the two rescue laws. Sort TARP from the stimulus.

[10-second pause]

Maya: Two presidents, two years, two laws. TARP was Bush, October 2008: seven hundred billion to steady the banks. The stimulus was Obama, 2009: almost eight hundred billion in spending and tax cuts.

Maya: Ninth: in one sentence, why did the 2016 winner lose the popular vote and still take the White House?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Because electors pick the president. Trump took 304 electors to Clinton's 227, while Clinton won the popular vote by nearly three million. The national tally runs a different race than the Electoral College.

Maya: Tenth: a student writes that polarization started in 2016. Where does the intensification really start?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Back in the nineties. One of the books traces the intensification there, and 2010 gave the preview: the Affordable Care Act passed with zero Republican votes in either chamber, a landmark law on straight party lines.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Six lessons, one cram, nothing dropped — and it's the last one of the whole course. From thirteen colonies to fifty states: every unit was the same fight, a country arguing over what it's for. 1980 to the present is just the latest round.
