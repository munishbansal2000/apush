  1994), the WTO (1995), Perot's 1992 warning, Seattle 1999, China PNTR 2000;

  broadband as lived experience, the dot-com boom and bust (NASDAQ peak March
  2000, crash — the internet kept growing); (3) the nineties economy —
  120-month expansion, sub-4% unemployment, surpluses 1998–2001, with flat
  wages and the union slide; (4) the verdict — stockholders vs wage earners,
  Fukuyama's "end of history," the warnings (1993 WTC, 1998 embassies,

## Suspected book error (flagged, taught around)
- 5 Steps to a 5 APUSH 2024, OEBPS/ch28.../ch29 L37 (the Clinton-presidency
  paragraph): "Clinton persuaded the Senate to ratify the North American
  Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA)." Wrong on the mechanism: NAFTA was not
  ratified by the Senate as a treaty. It was implemented by legislation —
  H.R. 3450, the NAFTA Implementation Act, passed by BOTH chambers
  (House 234–200, Senate 61–38) and signed by Clinton on Dec 8, 1993
  (P.L. 103-182), in effect Jan 1, 1994 (Congress.gov/CRS R45846). The script
  never says "ratify" — it says "pushed through Congress" — and the
  registry entry F-U8-021 carries the falsehood pattern
  "Senate.{0,15}ratified.{0,15}NAFTA". (A separate U8-L1 entry, F-U8-012,
  already covers 5steps misnaming the 1986 tax law.)

## Framing decisions that matter
- "Longest expansion on record" would be WRONG today: NBER confirms the
  1991–2001 run (120 months) was the record only until the 2009–2020
  expansion (128 months) broke it. The script teaches "the longest in
  American history at the time" (F-U8-026 carries the framing guard).
- Surplus causes attributed honestly and split by party: peace dividend
  (Tier 1), Clinton's 1993 tax increase (Tier 1), boom revenues, the 1997
  budget deal with a Republican Congress (Tier 1); "nobody's fully sure
  where the boom ends and the policy begins."
- China PNTR taught as an open debate: the "trade will open China up"
  argument in Clinton's own words (State Dept), the counter-argument, and
  Marcus's explicit "I don't think that's settled."
- Fukuyama taught as the ideological-settlement claim, not "no more wars" —
  Maya's "So he said history was over?" gets the firm correction, and the
  critics' "premature" verdict is attributed.
