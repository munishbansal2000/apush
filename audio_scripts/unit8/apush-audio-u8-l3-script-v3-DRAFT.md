Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: the Wall fell and the Soviet Union collapsed. Marcus promised the nineties: globalization, the tech boom, an economy going worldwide. The dare: explain why the longest boom in American history — at the time, anyway — left most paychecks flat. This time: how the trade machinery redrew the map, how the tech boom rewired daily life, who won the surplus fight, and why a decade declared history finished. Four boxes: NAFTA and the WTO, the tech boom and the dot-com bust, the nineties economy, the winners and losers. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. About eleven and a half minutes, pauses and all.

Marcus: Start with the machine. In the nineties, Washington stopped just trading with the world and started writing the rules of world trade. The first rulebook was regional: the North American Free Trade Agreement, NAFTA, negotiated under President Bush and pushed through Congress by President Clinton. It wiped out tariffs between the United States, Canada, and Mexico, and it took effect on New Year's Day, 1994.

Maya: Wait, so NAFTA was Clinton's big idea?

Marcus: [firm] Common mix-up, and it costs students points. Bush negotiated it; Clinton pushed it through Congress, because he believed cutting tariffs would ride the wave of what he called the growing globalization of the American economy. The unions hated it. They said factories would chase cheaper labor south, and corporate interests lined up behind it.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And that's where the giant sucking sound comes in?

Marcus: That's Ross Perot, the Texas billionaire running as an independent in ninety-two. In the presidential debates he warned that NAFTA would produce a "giant sucking sound" of American jobs draining into Mexico, where factory wages were a fraction of ours. He pulled nineteen million votes, nineteen percent, the best third-party showing since Teddy Roosevelt's Bull Moose run in 1912.

Maya: Exam move: if a prompt hands you the "giant sucking sound" line, anchor it: ninety-two, third-party candidate, the trade warning. The quote is the argument.

Marcus: The second rulebook was global. In 1995 the World Trade Organization opened its doors, replacing GATT, the looser postwar framework that had been lowering tariffs since the late forties. The WTO set worldwide rules for trade between nations and ran a formal system for settling trade disputes.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Your turn. Trade is getting freer and everything in the mall is getting cheaper. So why did tens of thousands of people march on Seattle in ninety-nine to fight it?

[10-second pause]

Marcus: Because "cheaper" has a cost side. The WTO held its big ministerial in Seattle in late ninety-nine, and the meeting got hit by a coalition nobody had seen before: the AFL-CIO and other labor unions, student groups, NGOs, farm and industrial workers. Their case was that WTO rules put corporate interests ahead of workers and the environment. The protests disrupted the whole ministerial. Some historians call Seattle the birth of the anti-globalization movement.

Maya: And then China. Trade with Beijing was supposed to be the grand finale?

Marcus: The China fight was the loudest trade vote of the decade, and it happened in 2000. Congress voted to grant China permanent normal trade relations (PNTR), and Clinton signed it that October. The word that mattered was "permanent." The supporters' pitch was simple: trade will open China up. New markets, new prosperity, and political change to follow.

Maya: Did it? Trade was supposed to open China up — did it?

Marcus: I don't think that's settled, and I'm not going to pretend it is. The supporters believed opening would change China; the critics said American workers would eat the cheap-import costs and the labor and human-rights standards got traded away. China joined the WTO the next year. Twenty-six years on, both sides still claim the evidence.

Maya: On the exam, date the China PNTR fight to 2000. China's WTO entry came in 2001.

Marcus: Box two is the revolution nobody voted for. Start in a bedroom with a modem. The personal computer had already spread through the eighties, but in the nineties the internet went commercial, and daily life started rewiring. Watch the names arrive like stations on a dial: Amazon in ninety-four, selling books online; eBay in ninety-five, the auction site; Google in ninety-eight, search.

Maya: I know this part from the Beanie Baby wars. [chuckle] My mom sat at the kitchen computer refreshing an eBay auction for a stuffed bear she could've bought at the mall. She was bidding against strangers at midnight. That was the internet becoming real life.

Marcus: The lived experience changed in one big jump: dial-up to broadband. Dial-up meant one phone line and a screeching handshake that died every time someone picked up the receiver. Broadband meant always on, no waiting, no screech. The web stopped being a place you visited and became the default.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] The tech was real, though. So how did a real revolution turn into a stock-market delusion?

Marcus: The boom first. Companies with no profits were valued like giants, because investors decided the internet changed the math. Even Alan Greenspan, the Fed chairman, warned about "irrational exuberance" in ninety-six. Then came the peak. [speaking slowly] March of 2000. The NASDAQ stock index closed just over five thousand. And then it crashed, and by 2002 it had lost roughly three-quarters of its value.

Maya: [surprised] So the internet died?

Marcus: The bubble died. The internet kept growing. That distinction is the whole box: the stocks crashed, the web kept adding users every year.

Maya: Two down. Checking that one.

Marcus: Box three is the economy everybody remembers fondly and nobody should remember simply. The expansion ran one hundred twenty months, March ninety-one to March 2001, the longest in American history at the time. Unemployment dipped below four percent, the lowest since 1969. And the federal budget ran surpluses four straight years, ninety-eight through 2001.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Who gets the credit? That smells like a campaign ad.

Marcus: Both parties spent the next decade fighting over exactly that. The honest version: the peace dividend helped, because defense spending fell after the Cold War ended. Clinton's 1993 tax increase on higher earners helped. The boom itself generated enormous revenues. And the 1997 budget deal with a Republican Congress helped. Democrats pointed at the ninety-three taxes; Republicans pointed at spending restraint. Nobody's fully sure where the boom ends and the policy begins.

Maya: [incredulous] So the surplus wiped out the national debt?

Marcus: [firm] Four good years put a dent in the debt, and then the red ink came roaring back.

Maya: But the paychecks. If everything's booming, where's my raise?

Marcus: Mostly nowhere. That's the shadow under the box: average wages barely moved for most workers through the boom. Union membership fell from about one worker in five in 1980 to about one in eight by 2000. The people who owned stock did very well. The people who drew a paycheck mostly held still.

Maya: Exam habit: when a prompt asks what made the nineties economy unusual, pair the surplus with the flat wages. The contrast is the point. The surplus shrank the debt for four years, and that was the whole story.

Marcus: Box four is the verdict, and the exam wants this thread: who won the new economy. Stockholders won. Exporters won. Consumers got cheaper goods. Wage earners and factory towns lost ground. The whole unit keeps circling back to that inequality story.

Maya: And the mood everywhere was that history had just... closed up shop?

Marcus: That was the mood, and it had a famous name. In 1992 Francis Fukuyama published The End of History and the Last Man. His argument: with communism gone, the big ideological argument was settled. Liberal democracy and market economies had won, and no rival system was left standing. Call it the nineties' holiday from history.

Maya: [incredulous] So he said history was over? No more wars?

Marcus: [firm] His claim was narrower than the legend: the ideological argument was settled, and events kept coming anyway. The critics called even that premature. Because the warnings were already piling up. In 1993, Islamic extremists set off a bomb at the World Trade Center. In 1998, bin Laden's Al Qaeda bombed the American embassies in Tanzania and Kenya, killing hundreds; Clinton's cruise missiles missed bin Laden. Somalia, ninety-three: eighteen Americans dead, troops pulled out. Rwanda, ninety-four: genocide, and America did nothing. Bosnia, then Kosovo in ninety-nine, where NATO finally bombed Serbia to stop ethnic cleansing.

Maya: The Balkans got the bombs; Rwanda got the silence.

Marcus: [confident tone] The moral limit of American attention in the decade: intervene where the cameras and the alliances pointed, look away where they didn't. The holiday from history was an illusion, and the nineties kept filing the evidence.

Maya: The stockholders won, the wage earners stalled, and history refused to end on schedule.

Maya: [sheepish] Four boxes, let's land them. Box one: the globalization machinery. NAFTA took effect in ninety-four, negotiated by Bush and signed into law by Clinton, and the WTO opened in — ninety-four? No wait, ninety-five. Perot's ninety-two "giant sucking sound" warning. Seattle, ninety-nine — some historians call it the birth of the anti-globalization movement. China PNTR, twenty hundred, the vote that divided Washington.

Marcus: Twenty hundred. October 2000. China joined the WTO in 2001.

Maya: Box two: the tech revolution. Amazon ninety-four, eBay ninety-five, Google ninety-eight. Dial-up to broadband. The NASDAQ peaked in March 2000 and crashed. The internet kept growing. Box three: the nineties economy. One hundred twenty months of growth, unemployment under four percent, four straight surpluses. Underneath those numbers, wages barely moved for most workers while union membership kept sliding. The debt stood through all of it. Box four: the verdict. Stockholders won, wage earners stalled, Fukuyama's end of history, and the warnings: ninety-three, ninety-eight, and Rwanda's lesson about the limits of American attention.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One: an SAQ hands you Perot's "giant sucking sound" line from the ninety-two debate and asks what concern it expresses.

[10-second pause]

Maya: The line warns that NAFTA will drain American factory jobs to Mexico, so the concern is cheap-labor competition. You can read it right in the phrasing, jobs "going south," and his nineteen percent of the vote shows the fear was mainstream. The reason it stuck is that it turns an abstract trade deal into a kitchen-table argument.

Maya: Two: the budget runs surpluses from ninety-eight to 2001, and most workers' wages barely move. What's the AP point of putting those two facts side by side?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The boom had two faces. Four straight surpluses sat next to flat wages and falling union numbers, which means GDP and paychecks are different stories. The nineties are the decade that shows it.

Maya: Three, stimulus-style: imagine a cartoon from ninety-nine — a protester in Seattle smashing a corporate logo shaped like a globe. What's the point of this source?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The cartoonist sides with the protesters: corporations pocket the gains, workers absorb the costs. The corporate logo and the Seattle setting in ninety-nine give it away, right at the WTO ministerial. It marks the moment the trade debate moved from Congress to the streets. One more, fast: Fukuyama's "end of history" — what did he actually claim?

Maya: That liberal democracy had won for good — communism gone, no rival system left standing. The nineties kept proving the holiday wrong: the bombings, the Balkans, Rwanda.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Check your boxes. Next time: the holiday ends — September 2001. [production note: do not rush the closing line]

Marcus: The decade America declared history over.

Maya: History had other plans.
