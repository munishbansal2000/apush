Marcus: The Spanish had been in New Mexico since the 1590s, and the Franciscans ran the missions to replace Pueblo religion with Catholicism. The kivas, the underground ceremonial rooms, were banned. Masks and sacred objects were burned.

Maya: And the missions weren't just churches, right? The economy ran through them too.

Marcus: That's the other half of box one. Pueblo labor built the mission farms. On top of that, the Spanish encomienda system pulled Pueblo men into labor for colonists. Religion and work were one machine, and the kivas were where the Pueblos still controlled the day.

Maya: Give me the extremes. What did the crackdown actually look like?

Marcus: Ceremonies banned, kivas filled in, ritual objects seized and burned. Medicine men, the Pueblo religious leaders, were rounded up as sorcerers. But the old religion never died. It went underground.

Marcus: Common mistake for box one: writing that Spain banned Pueblo religion in 1680. The ban built over decades, and the old religion never died. It went underground.

Maya: Exam warning, can I take this one? If an SAQ asks why they revolted, "religion" alone is a half answer. The graders want the kivas AND the fields.

Marcus: Both. And after eighty years of it, the 1670s turned brutal: drought year after year, Apache raids hammering the pueblos, and the Spanish unable to stop them.

Maya: So the empire couldn't protect them, but it could still punish them for praying.

Marcus: That's the sentence. The bargain of empire is protection for obedience, and Spain was defaulting on its half.

Marcus: The spark came in 1675. Governor Treviño arrested forty-seven medicine men for practicing sorcery. Forty-seven men, for praying wrong. Four got death sentences.

Maya: And the sentences?

Marcus: Three were hanged. One killed himself in prison. The rest were whipped in public and thrown in jail.

Maya: A crackdown that doubled as a recruitment poster.

Marcus: It was. Then the pueblos marched on Santa Fe and demanded the prisoners back. The soldiers were away fighting Apaches. The governor folded, and every prisoner walked free.

Maya: Wait, among them was Popé, and I know this one. There's a statue of him in the U.S. Capitol. I saw it on the eighth-grade trip.

Marcus: Popé, carved by Cliff Fragua in 2005: a Tewa religious leader from Ohkay Owingeh. And no, he wasn't a king.

Maya: I was about to say "the Pueblo king."

Marcus: Popé was a holy man, not a ruler, and the pueblos had never had a single king. He went north to Taos, out of Santa Fe's reach, and spent five years planning.

Maya: Five years holding a secret across half a dozen languages and hundreds of miles of desert?

Marcus: Your turn. You're Popé. Half a dozen languages, hundreds of miles, and anything you write down, the Spanish can read. How do you set one date for every pueblo to rise?

[9-second pause]

Marcus: A calendar made of knots. Runners carried cords of maguey fiber, a tough desert plant, to each pueblo. One knot for each day. When the last knot comes loose, everybody rises the same day.

Maya: I did that on a road trip once. Untied a knot in my shoelace every hour so I'd know when we were close. It did not start a revolution.

Marcus: Yours lacked the runners, who could cross a desert the Spanish could barely map. The Spanish did catch two, runners from Tesuque, so Popé moved the date up. On August 10th, 1680, the pueblos rose.

Maya: Hold on, how do we know about the cords? Did Popé leave notes?

Marcus: A Spanish interrogator wrote it down. A Pueblo man named Pedro Naranjo, questioned after the revolt, described the cord, the knots, the runners. We're hearing the plan through Spanish pens, and that matters.

Marcus: Naranjo said what drove Popé. In a ceremonial room at Taos, three spirits visited him, figures with fire shooting from their bodies, commanding him to purge the Spanish, and the world would be made whole: rain, crops, health.

Maya: And we believe the fire part?

Marcus: The Spanish records say he claimed it, and that's the honest version. The spirits are Popé's claim, carried to us by men who wanted him hanged. The runners' message isn't in doubt: the gods are done waiting.

Marcus: And it worked. On August 10th the pueblos rose almost everywhere at once: close to four hundred Spaniards killed, twenty-one priests. They besieged Santa Fe and cut the water. For days the Spanish held the plaza while the pueblos held everything else, until the governor, Otermín, abandoned the city. On August 21st he led them south to El Paso, the whole Spanish presence in New Mexico gone on foot. The Spanish wrote it all down, including the parts that made them look bad.

Maya: Second box checked. The cord, the date, the siege.

Marcus: And here's your timing trap: 1680 is the rising. 1692 is the return. Exam writers love swapping them.

Marcus: By late August, New Mexico was Pueblo land again. It stayed that way for twelve years: the missions empty, the old dances back in the open. One of the most successful Native uprisings, and the one that held an entire Spanish province.

Maya: So that's a permanent win?

Marcus: It was a twelve-year win, not a permanent one. Popé ordered everything Spanish destroyed: churches, crosses, baptismal names. Spanish records quote him saying the Christians' God was dead, "made of rotten wood," as the scribes wrote it down. Converts scrubbed themselves with yucca to wash off baptism.

Maya: The purge is on. What cracks first, the drought or Popé?

Marcus: Then the revolution ate its leader. The drought kept killing crops, old feuds came back, and within a few years Popé was deposed: too much control, too little rain. The Spanish called him a tyrant, which is exactly what you'd expect the losers to write about the winner. But the pueblos re-elected him in 1688, and he died not long after. Britannica gives 1692.

Maya: So what actually went wrong with "died a tyrant in 1688"?

Marcus: Nearly everything. Within a few years the pueblos deposed him: too much control, too little rain. Then they re-elected him in 1688, and he died not long after. Britannica gives 1692. And the trap: don't write that Popé led the pueblos for twelve years; he didn't. The pueblos held New Mexico for twelve years.

Maya: Third box. The Spanish come back. Vargas walks into Santa Fe in 1692 with a small force. The pueblos have held the province for twelve years. Does he fight his way in or talk his way in?

[9-second pause]

Marcus: He talked. Pardons for the rising, respect for Pueblo lands, at least on paper. The pueblos swore allegiance, and by the Spanish accounts it was bloodless. Twelve years after the cords, the Spanish walked back through the front door.

Maya: That sounds too clean.

Marcus: It was. A year later Vargas was back with hundreds of colonists, and this time Santa Fe fell by force. The Spanish accounts record dozens of Pueblo men executed.

Marcus: Common mistake for box three: writing that Vargas retook Santa Fe by force in 1692. He talked his way in the first time, with pardons and promises; the force came the second time around.

Maya: So Spain wins eventually. Why isn't the lesson "resistance fails"?

Marcus: Because of what Spain did differently this time. The empire that came back was chastened by fear: the crown had watched an entire province rise and walk out, and it didn't want a second lesson. Each Pueblo family got land under Spanish law. A public defender was appointed to protect Pueblo rights in Spanish courts. And the ceremonies, the kivas and the dances, were tolerated in a way they'd never been.

Maya: Not nicer, then.

It forced the empire to change how it ruled. Before 1680: masks burned, kivas banned, medicine men hanged. After: the ceremonies survive, and they're still practiced today.
