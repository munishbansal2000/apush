# Pronunciation: Popé (poh-PAY), Otermín (oh-ter-MEEN), Treviño (treh-VEE-nyo), Vargas (VAR-gahs), kiva (KEE-vah), Tewa (TAY-wah), Ohkay Owingeh (oh-KAY oh-WIN-geh), maguey (mah-GAY), yucca (YOO-kah), Tesuque (teh-SOO-keh), Fragua (FRAH-gwah).

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: Valladolid, 1550: Las Casas and Sepúlveda arguing over whether conquering the Americas was even just. This time, the conquered answer back. August, 1680: runners are crossing the New Mexico desert carrying knotted cords. One knot for every day. When the last knot comes loose, every pueblo rises at once. Three boxes: the mission system, Popé's Rebellion, and the reconquest bargain. Circle the ones you couldn't explain. Twelve minutes. Santa Fe has no idea.

Marcus: The Spanish had been in New Mexico since the 1590s, and the Franciscans ran the missions to replace Pueblo religion with Catholicism. The kivas, the underground ceremonial rooms, were banned. Masks and sacred objects were burned.

Maya: And the missions weren't just churches, right? The economy ran through them too.

Marcus: That's the other half of box one. Pueblo labor built the mission farms. On top of that, the Spanish encomienda system pulled Pueblo men into labor for colonists. Religion and work were one machine, and the kivas were where the Pueblos still controlled the day.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Give me the extremes. What did the crackdown actually look like?

Marcus: Ceremonies banned, kivas filled in, ritual objects seized and burned. Medicine men, the Pueblo religious leaders, were rounded up as sorcerers. But the old religion never died. It went underground.

Maya: Wait, so the ban on Pueblo religion started in 1680? Same year as the revolt?

Marcus: [firm] Not quite. The ban built over decades. Spain had been burning masks and filling kivas since the 1590s. And the old religion never died. It went underground.

Maya: [playful] Exam warning, can I take this one? If an SAQ asks why they revolted, "religion" alone is a half answer. The graders want the kivas AND the fields.

Marcus: Both. And after eighty years of it, the 1670s turned brutal: drought year after year, Apache raids hammering the pueblos, and the Spanish unable to stop them.

Maya: So the empire couldn't protect them, but it could still punish them for praying.

Marcus: That's the sentence. The bargain of empire is protection for obedience, and Spain was defaulting on its half.

Marcus: The spark came in 1675. Governor Treviño arrested forty-seven medicine men for practicing sorcery. Forty-seven men, for praying wrong. Four got death sentences.

Maya: And the sentences?

Marcus: [serious tone] Three were hanged. One killed himself in prison. The rest were whipped in public and thrown in jail.

Maya: A crackdown that doubled as a recruitment poster.

Marcus: It was. Then the pueblos marched on Santa Fe and demanded the prisoners back. The soldiers were away fighting Apaches. The governor folded, and every prisoner walked free.

Maya: [incredulous] Wait, among them was Popé, and I know this one. There's a statue of him in the U.S. Capitol. I saw it on the eighth-grade trip.

Marcus: Popé, carved by Cliff Fragua in 2005: a Tewa religious leader from Ohkay Owingeh. And no, he wasn't a king.

Maya: [catching] I was about to say "the Pueblo king."

Marcus: Popé was a holy man, not a ruler, and the pueblos had never had a single king. He went north to Taos, out of Santa Fe's reach, and spent five years planning.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Five years holding a secret across half a dozen languages and hundreds of miles of desert?

Marcus: Your turn. You're Popé. Half a dozen languages, hundreds of miles, and anything you write down, the Spanish can read. How do you set one date for every pueblo to rise?

[10-second pause]

Marcus: A calendar made of knots. Runners carried cords of maguey fiber, a tough desert plant, to each pueblo. One knot for each day. When the last knot comes loose, everybody rises the same day.

Maya: [conversational] I did that on a road trip once. Untied a knot in my shoelace every hour so I'd know when we were close. It did not start a revolution.

Marcus: Yours lacked the runners, who could cross a desert the Spanish could barely map. The Spanish did catch two, runners from Tesuque, so Popé moved the date up. On August 10th, 1680, the pueblos rose.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Hold on, how do we know about the cords? Did Popé leave notes?

Marcus: A Spanish interrogator wrote it down. A Pueblo man named Pedro Naranjo, questioned after the revolt, described the cord, the knots, the runners. We're hearing the plan through Spanish pens, and that matters.

Marcus: Naranjo said what drove Popé. In a ceremonial room at Taos, three spirits visited him, figures with fire shooting from their bodies, commanding him to purge the Spanish, and the world would be made whole: rain, crops, health.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And we believe the fire part?

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] The Spanish records say he claimed it, and that's the honest version. The spirits are Popé's claim, carried to us by men who wanted him hanged. The runners' message isn't in doubt: the gods are done waiting.

Maya: The gods are done waiting — sent by runner, on a knotted cord. A terrifying message to carry.

Marcus: [dramatic] And it worked. On August 10th the pueblos rose almost everywhere at once: close to four hundred Spaniards killed, twenty-one priests. They besieged Santa Fe and cut the water. For days the Spanish held the plaza while the pueblos held everything else, until the governor, Otermín, abandoned the city. On August 21st he led them south to El Paso, the whole Spanish presence in New Mexico gone on foot. The Spanish wrote it all down, including the parts that made them look bad.

Maya: [confident tone] Second box checked. The cord, the date, the siege.

Marcus: [firm] And here's your timing trap: 1680 is the rising. 1692 is the return — the swap the answer choices will try on you.

Marcus: By late August, New Mexico was Pueblo land again. It stayed that way for twelve years: the missions empty, the old dances back in the open. One of the most successful Native uprisings, and the one that held an entire Spanish province.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So that's a permanent win?

Marcus: It was a twelve-year win, not a permanent one. Popé ordered everything Spanish destroyed: churches, crosses, baptismal names. Spanish records quote him saying the Christians' God was dead, "made of rotten wood," as the scribes wrote it down. Converts scrubbed themselves with yucca to wash off baptism.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] The purge is on. What cracks first, the drought or Popé?

Marcus: Then the revolution ate its leader. The drought kept killing crops, old feuds came back, and within a few years Popé was deposed: too much control, too little rain. The Spanish called him a tyrant, which is what you'd expect the losers to write about the winner. After that the records go quiet on him. Britannica gives 1692.

Maya: So Popé led the pueblos for twelve years straight — then died 1692?

Marcus: [firm] Not exactly — he didn't lead the pueblos for twelve years. The pueblos deposed him within a few years, and the records go quiet after that. The pueblos held New Mexico for twelve years. Popé didn't. Britannica gives his death as 1692.

Maya: Third box. The Spanish come back. Vargas walks into Santa Fe in 1692 with a small force. The pueblos have held the province for twelve years. Does he fight his way in or talk his way in?

[10-second pause]

Marcus: He talked. Pardons for the rising, respect for Pueblo lands, at least on paper. The pueblos swore allegiance, and by the Spanish accounts it was bloodless. Twelve years after the cords, the Spanish walked back through the front door.

Maya: That sounds too clean.

Marcus: [serious tone] It was. A year later Vargas was back with hundreds of colonists, and this time Santa Fe fell by force. The Spanish accounts record dozens of Pueblo men executed.

Maya: So Vargas retook Santa Fe by force in 1692.

Marcus: [firm] Not quite. 1692 was the talking. Pardons and promises; the Spanish accounts call it bloodless. The force came the second time around, in 1693.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So Spain wins eventually. Why isn't the lesson "resistance fails"?

Marcus: Because of what Spain did differently this time. The empire that came back was chastened by fear: the crown had watched an entire province rise and walk out, and it didn't want a second lesson. Each Pueblo got substantial land grants under Spanish law. A public defender was appointed to protect Pueblo rights in Spanish courts. And the ceremonies, the kivas and the dances, were tolerated in a way they'd never been.

Maya: Not nicer, then.

Marcus: Scared is the word. The exam spine of this whole episode, said plain: this is the unit's proof that resistance worked. The revolt didn't hold New Mexico forever. It forced the empire to change how it ruled. Before 1680: masks burned, kivas banned, medicine men hanged. After: the ceremonies survive, and they're still practiced today.

Maya: The reconquest bargain: land, courts, tolerated ceremonies, bought with twelve years of freedom.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: the mission system: the friars banned the kivas and burned the masks, and Pueblo labor ran the whole machine. The 1675 arrests were the spark: forty-seven medicine men, and Popé walked out of that jail with a plan.

Maya: Two: Popé's Rebellion: the knotted cord, one knot per day, August 10th 1680. Close to four hundred Spaniards dead, twenty-one of them priests.

Maya: [sheepish] And Santa Fe emptied by August 21st. Three: the reconquest bargain: twelve years free, then Vargas in 1692 with promises and back with soldiers in — 1694?

Marcus: 1693.

Maya: [confident tone] 1693. Bloodless the first time, bloody the second, and a chastened empire that granted land and left the kivas alone. Checking that one. Two down. And the third one's checked.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. And on the long essay: this episode is your "resistance worked" paragraph. When the prompt asks whether Native resistance changed colonial rule, the Pueblo Revolt is your evidence: not just that they fought, but that the empire bent.

Maya: One. A lot of textbooks frame the Pueblo Revolt as a religious war, the friars versus the kivas. Using evidence from the episode, argue it was about more than religion.

[10-second pause]

Maya: Labor and land drove it too — Pueblo workers built the mission farms, the encomienda pulled Pueblo men into labor for colonists, and the 1670s drought and Apache raids broke the empire's protection bargain. Which is why the revolt targeted the whole colonial machine, priests and settlers alike, not just the friars.

Maya: Two. A Pueblo man interrogated by the Spanish in 1681 said Popé was told to "make a cord of maguey fiber and tie some knots in it which would signify the number of days that they must wait before the rebellion." What's the point of this source? What does it tell you about the revolt?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The revolt was centrally planned — the cord was a countdown every pueblo could read without a shared language, carried by runners across hundreds of miles. And the proof is a source the Spanish recorded themselves: this wasn't spontaneous rage, it was organized, which is why it succeeded where earlier plots never got off the ground.

Maya: Three. A student writes: "The Pueblo Revolt failed because the Spanish returned in 1692." Why would a grader mark that wrong?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The revolt succeeded in its deeper aim — twelve years of self-rule, and when Spain returned it issued land grants to each Pueblo, appointed a public defender, and tolerated the banned ceremonies. So "failed" measures the wrong thing: the revolt forced the empire to change how it ruled.

Maya: One more, fast. An LEQ asks whether Native resistance changed colonial rule. You've got the 1680 rising and the twelve free years. Which one leads your paragraph, and why?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The twelve years. 1680 proves they could fight; the twelve years prove the empire had to change — land grants, a public defender, tolerated ceremonies. Only the twelve years answer the prompt.

Maya: [intrigued] One thing to carry forward: the story of this continent isn't only defeat. Sometimes the conquered organized, counted down the days in knots, and took a whole province back — and the empire that returned was never the same. Check your three boxes.
