# Pronunciation: POW-uh-tan; poh-kuh-HON-tuhs; BUR-juh-sez; oh-pech-an-KAH-noh; DEL-uh-wair; ROLF (rhymes with golf).

Maya: Last time: what crossed the Atlantic in both directions, and the cargo nobody chose. This time: England starts planting, not just raiding. Jamestown, 1607. A company town that starved, nearly quit, and survived. Four boxes: Jamestown, tobacco, the House of Burgesses, and 1619, the year the assembly and the slave ship arrived together. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Twelve minutes, and you'll check all four off.

Marcus: Start with the money, because no king paid for Jamestown. London merchants formed a joint-stock company, the Virginia Company: a pool of private money chasing a profit. James the First signed the charter in 1606 — the crown's permission, in writing. But the cash was theirs, and so was the risk.

Maya: Wait, I always assumed the crown just sent them. The king's colony, the king's money.
Marcus: The king's charter, the merchants' money. Nobody's money but theirs was on the line — which is exactly why the payoff could be enormous. Private money, private risk. That was the whole deal.

Maya: So who actually got on the boats?
Marcus: About a hundred and five men and boys, on three ships. They landed in the spring of 1607 on a swampy peninsula up the James River. And the site was a trade-off: easy to defend, with deep water right at the shore, and terrible for everything else. Marsh, mosquitoes, and brackish river water.

Maya: I got sick off lake water on one camping trip in fifth grade, and I still won't drink from a thermos that smells funny. These people had no choice.
Marcus: They paid for it. Bad water, fever, dysentery. Within months, more than half of them were dead. And here's the part that stings: most were gentlemen. Sons of gentry, jewelers, men who'd never held a plow. They came hunting gold, not planting wheat. One of them wrote that in camp there was nothing but …dig gold, refine gold, load gold.

Maya: Digging for gold. In Virginia.
Marcus: Iron pyrite, mostly. Fool's gold. Which is where John Smith comes in. He takes over in the fall of 1608 and lays down the law: "He that will not work, shall not eat." Plant, build, or starve.

Maya: Cold.
Marcus: It worked. Then, in the fall of 1609, Smith's gunpowder bag catches fire. He's badly burned, and he sails home to England. The colony falls apart without him.

Maya: What about the famous rescue? Pocahontas saving Smith's life?
Marcus: Smith told that story years later: captured by the Powhatan and about to be executed, when the chief's daughter steps in. Some scholars think he made it up. Others think it was a ceremony he misunderstood, an adoption, not an execution. Nobody's fully sure.

Maya: Your turn. A camp full of gentlemen who came hunting gold, nobody planting anything, and the one man enforcing work just sailed home. It's the winter of 1609. What happens next?

[10-second pause]

Maya: They starve. About five hundred settlers go into that winter. About sixty come out. They ate dogs, horses, shoe leather. The archaeology says some ate each other.

Marcus: The starving time. And in the spring of 1610, the survivors actually quit. They boarded ships for England, and on the way out they ran into the new governor, De La Warr, sailing in with three ships, about a hundred and fifty fresh colonists, and supplies. He turned them around.

Maya: Imagine quitting and running into your replacement on the way out the door. Wait — Delaware. That's him, right? The state?
Marcus: The bay, the river, and the state. All named for him.

Maya: So the exam question writes itself. Why did Jamestown almost die: the swamp, or the people?
Marcus: The people. The swamp hurt, but the gold-fever killed. If a question asks for the cause, pick the gentlemen who wouldn't plant over the geography.

Maya: And the common mistake for box one: don't write that the king funded Jamestown. London merchants did, on a royal charter.

Marcus: So how does this place not just die forever? One plant. John Rolfe, the same man who'd later marry Pocahontas, starts growing tobacco around 1612. Not the harsh native leaf. A sweeter Caribbean strain, from seeds out of Trinidad.

Maya: Rolfe the tobacco guy is Rolfe the husband guy.
Marcus: Same guy. And it worked: within a few years, Virginia tobacco was selling in London, and the colony finally had a reason to exist. The answer was a weed.

Maya: Didn't the king hate smoking, though?
Marcus: James wrote a whole pamphlet against it in 1604, A Counterblaste to Tobacco. Called it a vile custom. James's pamphlet never stood a chance against the money.

Maya: So the Disney version: how much of it survives?
Marcus: Almost none of it. The English captured Pocahontas in 1613. She married Rolfe in 1614, and that bought a few years of peace. She died in England in 1617, about twenty years old.

Maya: Okay, but a plant doesn't plant itself. Who's working these fields?
Marcus: Tobacco needed land and workers, and workers needed paying. The company's answer, in 1618, was the headright: fifty acres to anyone who paid a settler's passage. Bring ten workers, claim five hundred acres.

Maya: So the workers each got fifty acres for coming over?
Marcus: The person who paid got the acres. The boss, not the boat ride. The engine ran like this: planters import workers, workers become acres, acres grow tobacco.

Maya: And the workers signed up for this?
Marcus: They signed away four to seven years of labor for the passage over: indentured servants, most of them young, poor, and English. The majority of English migrants to the Chesapeake came this way.

Maya: Your turn. Tobacco exhausts the soil fast. Every few years, a planter needs fresh fields. Fresh fields have to come from somewhere. Where?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Powhatan land. The colony keeps planting past every boundary it agreed to. Remember that. It's loaded for later.

Marcus: One exam warning for box two: when a question pairs tobacco with a labor system, connect them instead of memorizing two facts. The headright buys the passage; the indenture pays it off.

Maya: And the common mistake: the headright gave land to the planter who paid, not the servant who sailed. Two boxes down.

Marcus: Now, who's running this place? For the first decade, the company, from London. Then, in the summer of 1619, the company lets the colony elect an assembly: the House of Burgesses. Burgess just means representative.

Maya: The company just handed over power?
Marcus: It traded a little power for a lot of settlers. An elected assembly made Virginia look stable and permanent. That permanence is what turned cautious investors into steady profits back in London.

Maya: So 1619 is the democracy year.
Marcus: Careful. At first, all free adult men could vote, but the franchise narrowed over time, and it never included women, servants, or anyone enslaved. It's self-government, not democracy as we'd mean it.

Maya: Common mistake for box three, then: don't write that everyone voted. Free men voted, and even that narrowed.
Marcus: And the exam point: if a question asks what the Burgesses prove, the answer is that English colonists expected a say in their government, even under a company charter, an ocean from the king.

Maya: Same year, though — 1619. Wait, wasn't something else arriving that year too?
Marcus: Late that August, a privateer ship called the White Lion pulled into Point Comfort. A privateer is a pirate with a government's permission slip. This one sailed under Dutch papers and carried about twenty Africans, seized from a Portuguese slave ship. Rolfe wrote it down himself: "20 and odd" Africans, traded for food.

Maya: So that's the start of slavery in Virginia?
Marcus: The start of Africans in Virginia, and the status is murky. On paper they were sold as servants. In reality they'd been stolen twice over, and nobody asked them. But most field workers for decades after were still indentured servants, not enslaved Africans. The full racial slave system comes later in the century.

Maya: Same year. An assembly votes, and a slave ship lands.
Marcus: The contradiction at the start of English America. And it gets bloodier. Tobacco keeps eating land, the English keep planting past the boundary lines, and in March of 1622, Opechancanough, Powhatan's successor, coordinates a surprise attack across the plantations. Three hundred and forty-seven dead. About a quarter of the colony, in a single day.

Maya: A quarter. The Powhatan just snapped?
Marcus: They'd watched the English plant past every boundary for fifteen years. "Snapped" makes it sound sudden. It wasn't.

Marcus: The English answered with years of war: poisoned wine at a parley the next year, burned villages. And back in London, the king had seen enough. In 1624, James revoked the company's charter and made Virginia a royal colony. But Jamestown survived, because by then tobacco paid for everything.

Maya: One more exam warning, box four: the 1619 double event is built for a compare question. An assembly voting while a slave ship unloads — same year. If they ask what it shows, the answer is the contradiction starting early.

Maya: And the common mistake: 1619 didn't make Virginia a slave colony overnight. Servants still worked most of the fields for decades.

Maya: All four are on the board. Four boxes, let's land them. One: Jamestown. Sixteen-oh-seven, company money, swamp site, gold-fever gentlemen. Smith's rule held it together until 1609, the starving time nearly finished it, and De La Warr turned the quitters around in 1610.

Maya: Box one, checked. Two: tobacco. Rolfe's Caribbean strain, around 1612. The headright hands fifty acres a head to whoever pays the passage, the planter who paid, not the servant who sailed, and indentured servants work the fields for four to seven years.

Maya: Box two, checked. Three: the House of Burgesses, summer 1619. First elected assembly in English America. Free men voted at first. Not everyone, and it narrowed.

Maya: Box three, checked. Four: 1619. The White Lion lands "20 and odd" Africans at Point Comfort. And that's when the slave system starts?

Marcus: The first Africans arrive that year. The system comes decades later.

Maya: Right. Arrivals in 1619, system decades later. And three years after that, 1622: Opechancanough's attack kills about a quarter of the colony, and the company loses its charter in 1624.

Maya: Box four, checked.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it.

Maya: One. A colonist wrote that in the Jamestown camp there was nothing but …dig gold, refine gold, load gold. What's the point of this source: why did Jamestown nearly starve?

[15-second pause]

Maya: Claim: the settlers chased gold instead of planting food. Evidence: gentlemen hunting iron pyrite while the food stores ran out. Reasoning: the cause was human, not geographic. Gold-fever beat the swamp.

Maya: Two. A planter pays the passage for six servants and claims three hundred acres. Name the system, and say who benefits.

[15-second pause]

Maya: Claim: the headright system. Evidence: fifty acres a head, six heads, three hundred acres. Reasoning: the planter who paid benefits. The servants get years of labor, not land.

Maya: Three. 1619: an assembly meets in Jamestown, and the White Lion lands "20 and odd" Africans at Point Comfort. A historian pairs these two events. What's the argument?

[20-second pause]

Maya: Claim: self-government and forced labor arrived together. Evidence: the House of Burgesses and the White Lion, same year. Reasoning: the contradiction at the start of English America. The vote and the slave ship, side by side.

Maya: One more, fast. Virginia becomes a royal colony in 1624. Whose charter got revoked?

[5-second pause]

Maya: The Virginia Company's. The king took it back after the wars and the death toll.

Maya: One thing to carry forward: they came hunting gold, and a weed saved them. Then it demanded land and workers until it broke the peace. Check your four boxes. Next time: rewind sixty years, to the silver mountain that bankrolled the Spanish Empire.

Marcus: They sailed here hunting gold —
Maya: and ended up planting themselves.
