# APUSH Unit 1 FRQ Practice Pack
### Columbian Exchange · Spanish Labor & Caste · Valladolid Debate · Spanish vs. English Colonization

**How to use this pack (~35–40 minutes).** Do it in one sitting, timed, no notes:
- SAQ Set 1 — 12 minutes
- SAQ Set 2 — 12 minutes
- LEQ — 10 minutes, outline only (thesis + one paragraph plan), not a full essay
- DBQ skill set — 8 minutes

**One honest note before you start.** On the real exam, Unit 1 (1491–1607) is 4–6% of the test. It can show up in SAQs. It will almost never be the DBQ (the DBQ topic range starts at 1754) or a standalone LEQ. So this pack trains the formats where Unit 1 actually appears — SAQs — plus the *skills* the later-period essays demand (sourcing, comparison, contextualization) using Unit 1 sources you already know.

**Describe vs. explain — the distinction that decides SAQ points.** "Describe" or "identify" wants *what*: name it, characterize it, one clear detail. "Explain" wants *how or why*: the cause, the mechanism, the consequence. A response that only restates the question, or answers "explain" with another description, earns nothing. Every scoring note below enforces this line.

## SAQ Set 1 — The Columbian Exchange (Topic 1.4)

### Question 1 — secondary text source

*Secondary source (written for this pack, in the style of a textbook passage):*

> The Columbian Exchange moved more than goods; it moved biology, and biology did not move evenly. European crops and livestock took hold in the Americas, but the decisive transfer ran the other way in human cost: smallpox, measles, and influenza crossed the Atlantic westward into populations with no prior exposure, while no comparable epidemic traveled east. At the same time, American crops — maize, potatoes, tomatoes — reached Europe and helped feed a population boom there. The exchange enriched one continent's diet while devastating the other's people.

a. Identify ONE way the Columbian Exchange affected Europe.
b. Explain ONE reason disease spread mainly from east to west rather than the reverse.
c. Explain ONE long-term effect of the Columbian Exchange on Native societies.

**Scoring notes (1 point each, A/B/C independent).**
- (a) Earns the point: names a Europe-directed transfer *and* its effect — e.g., American crops (maize, potatoes, tomatoes) arriving in Europe and supporting population growth. Naming a crop alone, without the effect, is a describe-level answer and still earns this point, because (a) asks only to identify. Does not earn: listing something that went west (horses, wheat).
- (b) Earns the point: explains the mechanism — Native populations had never encountered these diseases, so they had no immunity; European populations had centuries of exposure. Must go beyond "Europeans brought disease" (that's description). Does not earn: "because Europeans were dirtier" or any version that reverses the direction.
- (c) Earns the point: a consequence tied to the exchange — e.g., catastrophic population loss (in the hardest-hit places, estimates run as high as eight or nine in ten) emptied villages and created the labor shortage that pushed Spain toward African slavery; *or* the horse remade Plains life. Must connect cause to consequence. Does not earn: "Native people died" with no link to a longer-term change.

**Full-credit exemplar.**
> (a) Maize, potatoes, and tomatoes from the Americas reached Europe and helped feed a population boom there. (b) Smallpox, measles, and influenza spread westward because Native populations had no prior exposure and therefore no immunity, while Europeans had lived with these diseases for centuries. (c) The population collapse emptied Native towns, which created the labor shortage that pushed Spain first to draft systems like the repartimiento and then to enslaved African labor.

### Question 2 — primary text source

*Primary source: Christopher Columbus, letter on his first voyage, 1493 (public domain; plainly rendered):*

> "They are a loving people, without covetousness. They love their neighbors as themselves, and they have the sweetest speech in the world, and are gentle and always laughing. They go naked, men and women, as their mothers bore them… They have no iron or steel or weapons, nor are they fit to use them."

a. Identify ONE European motive for exploration that forms the context for this letter.
b. Explain how the contact described in this letter contributed to the Columbian Exchange.
c. Explain ONE limitation of this letter as evidence about Native societies.

**Scoring notes.**
- (a) Earns the point: gold, God, or glory — e.g., the search for wealth/Asian trade routes, the Reconquista-era drive to convert new souls, or competition with Portugal. Any one, tied to 1492. Does not earn: "curiosity" alone.
- (b) Earns the point: explains the link — sustained contact after 1492 is what made the two-way transfer possible; without the encounter there is no exchange. Must say *how* contact leads to transfer, not just that both happened. Does not earn: "Columbus discovered America, so the exchange started" (that's sequencing, not mechanism).
- (c) Earns the point: a sourcing-based limitation — the letter was written to the Spanish monarchs to justify the voyage and secure backing, so it frames Native peoples as gentle and unarmed (useful subjects, easy to convert or control); *or* it reflects a few weeks of coastal encounters, not the diversity of Native societies. Identifying the audience alone is not enough — the point requires saying *how* the audience or context shapes the content. Does not earn: "it's old" or "he was biased" with no explanation.

**Full-credit exemplar.**
> (a) The drive for wealth — Spain wanted its own route to Asian spices and riches after Ottoman tariffs made the old land routes expensive. (b) The letter marks the start of sustained European presence in the Americas; only once Europeans kept coming — settling, planting, shipping — could crops, livestock, and diseases begin crossing the Atlantic in both directions. (c) Columbus wrote to the monarchs who funded him, so he had every reason to describe the peoples he met as gentle and easily governed — it sold the voyage as a good investment. A few weeks on Caribbean beaches also tells us nothing about the Pueblo, the Iroquois, or Cahokia.

### Question 3 — non-text source

*Non-text source (described): a modern textbook map summarizing the Columbian Exchange. Thick arrows cross the Atlantic westward labeled "smallpox, measles, influenza." Thinner arrows cross eastward labeled "maize, potatoes, tomatoes." Thinner arrows cross westward labeled "wheat, sugarcane, horses, cattle." One thin dashed arrow points east, labeled "syphilis? (debated)."*

a. Identify ONE item transferred from the Americas to Europe shown on the map.
b. Explain why the map's disease arrows point mostly in one direction.
c. Explain ONE way the map could mislead a student about the Columbian Exchange.

**Scoring notes.**
- (a) Earns the point: maize, potatoes, or tomatoes. Straight identification.
- (b) Earns the point: same mechanism as Q1(b) — no prior exposure, no immunity in the Americas. Accept a correct cross-reference in the student's own words.
- (c) Earns the point: any explained distortion — e.g., the clean arrows make the transfer look deliberate and instant, when it was uneven, took generations, and much of it (especially disease) was unintended; *or* the dashed syphilis arrow is honest about uncertainty, and a student who treats every arrow as equally certain is misreading the evidence. Must explain the *misleading effect*, not just notice a feature. Does not earn: "maps aren't accurate" or "it's too simple" without saying how.

**Full-credit exemplar.**
> (a) Potatoes. (b) The diseases shown — smallpox, measles, influenza — were Old World diseases; Native populations had never encountered them and had no immunity, so the epidemics ran westward. (c) The neat arrows make the exchange look planned and simultaneous, but most of it was unintended and unfolded over generations — nobody scheduled smallpox, and American crops took decades to remake European farming.

## SAQ Set 2 — Labor and Caste in Spanish America (Topics 1.5–1.6)

### Question 1 — secondary text source

*Secondary source (written for this pack, in the style of a textbook passage):*

> Spain never solved its labor problem; it replaced the system. The encomienda granted a colonist a town's labor outright until Las Casas pushed the crown to pass the New Laws of 1542, which the colonists nearly went to war over and the crown then gutted. The replacement, the repartimiento, was a rotating crown draft — workers assigned for a few weeks at a time — but disease kept emptying the towns it drafted from. So Spain turned to enslaved African labor across the Middle Passage, and by the 1600s the countryside settled into the hacienda, where debt rather than law kept peons on the estate. Above it all sat the casta hierarchy — peninsulares, criollos, mestizos, Indigenous peoples, enslaved Africans — enforced by custom and by law.

a. Identify ONE difference between the encomienda and the repartimiento.
b. Explain ONE reason Spain turned to enslaved African labor.
c. Explain how the casta system helped maintain Spanish colonial order.

**Scoring notes.**
- (a) Earns the point: grant vs. draft — the encomienda gave a colonist a town's labor outright; the repartimiento was a crown-run rotating draft for a few weeks at a time. One clear contrast. Does not earn: "the repartimiento came later" (sequencing, not a difference in how it worked).
- (b) Earns the point: a reason tied to the labor shortage — Native populations were collapsing from disease so the draft kept coming up short; Africans carried some immunity to Old World diseases; many came from farming societies; the Portuguese were already running the route. Any one, explained as a *reason Spain chose it*. Does not earn: "slavery is profitable" in the abstract.
- (c) Earns the point: explains the function — the hierarchy ranked everyone (who could hold office, who paid tribute), which divided the population and made the top of the ladder invested in defending it. Must explain *how* it maintained order, not just list the ranks. Does not earn: a bare list of peninsulares/criollos/mestizos.

**Full-credit exemplar.**
> (a) The encomienda handed a colonist a whole town's labor as a grant; the repartimiento was a crown draft that assigned workers for only a few weeks at a time. (b) Disease was emptying the Native towns the draft depended on, so the pool of workers kept shrinking — Spain needed a labor source that would not run out, and the Portuguese were already running ships across the Atlantic. (c) The casta ranks decided who could hold office and who paid tribute, so everyone had a defined place and the people at the top had a reason to defend the system.

### Question 2 — primary text source

*Primary source: Bartolomé de las Casas,* A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies, *finished 1542 (public domain; plainly rendered):*

> "The Spaniards set upon the Indians with cruelty never before seen… they laid wagers on who could slice a man in two with one stroke, or cut off his head with a single blow. They took nursing babes from their mothers' breasts and dashed their heads against the rocks."

a. Identify the labor system Las Casas is criticizing.
b. Explain ONE action the Spanish crown took in response to criticisms like his.
c. Explain ONE reason a historian would be cautious using this account as evidence of typical conditions.

**Scoring notes.**
- (a) Earns the point: the encomienda. (The passage describes the world the encomienda built; accept "the encomienda system / forced Native labor under the encomienda.")
- (b) Earns the point: the New Laws of 1542 — no more enslaving Natives, the encomienda to phase out. Full credit does not require adding "barely enforced," but a response that does is still correct. Does not earn: the Valladolid debate (that was a debate, not a crown action) or the repartimiento (that came later and was a different fix).
- (c) Earns the point: a sourcing-based caution — Las Casas wrote to persuade the king to act, so he selected the worst cases and his numbers are his own (his Hispaniola figures are far above modern estimates); *or* he wrote as an advocate, not a neutral observer. As with Set 1, naming the purpose alone is not enough — the point requires explaining *how* the purpose affects reliability. Does not earn: "he was Spanish so he was biased" without explanation.

**Full-credit exemplar.**
> (a) The encomienda. (b) The crown passed the New Laws in 1542, banning the enslavement of Natives and ordering the encomienda to die out with its current holders. (c) Las Casas was lobbying the king for reform, so he had every reason to choose the most horrifying cases and inflate the scale — his population figures for Hispaniola are his own and run far above what modern historians accept.

### Question 3 — non-text source

*Non-text source (described): an 18th-century Mexican casta painting, one panel of a series. A well-dressed Spanish father and an African mother stand with their child; a caption beneath reads, in effect, "From Spaniard and Black, Mulatto." All three wear fine clothing; the setting is orderly and domestic.*

a. Identify ONE thing the painting's labels tell you about colonial society.
b. Explain how the painting reflects the casta system as BOTH custom and law.
c. Explain ONE limitation of casta paintings as evidence of daily life in colonial Mexico.

**Scoring notes.**
- (a) Earns the point: that colonial society formally categorized people by ancestry — the labels themselves are the evidence. Does not earn: describing the clothing without connecting it to categorization.
- (b) Earns the point: both halves — *custom*: the clothing, the domestic order, the social performance of rank; *law*: those categories carried legal consequences (who could hold office, who paid tribute). Must address both. A response with only one half earns nothing here, because (b) asks for the combination.
- (c) Earns the point: an explained limitation — the paintings were an elite genre showing an ordered ideal, often painted for peninsular buyers; they show how the elite wanted the hierarchy to look, not necessarily how mixed neighborhoods actually lived. Does not earn: "it's just a painting" without explanation.

**Full-credit exemplar.**
> (a) The labels show that ancestry was formally classified — every mixture got a name and a place. (b) The fine clothing and orderly household show the custom side, rank performed through dress and manners; but the categories also had legal teeth, deciding who could hold office and who owed tribute. (c) These were luxury objects for elite buyers, so they present the hierarchy as neat and complete — a sales pitch for order, not a photograph of how people actually lived.

## LEQ Practice — Comparison

*The real LEQ covers broader ground than one unit, so treat this as comparison-skill practice using Unit 1 content you know cold.*

**Prompt (in CB style).** *European powers built American empires on forced labor, but they did not build them the same way.*

Evaluate the extent to which Spanish and English approaches to colonization differed in the period 1492–1622. In your response, you may consider labor systems, relations with Native peoples, and the role of the state.

**What a defensible thesis looks like.** It takes a position *and* sets up the contrast — two examples minimum, on your side and against it. Weak: "Spain and England were different." Defensible: "Spanish and English colonization differed fundamentally in labor and in the state's role — Spain ran a crown-managed system of Native drafts that hardened into African slavery, while England's private joint-stock colonies leaned on indentured servants and land grants — though both ended up seizing Native land to feed a cash crop."

**Contextualization guidance.** One to two sentences *before* your argument, connecting the prompt to something bigger — not a passing phrase. Usable context: the Reconquista mindset and the Ottoman chokehold on Asian trade (why Spain sailed as a crown project for gold, God, and glory); England's late entry after 1588 and its reliance on private investors (why Jamestown was a company town, not a royal one). "Spain wanted gold" alone is a passing phrase, not contextualization — say *why* that motive existed when it did.

**The 6-point rubric, in plain language.**
1. **Thesis (1 pt):** answers the question with a historically defensible claim that sets up comparison. Must appear in the intro or conclusion.
2. **Contextualization (1 pt):** broader developments framing the argument (see above). A phrase like "during this time" earns nothing.
3. **Evidence (2 pts):** specific facts tied to the argument — one point per well-chosen piece, up to two. "The mita draft rotated men about one year in seven" earns; "Spain used labor" does not.
4. **Analysis and reasoning (2 pts):** the comparison actually *does work* — explains how the evidence shows difference or similarity (one point), and ties it back to the thesis across the response (second point). Describing Spain, then describing England, with no sentence comparing them, caps you at one.

**10-minute outline check.** If your outline has (1) a two-sided thesis, (2) two context sentences, (3) four specific facts with the comparison stated outright, you are on pace for 5–6.

## DBQ Skill Set — Valladolid and Its Afterlife (skill practice)

*The real DBQ topic range starts at 1754, so this is not a DBQ prediction — it is sourcing and comparison practice on sources you know, aimed at the exact skills the Chief Reader Report flags: sourcing (0.39/1 — students name a POV but never explain its relevance) and complex understanding (0.15/1).*

**Document 1.** Bartolomé de las Casas, *A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies*, finished 1542 (public domain; plainly rendered): *"The Christians have slain and destroyed so many… that the islands are left desolate. For the greed all men have for gold, they have depopulated the land."*

**Document 2.** Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda, *Democrates alter* (argued at Valladolid, 1550–51; public domain; plainly rendered): *"These people are homunculi — little men — in whom one can scarcely find traces of humanity… war against them is just, for it ends idolatry and human sacrifice and brings them true religion and just laws."* (Note: Sepúlveda never crossed the Atlantic; his case was built on Aristotle.)

**Document 3 (described).** A 1590s engraving by Theodor de Bry, a Flemish artist working in Frankfurt who never crossed the Atlantic: Spaniards set war dogs on Native villagers while a priest looks on. Printed for the English and Dutch market, decades after Las Casas wrote.

**Questions (answer in sentences, not bullets).**

1. **Sourcing, Doc 1.** Identify Las Casas's purpose in writing AND explain how that purpose shapes what he emphasizes. (Naming the purpose alone does not earn the point — connect it to the content.)
2. **Sourcing, Doc 2.** Explain how Sepúlveda's situation — who he was, who he wrote for — limits what his account can prove about Native societies.
3. **Comparison.** Explain ONE fundamental difference between Documents 1 and 2 in their view of whether Native peoples could govern themselves.
4. **Using Doc 3.** Explain how de Bry's engraving both *uses* and *distorts* Las Casas's account.

**Scoring notes.**
- Q1 earns the point: purpose (move the Spanish king to reform/end the encomienda system) + the link (he selects the worst atrocities, frames Spaniards as uniquely cruel *in order to* trigger royal action; his numbers are his own). Purpose without the link = no point.
- Q2 earns the point: Sepúlveda was a court scholar writing for the imperial court, building a philosophical defense from Aristotle without ever seeing the Americas — so his "evidence" about Native peoples is assertion serving an argument, not observation. Must tie his situation to the *limit on reliability*.
- Q3 earns the point: Las Casas — rational souls, capable of faith and self-government; Sepúlveda — natural slaves, unfit to rule themselves, needing conquest's "order." One clean contrast, stated as a difference.
- Q4 earns the point: *uses* — the cruelty underneath was real and came from accounts like Las Casas's; *distorts* — de Bry, a Fleming working in Frankfurt for the English and Dutch market, cranked the horror up and aimed it at Spain alone, turning reform literature into anti-Spanish propaganda (the Black Legend). Both halves required.
- **Complex-understanding hint (the 0.15/1 skill):** the strongest responses notice the irony crossing all three documents — the friar who fought the conquest armed Spain's enemies with their best weapon. If you can state that in a sentence, you are thinking the way the top score band thinks.
