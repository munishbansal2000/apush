Maya: Grab the episode sheet. This is episode one, so everything starts here. Four boxes: environment shaped Native societies, maize, the Iroquois Confederacy, and the pristine wilderness myth. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eleven minutes, and you'll check all four off. And start with this: around 1100, a city on the Mississippi River held as many people as London. Nobody there had wheels or horses, and nobody wrote any of it down.

Marcus: First, the rule that runs this whole episode: the land decided how people lived. Same continent, totally different lives, and most of the difference starts with the ground under your feet.

Maya: Give me the extremes.

Marcus: Start in the Southwest. Hot, dry, and unforgiving. And the Pueblo peoples farmed it anyway. Adobe villages, that's sun-dried mud brick, with irrigation pulling water out of dry ground. The Spanish would later call these towns pueblos, villages — the name stuck. Corn, beans and squash, the three sisters, planted together so each one helps the others grow. That took real engineering, and it let them stay in one place.

Maya: Okay, desert farmers. So who's the opposite?

Marcus: The Plains. Open grassland, enormous bison herds. And no horses yet. Those come with the Spanish. So people hunted on foot, lived in tipis, those portable hide tents, and followed the herds with the seasons.

Maya: Hold on. Every Western movie has the buffalo chase on horseback.

Marcus: Every one of those is set after the horses arrived. Before that? On foot. Which tells you something about how they lived — and that's your first exam trap.

Maya: Your turn. No horses on the Plains, and the bison get hunted on foot. So: portable tents, or permanent towns? Think it through.

[10-second pause]

Maya: Portable tents. You can't drag a town after a moving herd. The food moves, so you move.

Marcus: Now swing east, to the woodlands. The Iroquois built longhouses: long shared houses, whole extended families under one roof, inside towns ringed by palisades, fences of tall wooden stakes. Corn in the summer, hunting and fishing the rest of the year. A mixed economy — which is why their villages stayed put, but never grew into cities.

Maya: So basically everybody farmed. Desert farming, forest farming. Same idea, different dirt.

Marcus: Careful. The Plains mostly hunted. They planted a little along the rivers, but the herds ran the show. You can't farm a food supply that walks away from you.

Marcus: Run the chain yourself: the land decides the food. The food decides whether you stay or move. And that decides how you live.

Maya: So the exam rarely asks "what did they eat." It asks why they lived that way, and the answer starts with the land.

Maya: One exam tip before we move on. The exam tests whether you can run that chain yourself. If a question asks why these societies looked so different, start with the land, not the people.

Maya: And the common mistake for box one: match the wrong society to the wrong land. Adobe and irrigation means the Southwest. Tents on the move means the Plains.

Maya: Checking that one.

Maya: Okay. Now the crop that changed everything.

Marcus: Maize. And the part people get backwards: nobody found it. Native farmers in what's now Mexico bred it from teosinte, a wild grass, over thousands of years. They didn't find this crop. They built it. Then it traveled north, century by century, and everywhere it arrived, populations grew and towns got bigger. It reached the Southwest first, then crept east and north. By the time Europeans arrived, cornfields stretched from the Mississippi to the Atlantic.

Maya: Wait — they invented corn? From a grass?

Marcus: Over thousands of years. And once you can grow food in one place, everything changes. No maize, no food surplus. No food surplus, no cities.

Maya: Is that where Cahokia comes in?

Marcus: Right across the river from what's now St. Louis. At its center, Monk's Mound: an earthen pyramid about a hundred feet tall, built basketful by basketful. Dug by hand, carried in baskets.

Maya: I got lost in St. Louis once, so I'll take your word on the river.

Marcus: Fair. At its peak in the 1200s, Cahokia held something like 10,000 to 20,000 people, roughly the size of London at the time. All of it with no wheels and no horses, and nobody wrote any of it down.

Maya: A city the size of London, and nobody wrote it down. How do we even know?

Marcus: Archaeology. The mounds are still there. Burials that show real inequality. Trade goods from hundreds of miles away: copper from the Great Lakes, shell from the Gulf Coast. Cahokia sat at the center of a trade web, and all of it moved on foot. Different technology, same human logic. Make something valuable, trade it far, get powerful.

Maya: So why did it empty out?

Marcus: Nobody's fully sure. By the 1300s it was mostly empty. The leading theory is environmental stress plus political breakdown, but honestly, it's a mystery.

Maya: Exam tip for box two. If a question hands you a big pre-contact city, the follow-up is usually about food. Population density comes from surplus. Run that chain.

Maya: And don't write that anyone "found" maize. You don't find a crop that took thousands of years to breed.

Maya: Box three: the Iroquois Confederacy. Five nations, one council. How did anything get done?

Marcus: Slowly. The Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga and Seneca bound themselves together under the Great Law of Peace. It started as a peace deal — five nations that had been fighting each other agreed to stop. The Grand Council handled war and diplomacy. The rule was consensus. Every nation had to agree.

Maya: Not six nations?

Marcus: A sixth joined later. The founding five are the ones the exam asks about.

Marcus: And the clans ran through the mother's line. The word is matrilineal: clans traced through the mother's line. The clan mothers chose the council leaders, and they could remove them. Iroquois women held real political power, which surprised just about every European who encountered it.

Maya: So did the Iroquois invent American democracy? I've heard that.

Marcus: Watch it — that's the trap answer. Some historians argue the confederacy influenced the founders. Others say it's overstated. It isn't settled, so the exam will never ask you to agree with it.

Maya: Read the federalism question twice, then. If an answer choice draws a straight line from the confederacy to the Constitution. Trap.

Maya: Common mistake for box three: it wasn't a vote. Every nation had to agree, or nothing happened.

Marcus: Now compare that to the Pueblo world. Town by town, each adobe village mostly running its own affairs, tied to its neighbors by trade and ceremony. At the center of town, the kiva: an underground ceremonial room, entered by ladder. Religion and government in the same room. No Grand Council. No five-nation consensus. Each town answered to itself.

Maya: So one confederacy, many villages. Same continent, different answers to the same problem. A comparison question wants exactly that shape.

Maya: Last box. And this one I learned in school: Native peoples lived in harmony with nature. Took what they needed, and left the land as they found it.

Marcus: Half of that holds. They knew their land cold. But the "no trace" part falls apart. They set controlled burns to clear brush and drive game. They cleared forests for fields. They built traps across rivers to catch fish.

Maya: Okay, that's not harmony. More like clearing, burning, and trapping.

Marcus: The historian William Cronon made the case in his book Changes in the Land: what Europeans called wilderness was a landscape people had been shaping for centuries.

Maya: But it looked empty when Europeans showed up. Why?

Marcus: Because in a lot of places, disease got there first. Epidemics swept through and emptied whole villages before Europeans ever saw the interior. What they called wilderness was land people had worked, then lost.

Maya: So the "harmony" version isn't wrong about the knowledge. Just about the footprint.

Marcus: And the garden is the reality. Tended for centuries, then emptied by disease.

Maya: A garden with no gardeners left.

Maya: Your turn. It's the 1600s. You sail into an empty stretch of coastline. What's the conclusion you should not draw?

[10-second pause]

Maya: That it was always empty. Ask what emptied it, because the answer might be an epidemic, not nature.

Maya: Last exam tip. The trap is the word "wilderness." When a source describes "empty" land, ask what emptied it.

Maya: And the common mistake: don't write "untouched." The whole point of the box is that it was touched.

Maya: All four topics covered. Now let's see what stuck.

Maya: Four boxes, let's land them. One: environment shaped Native societies: the land decides the food, the food decides the move.

Marcus: Southwest farms, Plains hunts, Northeast does both.

Maya: Box one, checked. Two: maize, bred from a wild grass, carried north, and it made cities like Cahokia possible.

Maya: Box two, checked. Three: the Iroquois Confederacy. Five nations, Grand Council, everybody... votes?

Marcus: Consensus. Not votes.

Maya: Right. Consensus or nothing, and the clan mothers chose the leaders. Box three, checked. Four: the pristine wilderness myth. False. By then they'd been burning it for centuries, clearing it for fields, and fishing it with traps. Then disease emptied it.

Marcus: Managed land, mistaken for wild.

Maya: False. Claim: it was a managed landscape. Evidence: controlled burns, cleared fields, fish traps across rivers. Reasoning: Europeans mistook worked land for wild, and in places, disease had emptied the villages before they arrived.
