Maya: Last time: the Valladolid debate — Las Casas versus Sepúlveda, 1550, arguing whether conquering Native peoples could ever be just. This time: Native peoples answer the question themselves. August 1680, New Mexico: the only Native uprising in North American history to drive Europeans out and hold the land for years. Three boxes on your sheet: the knotted cords, Popé, and Vargas's return (1692). Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes, and you'll check all three off.

Marcus: Start with the setup. The Spanish had been in New Mexico for about eighty years. The Franciscans ran the missions, and their goal was simple: replace Pueblo religion with Catholicism. Kivas were banned, masks and sacred objects burned.

Maya: Kivas: the underground ceremonial rooms, right? From episode one?

Marcus: Same rooms. On top of the religion, forced labor for the colonists and a slow squeeze on Pueblo farmland. Then the 1670s turned brutal: drought, year after year, crops failing, and Apache raids the Spanish couldn't stop.

Maya: So the empire can't protect them and it won't leave their gods alone.

Marcus: And that's the fuse. The spark comes in 1675. Governor Treviño arrests forty-seven medicine men for practicing "sorcery."

Maya: Forty-seven? For praying wrong?

Marcus: Four get death sentences. Three are hanged. One kills himself in prison. The rest are whipped in public and thrown in jail — until the pueblos march on Santa Fe and demand them back. The governor folds.

Maya: Wait, so they march on the capital and the governor just... folds?

Marcus: The soldiers were away fighting Apaches, and he couldn't afford a war at home. Among the freed men is a religious leader from Ohkay Owingeh, a Tewa man. His name was Popé.

Maya: And he's about to start a revolution.

Marcus: From Taos, far north, about as far from Santa Fe's reach as you could get. He had five years to plan. And he faced a problem nobody had solved: the pueblos spoke about half a dozen different languages and had never managed to act together.

Maya: So no group chat. No group anything.

Marcus: No group anything. His answer was a calendar made of knots. Runners carried cords to each pueblo, one knot for each day. Every morning, untie one knot. When the last knot comes loose, everybody rises at once.

Maya: Oh, this plays like a heist movie. The crew, the synchronized watches, the whole plan moving up because somebody gets spotted.

Marcus: Down to the last part. Two young runners from Tesuque, Pedro Omtua and Nicolas Catua in the Spanish records, get caught on the road. So Popé moves the date up — most accounts say a day, and on August 10th, 1680, they rose.

Maya: I once tried a paper-chain countdown to a concert and ate half the links. These guys ran a countdown across four hundred miles.

Marcus: Nearly four hundred miles, Taos to the Hopi villages. And it worked. They rose almost everywhere at once. Close to four hundred Spaniards were killed, including twenty-one of the thirty-three Franciscan priests in New Mexico. They besieged Santa Fe, cut off its water, and Governor Otermín wrote it all down in his journal — the siege, the retreat — then led his people south to El Paso.

Maya: The governor kept a journal during all that?

Marcus: Spanish officials documented everything, including their disasters. By late August, New Mexico was Pueblo land again. For twelve years.

Maya: One down: the knotted cords. Checking that one.

Marcus: Earned. Now box two: the man with the knots.

Maya: Okay so Popé was, like... the king of the pueblos? He gives the orders, everybody obeys?

Marcus: That's the wrong picture, and it's the one to drop. Popé wasn't a chief in the European sense. The pueblos had no single ruler — each town ran itself. Popé was a religious leader — a medicine man whose authority came from a vision.

Maya: What kind of vision?

Marcus: In a kiva at Taos, he said three spirits visited him, with glowing eyes and fire shooting from their fingertips. They told him to purge the Spanish and the world would be made whole: rain, crops, health. At least, that's what the accounts say. We're hearing this through Spanish pens.

Maya: So the message the runners carried was basically: the gods are done waiting.

Marcus: Right. After the victory, though, Popé turned hard. Churches, crosses, anything Spanish: destroyed. Converts scrubbed themselves with yucca branches to wash off baptism. People dropped their Spanish names and ripped out Spanish crops. Some accounts say even speaking Spanish was banned. "The God of the Christians is dead," he said. "He was made of rotten wood."

Maya: Okay. That's a lot.

Marcus: And the old rivalries came back. The drought didn't care about the victory. The crops still failed. About a year later he'd been pushed aside, and the Spanish accounts call him a tyrant by the end, which you take with a grain of salt. He's out of the records within a few years. The leading guess puts his death around 1688.

Maya: The liberator becomes the guy nobody wants in charge.

Marcus: That was the turn. Next up: 1692.

Maya: Vargas comes back with an army, big battle, end of the story?

Marcus: That part comes later, and the order matters. In 1692 Diego de Vargas walks into Santa Fe with a small force and negotiates. Promises clemency, gets the pueblos to swear allegiance. By the Spanish accounts, it was bloodless.

Maya: So no battle?

Marcus: Not in 1692. But the next year the fighting flared again. Vargas came back in 1693 with hundreds of colonists, retook Santa Fe by force, and about seventy Pueblo men were executed. It wasn't clean.

Maya: So how is this not just "Spain wins eventually"?

Marcus: Because of what Spain did differently this time. The empire that came back was chastened. Land grants for the pueblos. A public defender appointed to argue Native cases in Spanish courts. And the friars — the ones who returned didn't try to run a theocracy again. The old ceremonies went on, underground, tolerated in a way they'd never been.

Maya: I don't buy "won by losing," though. Spain came back. Spain's flag went back up.

Marcus: The flag went back up. The old Spain didn't. Before 1680: masks burned, kivas banned, medicine men hanged. After: the ceremonies survive, and they're still practiced today. The revolt couldn't hold New Mexico, but it forced the empire to change how it ruled. That's the paradox.

Maya: Okay — three boxes, let's land them. One: the knotted cords.

Marcus: One knot per day, untie one each morning, last knot means rise. It coordinated dozens of pueblos across hundreds of miles, speaking about six different languages. Two runners got caught, so Popé moved it up to August 10th, 1680.

Maya: First box, checked.

Maya: Two: Popé.

Marcus: Tewa religious leader from Ohkay Owingeh. Arrested in the 1675 crackdown, freed, planned from Taos. The vision of the three spirits. Won the revolt, turned theocratic, lost the pueblos, gone by the late 1680s.

Maya: Second one, checked.

Maya: Three: 1692. Vargas rolls in with the army, takes Santa Fe back —

Marcus: Almost. 1692 was the negotiated one. The army and the fighting, that was 1693. Then the chastened empire: land grants, a defender for Native rights, ceremonies tolerated underground.

Maya: The negotiated one, then the fighty one. Got it. Third box, checked.

Maya: Two questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One: you're handed a deer-hide cord with knots, and you're told runners carried copies of it hundreds of miles, one knot untied each morning. What does that artifact tell you about 1680?

[5-second pause]

Maya: That the revolt was planned, not spontaneous. Dozens of pueblos coordinated a single date across hundreds of miles and half a dozen languages. The cord was the calendar.

Maya: Two: a Spanish journal from Santa Fe describes burning churches, a siege, and a retreat south to El Paso. What does that source show about the outcome?

[5-second pause]

Maya: That the Spanish lost. They were driven out of New Mexico entirely and stayed gone for twelve years, until 1692.

Maya: Check your three boxes. Episode eight in the books. Next time: the Unit 1 cram session, the whole unit in one episode.

Marcus: They won by losing.

Maya: And the old ways survived.
