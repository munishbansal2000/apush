Marcus: Forget the mountain for a minute. The Potosí strike from episode three: 1545, silver in what's now Bolivia. But a strike isn't an economy — what Spain built around it, that was the machine.

Maya: And it started with... a gust of wind?

Marcus: The finder's story made it into the records. Diego Huallpa, an Indigenous prospector, went up the hill looking for a shrine, got knocked sideways by a gust of wind, and came up with his hands full of rich silver ore.

Maya: A gust of wind? Come on. I heard he was chasing a llama.

Marcus: Some tellings say a llama. The wind version is the one Huallpa told on his deathbed. Either way, the hill filled up fast.

Maya: I got altitude sickness on a ski lift at Mammoth once, at like eight thousand feet. This place is way higher than that. My nose would just give up.

Marcus: About thirteen thousand feet. And the ore had to become money. So where do you put the mint?

Maya: Seville? That's where the treasure fleets went.

Marcus: At the foot of the mountain. Potosí stamped its own coins, pieces of eight, and those coins traveled the world. The mint there grew into one of the biggest in the world. Silver went in one door and came out the other as money.

Maya: Wait. The dollar sign. Is that... from this coin?

Marcus: The leading theory says yes: the pillars stamped on the coin, wrapped in a ribbon, became the $. Nobody's fully sure, but it's the best story we've got.

Maya: I'm looking at my keyboard differently now.

Maya: Okay, so the mountain gets rich. Who's doing the actual digging?

Marcus: Mostly the mita draft from episode three, the forced rotation, plus paid hands. But the voice I want you to know is an Indigenous one. Around 1615, a man named Felipe Guamán Poma de Ayala, an Andean nobleman, finished a nearly twelve-hundred-page illustrated chronicle, hundreds of his own drawings, addressed as a letter to the king of Spain. Page after page, he documents the abuses of colonial rule. He wrote it in Spanish and Quechua, to a king who'd never set foot in the Andes.

Maya: And the king read it and fixed everything?

Marcus: The king never got it. It sat unread for centuries. But it's one of the most important Indigenous voices we have from the whole colonial period.

Maya: First box, checked off.

Maya: Box two: Bodin and the birth of inflation theory. The price revolution from episode three — silver flooding in, prices climbing across Europe for a century. Did people at the time know what was causing it?

Marcus: A French official named Malestroit blamed bad coins. Said rulers were watering down the silver. Jean Bodin answered him, by 1568: no, it's the American silver. More money chasing the same goods. That argument is basically the birth of the quantity theory of money.

Maya: So the silver makes everything more expensive, but nobody gets paid more?

Marcus: Exactly. For ordinary people, the silver age felt like a pay cut. Spain felt it first — the wave hit there before anywhere else. And wages didn't keep up.

Maya: But wait. Spain had the silver. How do you hold the mountain and end up broke?

Marcus: Historians are still arguing about that one. One side calls it the resource curse: easy silver money, prices climbing, local workshops undercut by cheaper imports, and the crown spending on wars faster than the mines could produce. The silver flowed in, and flowed right back out. The other side says "curse" overstates it. Spain ran the biggest empire on earth for a century.

Maya: Bankrupt? With the silver still coming in?

Marcus: Four times under Philip II. The crown spent on wars faster than the mines could produce — Genoese bankers got paid before the silver even unloaded.

Maya: So where did all that silver actually end up going?

Marcus: Last box: the silver that sailed west. The silver-for-silk trade from episode three — but the real story is the ships. From the 1560s, the Manila galleons ran between Acapulco and Manila, one of the longest trade routes on earth, on some of the biggest wooden ships ever built. Usually one or two a year, and the run lasted two and a half centuries. The way home rode the Pacific winds north past Japan — the only route that worked.

Maya: How long was that trip?

Marcus: Four to six months each way. Silver sailed west, silk and porcelain sailed back east. And the demand pulling it all was China — silver became the metal China craved most. After the 1580s, Ming reforms let families pay their taxes in silver, and the demand never stopped.

Maya: And hold on. The silver doesn't even go to Spain?

Marcus: A lot of it never did. The crown got its cut from the Atlantic fleets. But the Pacific was financing a trade with Asia where, by most accounts, the merchants running it kept most of the profit — not Madrid. The men in Manila and Mexico City got rich.
