Maya: Last time: the silver mountain, the price revolution, the galleons crossing the Pacific to Manila. All of it ran on one thing: workers. And Spain kept running out of them. This time: how Spain tried to solve a labor shortage three times, and every solution built a different kind of trap. Three boxes on your sheet: the repartimiento, the Middle Passage, and the hacienda. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes, and you'll check all three off.

Marcus: The chain starts where Episode 2 left it. The encomienda, the grant of Native labor. Las Casas pushes the crown to kill it, the New Laws of 1542 say the encomienda dies out with its holders. And then the colonists nearly tear the empire apart.

Maya: The Pizarro thing, right? In Peru?

Marcus: Gonzalo Pizarro, the conquistador's brother. His rebels rose against the New Laws and killed the viceroy the crown sent to enforce them, Blasco Núñez Vela. In Mexico, the viceroy just stalled. Within a few years the crown caved and gutted the inheritance ban. The encomienda limped on.

Maya: So the repartimiento was the crown's nice fix. Problem solved, everybody goes home?

Marcus: It solved the crown's problem, not the workers'. In the mines it got slavery-like fast. The ban on enslaving Natives mostly stuck. The inheritance ban didn't. And the crown still had the same problem: not enough workers, because disease kept emptying the Native towns. So it changed the system instead of the goal. The replacement was a draft.

Maya: A draft. Like names out of a hat?

Marcus: Close. The crown took the power to allot workers away from the encomenderos. Native towns owed the crown a rotating share of workers, and a crown official assigned them to settlers for a few weeks at a time. Farms, mines, workshops. When your turn ended, you went home. Then the next group came.

Maya: Wait, is that the mita?

Marcus: Cousins. The mita was Peru's draft, for the silver mines from Episode 3. The repartimiento ran New Spain. Same crown, same idea, different name. And the name tells you the logic. Repartir, to divide up. The crown decided the encomenderos couldn't be trusted with the workers, so the crown would divide them up itself.

Maya: Paid?

Marcus: A little. Intermittent, low pay. But in law, the workers were free.

Maya: Draft versus grant. In the draft, the crown picks your name off a list. In the grant, the colonist gets handed the whole town.

Marcus: And that's the tell. In the grant, the colonist owned the workers' labor outright. In the draft, the crown only rented it out a few weeks at a time.

Maya: Repartimiento's checked. Two to go.

Marcus: But the draft had a math problem. The Native population kept falling. Fewer towns, fewer turns to draft. So Spain looked somewhere the workers wouldn't run out.

Maya: Africa.

Marcus: The reasons were cold, and they added up. Africans carried some immunity to Old World disease. Many came from farming societies — they knew rice, they knew tropical crops. And the Portuguese were already running the route, so the ships could keep coming no matter how many died.

Maya: "No matter how many died." The Middle Passage, right there.

Marcus: The Atlantic crossing. Below decks, chained, for weeks, sometimes a couple of months. Disease and starvation did the rest. Roughly one in seven died on the way over. That's the common estimate, though the ship logs swing wider.

Maya: I used to play hide-and-seek where I'd squeeze into the hall closet and stay too long — dark, coats everywhere, couldn't move. That was a game. Two months of that, in the dark, in chains.

Marcus: And the ones who survived were sold into plantation and mine labor that worked them hard and bought replacements. Say it plainly: it became one of the largest forced migrations in human history.

Maya: And Las Casas is in this story too, right? He suggested this?

Marcus: Early on, yes. Around 1516 he proposed bringing in enslaved Africans as the alternative to working Natives to death. He even wrote it into a reform plan for the crown.

Maya: So the great defender of the Indians lit the fuse on the African slave trade.

Marcus: He didn't light it. Enslaved Africans were already being shipped to the islands before his proposal. What he did was lend it respectability, give the crown a moral-sounding reason. And decades later, in his history of the Indies, he took it back. Wrote that African slavery was every bit as unjust, and he wasn't sure his good intentions would excuse him.

Maya: An apology in a book nobody read for a century. Convenient.

Marcus: Partly convenient, I'll give you that. But he spent the rest of his life arguing both were wrong. The irony stands either way. The man who fought the encomienda helped open the door to something worse.

Maya: Okay, that's two systems down and neither one solved the shortage. So what finally stuck?

Marcus: By the 1600s, the countryside settles into something that lasts: the hacienda. Great estates, cattle, wheat, sugar. Here's the shift. No grant of people this time. The power was the land itself.

Maya: So who works it?

Marcus: Peons. Technically free workers. The chain was debt. You got your wages as an advance, or in credit at the estate store, where everything cost too much. The balance never cleared. Sometimes it passed to your kids.

Maya: Wait — peon. That's where the word comes from? Like calling someone a peon?

Marcus: Same word. It started here, on these estates.

Maya: So it's slavery with paperwork.

Marcus: Not in law, and the crown cared about the law. But if you can't leave the estate, the paperwork is doing the same work. How much of it was formal debt on the books, and how much was custom with nowhere else to go, historians still argue about that.

Maya: Three systems, three answers to one shortage. Three boxes, let's land them. One: the repartimiento. The New Laws, 1542 — that's when the draft started.

Marcus: Ordered then. The real swap came after the mid-1500s. Crown officials allotted workers to settlers for a few weeks at a time.

Maya: Draft, not grant. Checking that one.

Maya: Two: the Middle Passage. Spain turned to enslaved African labor because the Native workforce was collapsing. Africans had some disease immunity and farming skills. And the ships kept coming. Plus the Las Casas irony — he proposed it early, took it back decades later. Checking that one.

Maya: Three: the hacienda. Big estates, and the workers were technically free?

Marcus: Technically free. Debt did the chaining. Advances, the estate store, balances that never cleared.

Maya: Three for three.

Maya: Two questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One: a crown official assigns your village's workers to a mine for three weeks, then sends them home. Which system — and who did the assigning before it?

[5-second pause]

Maya: The repartimiento, the crown's rotating draft. Before it, the encomenderos did the assigning.

Maya: Two: a historian hands you a hacienda account book. One worker's debt grows every year for a decade, even though he's working the whole time. What's the point of a source like that?

[5-second pause]

Maya: Debt peonage. It shows the debt was the chain. The worker was technically free and practically trapped.

Maya: Check your three boxes. Episode six in the books. Next time: the Valladolid debate. Las Casas versus Sepúlveda, 1550, arguing whether Spain's conquest and enslavement of Native peoples could ever be just.

Marcus: Spain kept solving the same problem —

Maya: It never ran out of answers. Just people.
