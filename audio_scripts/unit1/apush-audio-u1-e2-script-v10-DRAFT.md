Maya: Last time: a continent that was never empty, and never simple. This time: the ocean in between, and the sailors who decided it was a road, not a wall. Three boxes: the three Gs, the toolkit, and the Treaty of Tordesillas. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eleven minutes, and you'll check all three off.

Marcus: Start with the why, because the why decided everything. Europe wanted Asian spices and silk. The old land routes ran east through Ottoman territory, and after Constantinople fell in 1453, those routes got pricier and shakier. So motive one goes up on the board: gold.

Maya: Gold first. But it's not really about spices, is it?

Marcus: It's about all of it. Historians point to a bullion shortage — not enough gold and silver coin for a growing economy. And the part the slogans skip: a pile of metal was power you could spend. No metal, no armies. No armies, no crowns.

Maya: Okay, but God is word two, and God is not about coin.

Marcus: God is the Reconquista. For centuries, Christian kingdoms in Iberia had been pushing Muslim rule off the peninsula, and it ended at Granada in January of 1492. Isabella and Ferdinand, the Catholic Monarchs, walked in that January. Columbus landed that October. Same year.

Maya: So a crown fresh off a holy war feels owed new souls.

Marcus: Conversion was the mission statement. Conquest was the habit underneath it.

Maya: And glory is word three. I don't buy it, though. Nobody risks drowning for bragging rights.

Marcus: Crowns did. Portugal was getting rich off the African coastal trade while Spain watched from the shore, and Renaissance crowns competed for prestige the way schools compete for rankings. Nobody writes the check without the gold, but nobody lets a rival get rich in peace either.

Maya: So the three Gs aren't equal partners. Gold did the heavy lifting, and the other two came along.

Marcus: Most historians would back you. Some say the whole thing was economics wearing a religious costume. Others say the state rivalry mattered more, that crowns sailed because other crowns were sailing. Nobody's fully sure which one turned the ship.

Maya: Your turn. You're running Castile in 1492. Portugal owns the African coastal trade, around Africa, and its ships are closing in on the sea road to Asia. The old land routes run through Ottoman hands. Do you fight Portugal for its road, or find your own way west?

[10-second pause]

Maya: West. You can't out-sail Portugal on its own route, so you bet on a shortcut across the ocean. That bet is what funded Columbus.

Maya: And the exam trap for box one: don't rank them equal. If a question asks for the most significant cause, lead with economics, then explain how the others rode along.

Maya: Common mistake, same box: writing that 1492 only means Columbus. Granada fell that January. If a question asks what made Spain ready to sail, the answer starts in January, not October.

Maya: Checking that one.

Marcus: Now the how, because for five hundred years nobody followed it up, and then one generation did. The caravel: small, quick, built to turn fast.

Marcus: The sails. Lateen sails let a ship claw into the wind instead of waiting for it. The astrolabe read latitude off the stars. And the compass plus portolan charts, hand-drawn coastal maps sailors actually trusted, did the rest.

Maya: Three tools. A boat, a sail, and a star-reader. That's the whole revolution?

Marcus: Plus a prince who never sailed. Henry the Navigator spent decades funding voyage after voyage down the African coast. Now the correction, because you've probably heard he ran a navigation school at Sagres.

Maya: I literally just learned that from a video essay. It's not real?

Marcus: Mostly legend. Historians say the academy with classrooms never existed. What existed was a wealthy patron paying for ships and hiring cartographers. The money was real. The school is the story.

Maya: That one stings. I cited it in a discussion post last month.

Marcus: Cite the correction instead. Teachers love that move.

Maya: Common mistake for box two: saying Henry sailed those voyages. He never sailed on the discovery voyages himself. He paid for them.

Maya: So where did the money get them?

Marcus: Down the coast, year by year. In 1488, Bartolomeu Dias rounded the southern tip of Africa. Ten years later, Vasco da Gama reached India. Portugal owned the sea road to Asia, and Spain was locked out of it completely. Which is where your second-grade song comes in.

Maya: Niña, Pinta, Santa María. The tune's still in my head.

Marcus: Three little ships, and the worst math that ever paid off. Columbus argued the earth was smaller than the cartographers believed, and Asia sat a few thousand miles west of the Canaries. The real number was closer to ten thousand, with a continent in the way. He got the shape of the earth right and the size of the trip wildly wrong.

Maya: And Portugal said no first, because their experts did the math correctly?

Marcus: They checked his numbers and passed. They were also committed: decades of money sunk into the African route. Spain's court said yes anyway: flush from Granada, and desperate to catch Portugal.

Maya: So why was it Iberia first, and not France or England?

Marcus: Geography helped: Atlantic-facing kingdoms. But mostly it was practice and timing. Portugal had spent decades working down the African coast. And Spain was a brand-new kingdom: Isabella of Castile and Ferdinand of Aragon had married two crowns together, then finished the Reconquista that same year. A new state proving itself, with a holy war's habits and a rival getting rich next door.

Maya: October 1492. He lands on a Caribbean island, calls it San Salvador, and thinks it's Asia.

Marcus: He called the people he met Indians, because he was sure he'd reached the East Indies, and the name stuck for centuries. And his journal from that first voyage says the mindset out loud. The people he met on the beach, quote, "would make fine servants" … The rest isn't his exact words — paraphrasing, he wrote that with fifty men he could subjugate them all. We don't even have his original log. This comes through Las Casas's copy.

Maya: He wrote that down. On the first voyage.

Marcus: Three more voyages after that, around the Caribbean and the mainland coast, and he died still insisting he'd found Asia.

Maya: Box two's the toolkit, then. Ships, sails, star-readers, charts, and a prince's money. Checking it off.

Marcus: Now the fight over the map. Spain's holding islands, Portugal's furious, and they call the referee. The part people miss: the referee was the Spanish-born pope Alexander the Sixth. His 1493 bulls — papal decrees — drew a line of demarcation pole to pole, about a hundred leagues, roughly three hundred miles, west of the Cape Verde islands. Everything west went to Spain. The one limit: neither side could claim land already ruled by a Christian king.

Maya: Wait, so the pope just handed Spain the ocean? That can't have held.

Marcus: It didn't hold. Portugal was never going to accept a Spanish pope handing Spain the ocean.

Maya: Your turn. Two crowns, both claiming the same ocean, and they agree to let one man draw the line. Why would Portugal accept a ruling from a Spanish pope?

[10-second pause]

Maya: It wouldn't just accept it. Portugal had the better navy and all the African charts — the leverage to demand a redo. So it argued, and the line moved west.

Marcus: In 1494, at Tordesillas in Spain, the two crowns signed the new deal. The line moved to three hundred and seventy leagues west of the Cape Verdes. West of the line, Spain. East of it, Portugal.

Maya: Three hundred and seventy leagues. A league's about three miles, so that's over a thousand miles of ocean.

Marcus: About that. And between the two of them, the line mostly held, which is why Brazil speaks Portuguese today. When Cabral landed on Brazil's coast in 1500, the bulge of it sat east of the line, on Portugal's side. One imaginary line, and it decided the language of half a continent.

Maya: But France and England just ignored it.

Marcus: Completely. No other European power ever accepted the pope's division or the treaty. The line bound two crowns and nobody else.

Maya: Box three's the treaty. Checking it off. And one exam warning: don't write that Tordesillas divided the world for everyone. It divided it for two crowns. The exam tests whether you know who wasn't bound.

Maya: Three boxes covered. Now the landing.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: the three Gs. Gold: the spice hunger plus the bullion shortage, and after 1453 the Ottoman squeeze on the old routes. God: the Reconquista's crusading energy, fresh off Granada in January of 1492. Glory: crowns racing each other. Gold did the heavy lifting.

Marcus: Economics first, the rest as accelerants. That's the ranking to argue.

Maya: Box one, checked. Two: the toolkit. Caravel, lateen sails, the astrolabe, compass and portolan charts. Henry the Navigator paid for the voyages — and the school was the legend, right?

Marcus: The school was the legend.

Maya: Dias rounded Africa in fourteen eighty-eight, da Gama reached India ten years later. Box two, checked.

Maya: Three: Tordesillas. Fourteen ninety-four. The Spanish pope's first line was a gift, Portugal forced the redo, and the new line sat three hundred and seventy leagues west of Cape Verde. Spain took the west, Portugal took the east. Brazil ended up speaking Portuguese, and France and England never signed. Box three, checked.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it.

Maya: One. A Portuguese captain's log from the 1480s lists new gear aboard: an astrolabe and updated portolan charts. What's the bigger pattern this source points to?

[15-second pause]

Maya: Claim: new instruments made the ocean crossable. Evidence: the astrolabe read latitude from the stars, and portolan charts mapped the coasts sailors trusted. Reasoning: Iberian sailors weren't braver than everyone else. They had the tools to sail beyond sight of land and find their way back, which is what made 1492 possible instead of 1392.

Maya: Two, and this one's a source. A Spanish priest writes in 1493 that the new lands will bring souls to the church and silver to the crown. A classmate says God mattered more than gold. How do you answer?

[15-second pause]

Maya: Claim: both, but ranked. Evidence: the crown spent money for metal: the spice trade, the bullion shortage, the Ottoman squeeze. The Reconquista gave the mission its language, and glory kept the race going. Reasoning: if the test asks for the most significant cause, lead with economics, because the crown spent money for metal first.

Maya: One more, fast. What did the Treaty of Tordesillas actually divide, and who ignored it?

[5-second pause]

Maya: The newly found lands outside Europe: west of the line to Spain, east to Portugal, along a meridian three hundred and seventy leagues west of the Cape Verdes. Everybody else ignored it, starting with France and England.

Maya: Check your three boxes. Next time: what crossed the Atlantic in both directions, and the cargo nobody chose.

Marcus: They wanted a shortcut to Asia —

Maya: and got the long way to everything.
