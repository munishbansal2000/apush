Marcus: August, 1680. Runners are moving across the desert with knotted cords in their hands. One knot for each day. Every morning, untie one knot. When the last knot comes loose, every pueblo rises at once. Santa Fe has no idea.

Marcus: This is the story of the single most successful Native uprising in North American history. Not the largest. The most successful. And it starts with forty-seven men in chains.

Marcus: Start eighty years earlier. The Spanish had been in New Mexico since the 1590s, and the Franciscans ran the missions with one goal: replace Pueblo religion with Catholicism. The kivas, the underground ceremonial rooms at the heart of Pueblo spiritual life, were banned. Masks and sacred objects were burned.

Marcus: The missions weren't just churches. They were the economy. Pueblo labor built the mission farms, wove the cloth, and harvested the fields, all under the friars' direction. The religion and the work were one machine. That's why the crackdown on the kivas cut so deep. It wasn't only about prayer. It was about who controlled the day.

Marcus: On top of the missions came forced labor for the colonists and a slow squeeze on Pueblo farmland.

Marcus: Then the 1670s turned brutal. Drought, year after year. Crops failing. Apache raids hammering the pueblos, and the Spanish unable to stop them. The empire couldn't protect the people it ruled, and it wouldn't leave their gods alone.

Marcus: The spark came in 1675. Governor Treviño arrested forty-seven medicine men for practicing sorcery. Forty-seven men, for praying wrong. The crackdown had been building for years. The friars knew the old religion had never died. It had gone underground, into the kivas, and every missionary could feel it down there, out of reach. The whippings were meant as a lesson. They became a recruitment poster.

Marcus: Four of the forty-seven received death sentences. Three were hanged. One killed himself in prison. The rest were whipped in public and thrown in jail.

Marcus: Then the pueblos did something the Spanish had never seen. They marched on Santa Fe and demanded the prisoners back. The soldiers were away fighting Apaches, and the governor couldn't afford a war at home. He folded. Every prisoner walked free.

Marcus: Among the freed men was a religious leader from Ohkay Owingeh, a Tewa man named Popé. He went north to Taos, as far from Santa Fe's reach as he could get, and he started planning. He had five years.

Marcus: Five years is a long time to hold a secret. Popé built it the way conspiracies get built: face to face, pueblo by pueblo, through men the Spanish had already marked. The runners were the network. Young men who could cross a desert the Spanish could barely map, carrying a cord the Spanish couldn't read even if they caught one.

Marcus: Popé faced a problem nobody had solved. The pueblos spoke about half a dozen different languages. They had never managed to act together. No group chat. No group anything.

Marcus: His answer was a calendar made of knots. Runners carried cords to each pueblo, one knot for each day. Untie one every morning. When the last knot comes loose, everybody rises the same day. The plan crossed nearly four hundred miles, from Taos to the Hopi villages.

Marcus: Think about what that coordination meant. Half a dozen languages. No writing the Spanish recognized. Hundreds of miles of desert. And one date, kept in knots. When historians call this the best-planned Native uprising in the continent's history, the cord is the evidence.

Marcus: What carried the plan was not just anger. In a kiva at Taos, Popé said three spirits visited him, with glowing eyes and fire shooting from their fingertips. They told him to purge the Spanish and the world would be made whole: rain, crops, health. At least, that's what the accounts say. We are hearing this through Spanish pens.

Marcus: The message the runners carried was simple: the gods are done waiting.

Marcus: Then the plan nearly broke. Two young runners from Tesuque, Pedro Omtua and Nicolas Catua in the Spanish records, were caught on the road. Under questioning, they talked. So Popé moved the date up. Most accounts say by a day. The northern pueblos rose on the 9th, the rest on the 10th. On August 10th, 1680, they rose.

Marcus: And it worked. They rose almost everywhere at once. Close to four hundred Spaniards were killed, including twenty-one of the thirty-three Franciscan priests in New Mexico. They besieged Santa Fe and cut the water. Otermín's journal is the Spanish account of their own collapse, and it reads like a man watching the walls close in. The city baked. For days the Spanish held the plaza while the pueblos held everything else. Then Otermín made the call: abandon Santa Fe. He led his people south to El Paso, the whole Spanish presence in New Mexico, gone on foot. Spanish officials documented everything, including their disasters.

Marcus: By late August, New Mexico was Pueblo land again. It would stay that way for twelve years. The missions stood empty. The old dances came back into the open. The longest stretch of Native self-rule anywhere in North America under colonial pressure. It was also the proof that the empire's grip was never as total as it looked.

Marcus: Victory, though, turned hard. Popé ordered everything Spanish destroyed: churches, crosses, anything from the old order. Converts scrubbed themselves with yucca branches to wash off baptism. People dropped their Spanish names and ripped out Spanish crops. Some accounts say even speaking Spanish was banned. The God of the Christians is dead, he said. He was made of rotten wood.

Marcus: Twelve years is a long time to hold a revolution with no common government. The pueblos had united against the Spanish, not under a single ruler, and once the Spanish were gone, the old differences came back. Taos feuded with its neighbors. The drought kept killing crops. Popé answered with control: tighter rules, his vision as law.

Marcus: But the drought didn't care about the victory. The crops still failed. About a year later Popé had been pushed aside, and the Spanish accounts call him a tyrant by the end, which you take with a grain of salt. He's out of the records within a few years. The leading guess puts his death around 1688.

Marcus: Historians argue about him still. The Spanish wrote him down as a tyrant, which is exactly what you'd expect the losers to write about the winner. The pueblos' own accounts are thinner, carried in oral tradition. What survives in both is the same shape: a man who freed his people, then tried to rule them like a priest-king. Liberator or theocrat? The honest answer is probably both, in sequence. The debate isn't really about Popé. It's about whether a revolution can survive its revolutionary. Every revolution faces that question. The pueblos faced it early.

Marcus: Then came 1692. Diego de Vargas walked into Santa Fe with a small force and negotiated. He promised clemency, pardons for the rising, and respect for the pueblos' lands. The pueblos swore allegiance. By the Spanish accounts, it was bloodless. Twelve years after the cords, the Spanish walked back in through the front door.

Marcus: It didn't stay bloodless. The next year the fighting flared again. Vargas came back in 1693 with hundreds of colonists and retook Santa Fe by force. The Spanish accounts record dozens of Pueblo men executed in the aftermath. It wasn't clean.

Marcus: So how is this not just Spain wins eventually? Because of what Spain did differently this time. The empire that came back was chastened, and the difference wasn't kindness. It was fear. The crown had watched an entire province rise and walk out, and it didn't want a second lesson. So the new governors came with orders the old ones never got: grant the pueblos farmland under Spanish law, hear their complaints in Spanish courts, and leave the kivas alone. The friars who returned were fewer, and they'd been warned. The old ceremonies went on, underground, tolerated in a way they'd never been.

Marcus: Before 1680: masks burned, kivas banned, medicine men hanged. After: the ceremonies survive, and they are still practiced today. The revolt couldn't hold New Mexico, but it forced the empire to change how it ruled.

Marcus: The one thing to carry forward: Native resistance was constant and creative, and sometimes it won. Popé's revolution didn't last. But no Spanish governor after 1680 ever forgot what the cords could do. The story of this continent is not only defeat.

Marcus: They won by losing —

Marcus: and the old ways survived.
