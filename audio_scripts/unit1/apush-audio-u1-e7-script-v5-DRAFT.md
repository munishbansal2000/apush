# Pronunciation: bah-yah-doh-LEED (Valladolid); seh-POOL-veh-dah (Sepúlveda); lahs KAH-sahs (Las Casas); bahr-toh-loh-MEH (Bartolomé); Aristotle (AIR-ih-stot-ul); uh-KWY-nus (Aquinas); hoh-MUN-kyoo-lye (homunculi); oh-VYEH-doh (Oviedo); deh-MOH-krah-tees AHL-tehr (Democrates Alter); ahn-yah-KEE-toh (Añaquito); gon-SAH-loh pee-SAH-roh (Gonzalo Pizarro); his-pahn-YOH-lah (Hispaniola).

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: the labor chain — encomienda, the repartimiento draft, African slavery, the casta ladder. Now the empire stops to argue with itself. Spain has conquered half a hemisphere, and in 1550 the crown puts its own conquests on trial: can conquest ever be just? Four boxes: Sepúlveda's case for natural slavery, Las Casas's answer, the New Laws and the verdict that wasn't, and the afterlife. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Twelve minutes. For the conquest: Sepúlveda, the court's scholar of Aristotle. Against it: Marcus, arguing Las Casas's case. I did mock trial in eighth grade, and my coach drilled one rule: a hung jury means nobody wins. Just saying.

Maya: One note first: the quoted lines you'll hear are Las Casas's actual words. The debate around them is our dramatization.

Maya: August 1550, Valladolid, Spain. The emperor Charles the Fifth has paused the conquests while his theologians and jurists hear two cases. For the conquest: Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda. Against it: Marcus, arguing the case of Bartolomé de las Casas — Dominican friar, decades in the Indies, former bishop of Chiapas. Sepúlveda, open your case.

Sepúlveda: [measured] I argue from Aristotle, who wrote nearly two thousand years before any ship of ours crossed the ocean: some peoples are slaves by nature, born to be ruled, incapable of governing themselves. Oviedo's chronicles, the fruit of decades in the Indies, describe peoples who fit the account. They are homunculi, little men, in whom you will barely find the vestiges of humanity. Spanish rule is not cruelty. It is order, carried to those who cannot produce it for themselves.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Homunculi. Wait, like the villains in Fullmetal Alchemist?

Sepúlveda: I do not know your alchemists. In my Latin the word means little men.

Marcus: You heard a cartoon word. He meant it as natural history, and that's the whole problem.

Maya: Marcus, your answer.

Marcus: [measured] His case starts with who counts as human. The peoples of the Indies are rational souls: capable of the Christian faith, and capable of governing themselves. They had towns and laws before they ever heard the word Spaniard. He argues from your own doctors: Aquinas taught that dominion comes from natural law rather than from grace. Even unbelievers hold true title to their lands. Deny that, and you deny your own theology. His standing: he held an encomienda himself, and gave it back in a sermon in 1514.

Maya: [energetic] Round one: Sepúlveda's case. The four causes, laid out.

Sepúlveda: [measured] Four just causes for war against them. Their natural condition, which makes them slaves by nature, whom it is just and useful to rule. Their idolatry and human sacrifice, which offend natural law itself, a law binding all peoples whether they acknowledge it or not. The innocent, dragged to their altars, crying out for rescue. And the faith, which they cannot receive unless we carry it to them. A war that ends such practices is an act of mercy. That is my case.

Maya: [confident tone] First box on the table: natural slavery and the four just causes. Scoring move: when a stimulus hands you Aristotle on natural slavery, write Sepúlveda, the court scholar, not a conquistador. The exam pairs the theory with the theorist.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So you sailed over, saw it for yourself, and came back convinced?

Sepúlveda: [measured] Not quite. I never crossed the Atlantic, and I won't hide it. I relied on the chronicles, above all Oviedo's. But my argument does not depend on which village I visited. It depends on Aristotle, on natural law, on what reason tells us about peoples given to idolatry and human sacrifice. Village reports vary; the principle holds regardless.

Maya: Your turn. You're one of the theologians on the junta. Sepúlveda argues from Aristotle and Oviedo's books. Las Casas answers: he was there, he watched it happen. Books or eyewitness: which evidence do you trust?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Most of you just picked the eyewitness. The theologians heard both and picked neither. First box checked: Sepúlveda's case.

Marcus: [serious tone] He read a Greek philosopher who died two thousand years before Columbus sailed, and wrote off a hemisphere. That's not an argument. That's a permission slip with Latin on it.

Maya: [energetic] Round two: Las Casas's answer. The book.

Marcus: He wrote it down. A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies, finished in 1542, printed ten years later. Village by village: the mines, the forced labor. Testimony from a man who watched it happen, not a scholar who read about it.

Maya: [speaking slowly] And the numbers in it? Three million on Hispaniola, down to a couple hundred?

Marcus: His numbers are the warning label on his own book. He claimed Hispaniola went from over three million souls to barely two hundred. Modern estimates put the starting figure far lower, and most of the dying was disease. The cruelty was real. The millions were not.

Maya: So if the millions were made up, the whole massacre story is a lie.

Marcus: [measured] Not exactly. The question isn't whether he's lying. It's what the exaggeration was for. He was writing to move a king, not to file a census.

Maya: Three million down to two hundred. I'm quoting that in my essay.

Marcus: Mix-up. The cruelty holds; the arithmetic doesn't. Don't quote the three million.

Maya: One blind spot first. Early on, Las Casas proposed shipping enslaved Africans to spare the Indians. His own plan.

Marcus: [serious tone] His own plan, and the worst page he ever wrote. In 1516 he told the crown to bring enslaved Africans instead of working Natives to death. Decades later he took it back: enslaving Africans was every bit as unjust, and he wasn't sure his good intentions would excuse him. If no people are natural slaves, it holds for Africans too, or it holds for no one.

Maya: Las Casas's answer so far: rational souls, ordered states, the eyewitness book, and the blind spots he owned.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] One more push. Ordered states, excellent laws: did Las Casas ever paint them gentler than they were?

Marcus: Perhaps he did. But what he saw was humanity enough. The question was never whether they were perfect. It was whether they were people.

Maya: Before the verdict that wasn't: the fight on the ground. The New Laws.

Marcus: [speaking slowly] 1542. The crown tried to wind the encomienda down: no new grants, and when a holder died, the grant went back to the crown instead of passing to his children. Las Casas pushed for exactly that. For once, the crown listened.

Sepúlveda: [passionate] And the men holding the grants revolted. In Peru, Gonzalo Pizarro raised an army of encomenderos against the viceroy sent to enforce the laws. An empire cannot be governed against the men who hold it.

Marcus: [speaking slowly] Charles the Fifth blinked. In 1545 he revoked the inheritance ban, and the encomienda survived. The next year the rebels killed the king's own viceroy at Añaquito, in January 1546.

Maya: So the debate settled it, and the New Laws came after. The crown acted on what the junta decided.

Marcus: [measured] Mix-up. The New Laws came in 1542, eight years before the debate. The laws failed first. The debate was the crown's second try.

Maya: So the crown tries the question a different way. Not with laws, with a debate.

Marcus: [speaking slowly] August 1550. The junta meets at Valladolid: theologians, jurists, the empire's best minds. Las Casas's case took days to lay out. Sepúlveda answered from his Democrates Alter. Then the junta met again in April 1551.

Maya: Your turn. You're the junta. Condemn the conquest, and you unwind Spain's title to half a hemisphere. Bless it, and you hand every conquistador a license. Is there a third option?

[10-second pause]

Maya: There's. Hear both cases, thank everyone, and never rule. The junta scattered without a verdict, and both sides claimed they'd won.

Maya: So the New Laws ended the encomienda, and Las Casas won the debate. Clean ending.

Marcus: [measured] Not quite on either. The New Laws never ended the encomienda, and nobody won the debate. The junta heard both cases and never ruled.

Maya: Last box: the afterlife. Marcus, the book he wrote to move a king.

Marcus: [measured] It moved his enemies instead. Dutch printers ran his Short Account first: the first Dutch edition in 1578, dozens more before their war with Spain ended. Then London, 1583, printed to turn the Low Countries against Spain. The friar who fought the conquest armed Spain's rivals with their best weapon. What historians later called the Black Legend: Spain the cruel tyrant, proven from a Spaniard's own pages, by men who cared nothing for the Indians.

Maya: So Las Casas invented the Black Legend.

Marcus: [measured] Not quite. He didn't invent it. English and Dutch printers built it from his pages. His reform book, turned into their propaganda.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] But the question outlived the book's misuse. Before Valladolid, had any empire ever put its own conquests on trial?

Marcus: [measured] Lewis Hanke called it the only time a mighty emperor ever halted his conquests to ask whether they were just. The junta never ruled. But the question survived: can a universal moral law judge a conqueror? Some historians see that question as one seed of the natural-rights language that would outlive every empire here.

Maya: Four boxes, let's land them. One: Sepúlveda's case. Aristotle's natural slaves, and the four just causes: natural slavery, idolatry and sacrifice offending nature, rescue of the innocents, and bringing the faith.

Sepúlveda: One case, four causes: conquest as mercy.

Maya: [confident tone] Box one, checked. Two: Las Casas's answer. Rational souls, ordered states, the Short Account, finished 1542, printed 1552. His numbers were exaggerated, his blind spots owned, his case unchanged: offer the faith, never force it.

Marcus: And the encomienda he gave back in 1514.

Maya: [confident tone] Right. Box two, checked. Three: the New Laws, 1542. No inheritance, dead on arrival. The crown revoked the ban in 1545, and the viceroy died at Añaquito in... 1546?

Marcus: January 1546. Then the junta: August 1550, April 1551, no verdict.

Maya: [confident tone] No verdict, both sides claiming victory. Box three, checked. Four: the afterlife. The Short Account becomes the Black Legend in English and Dutch hands, and the question becomes a seed of natural-rights talk. Lost the policy —

Marcus: won the afterlife.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. And on the long essay: any prompt about whether the Spanish conquest was justified, this debate is your counterargument paragraph. State the strongest case for the conquest, then answer it with Las Casas. Graders reward the student who sees both sides.

Maya: One, and it comes with a source. Las Casas, 1550: "They are not ignorant, inhuman, or bestial. Rather, long before they had heard the word Spaniard they had properly organized states, wisely ordered by excellent laws, religion, and custom." What is he claiming, and whose argument is he answering?

[10-second pause]

Maya: He's claiming the Native peoples are rational and self-governing — ordered states, excellent laws, religion, custom, all before contact. And the logic is: a people who govern themselves can't be natural slaves. He's answering Sepúlveda.

Maya: Two. A student writes: "Sepúlveda defended the conquest because he'd seen the Indies himself." What's the mistake?

[10-second pause]

Maya: He never went — never crossed the Atlantic; his whole case came from Aristotle and Oviedo's chronicles. Flip the expectation: the eyewitness case belonged to the side arguing against conquest, while the defense of conquest never left the library.

Maya: Three. The junta heard both cases in 1550 and 1551, and never ruled. Why was a ruling dangerous either way?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Either verdict broke something. Condemn the conquest, and Spain's title to half a hemisphere unwinds; bless it, and every conquistador gets a license. So the junta scattered, and both sides claimed victory.

Maya: One more, fast. Whose pages built the Black Legend, and whose presses printed it?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Las Casas's pages. English and Dutch presses.

Maya: [intrigued] Carry this one out the door: the empire put its own conquests on trial — and the trial outlived the empire. Check your four boxes. Next time: 1680, New Mexico. The Pueblos rise up.
