Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: Potosí, the silver mountain that bankrolled an empire. All of it ran on one thing: workers. And Spain kept running out of them. Four boxes on your sheet: the encomienda, the repartimiento, the Middle Passage, and the casta system with the hacienda hiding behind it. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Twelve minutes, and you'll check all four off.

Maya: [energetic] Warm-up. Name every labor system the Spanish tried.

Jay: [sheepish] Uh. Encomienda. Slavery. The... mita?

Maya: Four, minimum. And the mita's Peru's, the same one-in-seven draft you locked last time. The deep question: what was the encomienda?

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] The crown gives a colonist a bunch of Native people?

Maya: Close. The crown granted a colonist the labor and tribute of a Native town. Not the land, the people. The colonist gets the work and owes the town protection and Christian teaching in return.

Jay: So it's slavery with homework.

Maya: Slavery with paperwork — and the paperwork mattered to the crown. Legally these were free subjects of the king. In practice the work quotas were brutal and the protection was mostly theoretical. Then the system started eating itself.

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] How does a system eat itself?

Maya: [speaking slowly] Disease kept emptying the towns. Between half and nine in ten Native people died between 1500 and 1650, so every grant shrank while the mines and plantations wanted more hands. A machine with no parts left.

Maya: Somebody tried to fix it. Bartolomé de Las Casas, and he'd held an encomienda himself. Gave it back in 1514, in a sermon, and spent the next fifty years lobbying the crown. His big win came in 1542: the New Laws.

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] What did the new laws say?

Maya: Two things that matter. The encomienda dies with its holder: no passing it to your kids, the grant goes back to the crown. And no more forced personal service. Las Casas is said to have read his Short Account to the court, and the court was horrified enough to act.

Maya: Your turn. You're an encomendero in Peru, 1544. Your whole fortune is a grant that dies with you now. Your kids get nothing. What do you do?

[10-second pause]

Maya: You fight. Gonzalo Pizarro raised an army of encomenderos. The crown sent its first viceroy of Peru, Blasco Núñez Vela, to enforce the laws. Pizarro's rebels met him at Añaquito in January 1546, and killed the king's viceroy on the field.

Jay: They killed the viceroy. The king's guy.

Maya: The king's guy, dead. And here's the question nobody can fully answer: if the crown had held its nerve, would the New Laws have worked? We'll never know. The crown blinked. It dropped the inheritance ban, and the encomenderos kept their grants.

Jay: So the whole fix failed.

Maya: The fix failed, and the crown learned the lesson: it stopped trying to kill the grant and started taking the assigning power instead.

Jay: [casual] So the New Laws killed the encomienda. Box one, checked.

Maya: [firm] Not quite — they tried to kill it. The encomenderos killed the reform, and the viceroy.

Maya: [confident tone] One down. Checking that one.

Maya: Box two: the repartimiento. The crown's answer to the dead-viceroy problem.

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] The answer was what, exactly?

Maya: Take the assigning power away from the encomenderos and keep it. Native towns owed a rotating share of workers, and a crown official assigned them to settlers for short stints: farms, mines, workshops. Your turn ends, you go home.

Jay: A draft. Like names out of a hat.

Maya: A draft is exactly right. Repartimiento, from repartir, to divide up. The crown decided the encomenderos couldn't be trusted with the dividing, so it did the dividing itself. By 1549 the reforms were replacing the encomienda, and by 1550 the old system was done.

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] Was the draft actually better?

Maya: On paper: no more outright Indian slavery, and the workers were supposed to be paid wages. In practice? The empire stayed brutal. The crown just ran the operation more directly. That was the real change.

Maya: [firm] And don't mix up the drafts: repartimiento is New Spain's, the mita is Peru's. Same idea, two names. If a question hands you Potosí, the answer's the mita.

Jay: Repartimiento here, mita there. Got it.

Maya: Box three: the Middle Passage. And it starts with a math problem.

Jay: The towns just keep emptying.

Maya: [speaking slowly] Between half and nine in ten Native people died between 1500 and 1650, mostly disease, though the wars and the work helped. Every draft, every grant kept shrinking. Meanwhile the silver still had to come out and the sugar still had to get cut.

Maya: Your turn. The workers keep dying, the work doesn't shrink, and you need a workforce that won't run out. Where do you look?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Across the ocean. The Portuguese had already built the machine: sugar plantations on the Atlantic islands, running on enslaved African labor, São Tomé was the model. Spain scaled it up to the Americas.

Jay: "No matter how many died." Say the number.

Maya: [speaking slowly] About thirteen out of every hundred died crossing. Roughly one in eight. Weeks below decks, in chains, with barely room to turn over. And the ships kept sailing anyway. The dying was priced in.

Jay: They priced the dying in. Cold.

Maya: I used to play hide-and-seek where I'd squeeze into the hall closet and stay too long. Dark, coats everywhere, couldn't move. That was a game. Now make it weeks, in the dark, in chains, and nobody's coming to find you.

Jay: Okay. That's going to stick.

Maya: One more thread, and it's Las Casas again. Early on, in a 1516 plan for fixing the Indies, he suggested bringing in enslaved Africans instead of working Natives to death.

Jay: So the great defender of the Indians helped start the African slave trade.

Maya: [firm] He didn't start it. Enslaved Africans were already being shipped before his proposal. What he did was lend it respectability. And decades later, in his History of the Indies, he took it back. Wrote that enslaving Africans was every bit as unjust, and he wasn't sure his good intentions would excuse him.

Jay: An apology in a book almost nobody read for centuries. Convenient.

Maya: [firm] Partly convenient. But he came to believe both were wrong.

Maya: Last box. The casta system. And it's not a labor system. It's what the labor systems built.

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] A ladder, not a system?

Maya: A ladder with legal teeth. Peninsulares at the top, Spaniards born in the Americas. Then criollos, Spaniards born in Spain. Below them the mestizos, then the mulatos, then Indigenous peoples and Africans at the bottom.

Jay: [playful] Wait — you just said peninsulares were born in the Americas.

Maya: [sheepish] Did I? ...I absolutely said the wrong one. Peninsulares: born in Spain. Criollos: born here. If I can mix those up mid-sentence, you can mix them up on the test, so nail it now.

Jay: Peninsulares Spain, criollos Americas. Got it.

Maya: The system was part custom, part law. Your label mattered for who paid tribute and who could hold office. The famous painting series ran through the combinations, every mixture named and ranked. An empire terrified of mixture, trying to write race into law.

Jay: So where do the workers live in all this? The ladder's not a job.

Maya: By the 1600s, on the hacienda. Big estates. And the flip: no grant of people this time. The power was the land itself.

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] So who actually works it?

Maya: [firm] Peons. Technically free workers, and that's the exam trap. The chain was debt. They got paid barely enough to live on, then took credit for everything else they needed, against the promise of future work. The debt never cleared, so the labor never left. The term is debt peonage — Britannica calls it a permanent labor supply "resembling the serfdom of medieval Europe."

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] Peon. Like the little worker guys in Warcraft?

Maya: Same word. Work work.

Jay: Noted. Forever.

Jay: [casual] So peons were basically slaves.

Maya: [firm] Not quite — free on paper, bound by the ledger. The debt was the chain.

Maya: Lightning round. One hunger, four systems. I name the trigger, you answer.

Maya: [energetic] The crown grants a colonist a town's labor.

[10-second pause]

Jay: Encomienda. The grant.

Maya: [energetic] The crown takes the assigning power back.

[10-second pause]

Jay: Repartimiento. The draft.

Maya: [energetic] The towns keep emptying, so Spain looks across the ocean.

[10-second pause]

Jay: Enslaved African labor. The Middle Passage.

Maya: [energetic] The legal ladder, and the estates where the debt never clears.

[10-second pause]

Jay: The casta system. And the hacienda.

Maya: All four systems covered. Now let's see what stuck.

Maya: Four boxes, let's land them. One: the encomienda. The crown grants a colonist a town's labor and tribute. Labor and tribute — not the land itself. The New Laws of 1542 tried to kill the inheritance; the encomenderos killed the viceroy instead, and the crown backed down.

Jay: The grant, then the fight. Checked and filed.

Maya: [catching] Two: the repartimiento. The crown takes the assigning power and runs a rotating draft: short stints, wages by the book, brutal anyway. Took over in fifteen... forty-nine?

Jay: 1549. The crown does the dividing itself now.

Maya: 1549.

Jay: The draft, not the grant. Checked.

Maya: Three: the Middle Passage. The Native workforce collapses, Spain turns to enslaved African labor on the Portuguese model. About one in eight died crossing, and Las Casas blessed it in 1516, then took it back.

Jay: The crossing and the apology. Banked.

Maya: Four: the casta ladder and the hacienda. Peninsulares, criollos, mestizos, mulatos, part custom, part law. And the estates, where debt did what chains used to do.

Jay: The ladder and the ledger. All four checked.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it.

Maya: One, and it comes with a source. A ship's log from an Atlantic crossing lists four hundred captives boarded and three hundred forty-eight landed. A historian cites this log to make one point about the Middle Passage. What's the point?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The dying was built into the business. Fifty-two of four hundred died, about thirteen percent, right on the average. The traders sailed anyway. The ships kept coming, so the trade kept working. The mortality didn't stop it, because it was expected.

Maya: Two. The crown passed the New Laws in 1542. By the end of the decade the inheritance ban was dead. Why did the crown back down?

[10-second pause]

Maya: The encomenderos rebelled, and the crown chose Peru over the reform. Gonzalo Pizarro raised an army, and the king's own viceroy was killed at Añaquito in 1546. Enforcing the laws cost a viceroy's life, so the crown dropped the ban and kept the colony.

Maya: Three. A 1560s record shows a crown official assigning village workers to a silver mine for one month, then sending them home. Encomienda or repartimiento, and what detail decides it?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Repartimiento. A crown official does the assigning, and the workers go home after a month. The encomienda handed a colonist the town's labor outright — the draft is the crown renting it by rotation.

Maya: One more, fast. A peon owes the hacienda a debt he can never repay. Slave or free?

[10-second pause]

Maya: Free, technically. The debt's the chain.

Maya: [intrigued] Check your four boxes. Next time: 1550. Two scholars, one question: are the Native peoples of the Americas fully human, with rights, or natural slaves? The Valladolid debate.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Four systems, one hunger —

Jay: [professional broadcast tone] and the workers paid for all of them.
