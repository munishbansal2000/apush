Maya: Last time: the collision, Europe crashing into the Americas, and Spain grabbing an empire. This time: what Spain built with it, and why England spent decades trying to steal it. Three boxes on your sheet: Potosí, the Black Legend, and England's challenge. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes, and you'll check all three off.

Marcus: Start with the money, because everything else follows it. In 1545, silver is found at Potosí, a mountain in what's now Bolivia. A year later, another huge strike at Zacatecas, in Mexico. Potosí grew into one of the biggest cities on earth, up there with London and Paris, and for the rest of the 1500s, most of the world's silver came out of Spanish America.

Maya: Wait, pieces of eight? Like pirate treasure?

Marcus: Those exact coins. Spanish silver dollars, minted from this metal. And the crown took a cut of everything: the royal fifth, twenty percent off the top. The rest flooded the world economy. Silver dug out of a Bolivian mountain ended up paying for silk and spices from Asia.

Maya: So the whole economy runs on shiny rocks?

Marcus: The idea even has a name: mercantilism. A nation's wealth is the gold and silver it stockpiles. And all that silver pouring in pushed prices up across Europe for a century. Historians call it the price revolution.

Maya: Okay, that's disgustingly rich. Who's digging it out?

Marcus: Mostly forced Native labor, at least at first. At Potosí it was called the mita. The Spanish took the Inca labor draft and reorganized it in the 1570s: villages sent a rotating share of men, about one year in seven. Down in the mines they crushed ore and mixed it with mercury to pull the silver out, a trick the Spanish also brought in the 1570s, breathing the fumes the whole time. It killed huge numbers of workers. Later, paid hands joined them, but the draft never went away.

Maya: One year in seven.

Marcus: In the dark, breathing mercury.

Maya: And the crown's just... fine with that?

Marcus: The crown needed the money. That was the whole debate. And silver wasn't the only machine. The Franciscans built missions across the frontier. They said conversion, and they meant it. But the missions also controlled Native labor and anchored Spanish towns wherever they went. Then there's the social ladder. Peninsulares, literally 'from the peninsula,' Spaniards born in Spain, at the top. Criollos, Spaniards born in the Americas, a step down. Below them: mestizos of mixed Spanish and Native ancestry, Indigenous peoples, enslaved Africans, and the mixed castas. Part custom, part law. It decided who could hold office and who paid tribute.

Marcus: That machine ran the empire. England told a different story about it. Starting in the 1500s, English and Dutch writers painted Spain as uniquely cruel. A Flemish engraver named Theodor de Bry made lurid prints to prove it: Spaniards setting dogs on Native villagers while priests looked on. Centuries later, the whole package got a name: the Black Legend.

Maya: Dogs? On villagers? Okay, that's horrifying.

Marcus: That was the point. Nothing sold pamphlets like horror.

Maya: So was any of it true?

Marcus: Some of it, yes. Real accounts made the best propaganda. A Spanish friar, Las Casas, had written real accounts of the cruelty decades earlier. His reports even sparked a formal debate at Valladolid in 1550 over whether conquering and enslaving Native peoples could ever be just. The English and Dutch took those accounts and cranked up the horror, while leaving out the part where they were doing plenty of brutal things themselves.

Maya: But if the cruelty was real, where's the lie?

Marcus: The lie was the word 'uniquely.' Spain was brutal, and so was everyone else building an empire. The Legend just never mentioned them. And it worked. It gave England a moral excuse to go after Spanish ships.

Marcus: The empire's built. England couldn't beat Spain at colonies, so it beat Spain at sea, starting with Francis Drake.

Maya: Pirate phase! I had one in fourth grade. Eye patch and everything.

Marcus: Then you'll love this. Drake was a privateer, a pirate with a license from the queen. From 1577 to 1580 he sailed around the entire world, the first Englishman to do it and only the second expedition ever, after Magellan's, robbing Spanish ports and ships the whole way. Off South America he caught a treasure ship so loaded the Spanish crew had nicknamed it Cacafuego, roughly "fire-pooper," and yes, that's the polite translation.

Maya: I'm sorry, what?

Marcus: Sailors. The queen's share alone roughly matched an entire year of crown revenue. Elizabeth knighted him aboard his own ship. The Spanish called him El Draque, the Dragon.

Maya: Okay, Dragon is a great nickname. But stealing ships isn't a colony.

Marcus: Right. So Raleigh tries the real thing. 1587: a group of about 117 English colonists lands on Roanoke Island, off what's now North Carolina — including Virginia Dare, the first English kid born in the Americas. Their governor, John White, sails back to England for supplies.

Maya: And this is the Pocahontas one?

Marcus: Jamestown, twenty years later. Different colony. White gets stuck in England. War with Spain, no ships to spare. When he finally gets back in 1590, everyone's gone. One clue: the word CROATOAN carved into a palisade post.

Maya: That's genuinely creepy. So... the Spanish got them?

Marcus: No bodies. No signs of a battle, either, and no distress signal. The leading theory now: they didn't vanish, they joined the local Croatoan people. But nobody's fully sure.

Maya: Croatoan. That's Hatteras Island now, right?

Marcus: Same island, new name. America's first unsolved mystery.

Maya: So England's big colonial debut is a ghost town. Then what?

Marcus: Then 1588. Philip's motives stacked up: Drake's raid on Cádiz the year before, Elizabeth's money behind the Dutch rebels, and the execution of Mary, Queen of Scots, the Catholic heir. So he sends about 130 ships to invade England and put a Catholic back on the throne. Literally back, by the way. Philip had been married to Elizabeth's Catholic half-sister, Mary I.

Maya: Her brother-in-law invades? Families are complicated.

Marcus: The English meet them in the Channel in smaller, quicker ships, and at night they send burning ships drifting into the Spanish anchorage at Calais. Panic. Then storms smash the survivors trying to sail home around Scotland. Roughly half the fleet never comes back.

Maya: So for the test — 1588, why does it matter?

Marcus: Protestant England survives, which keeps the door open for everything England does next. And the myth cracks: Spain can be beaten. Spain rebuilt. The empire had decades left in it. But after 1588, England stops thinking like a pirate and starts thinking like a planter.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: Potosí. Silver mountain, 1545. And the crown took... twenty percent?

Marcus: Twenty percent, the royal fifth. 1545, a mountain of silver in Bolivia. Forced Native labor under the mita, mercury to pull the metal out. The money that ran the empire.

Maya: Two: the Black Legend. England and the Dutch exaggerated Spanish cruelty:

Marcus: Exaggerated, not invented, and it gave England its excuse.

Maya: Three: England's challenge.

Marcus: Drake's privateers, the Roanoke disappearance, then 1588. Philip sends about 130 ships to invade England. Between the fireships, the English guns, and the storms off Scotland, roughly half never make it home. Spain survives, but the fear of Spain doesn't.
