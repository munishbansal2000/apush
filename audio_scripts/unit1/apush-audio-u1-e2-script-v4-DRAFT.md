Maya: Last time: the Native world before Europe showed up. This time: the collision. Europe sails west and two worlds crash into each other. Nothing is the same after. Three boxes on your sheet: the Columbian Exchange, the encomienda system, and the Treaty of Tordesillas. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes, and you'll check all three off.

Marcus: Start with the why. Your textbook shortens it to three words: gold, God, and glory. Gold first. Europe was hungry for Asian spices and silk, and the old land routes ran straight through Ottoman territory.

Maya: Okay, that's the gold. What about God and glory?

Marcus: God is the Reconquista mindset. Spain just spent centuries pushing the Moors out, so holy war was still fresh. Converting new souls to Catholicism felt like duty and destiny at once. And glory is pure competition. Portugal's getting rich off the African route, and no crown wants to watch its rival sail off to Asia alone.

Maya: So it's an arms race with sails.

Marcus: Which is why they end up asking the pope to draw a line.

Maya: And after 1453, the Ottomans are the middleman and they charge whatever they want.

Marcus: Close enough. Constantinople falls and Ottoman tariffs go up. Suddenly finding your own route to Asia looks worth the risk. Portugal goes first, down the African coast, a little farther every year. Prince Henry the Navigator bankrolls it.

Maya: The guy who never actually sailed anywhere.

Marcus: Never went on a single voyage. But his captains got the tools that changed everything — the caravel, light and fast, with lateen sails that could claw against the wind; the astrolabe, for finding latitude from the stars; and the magnetic compass, for holding a course. By 1488 they've rounded the southern tip of Africa. Ten years later, da Gama reaches India.

Maya: I get queasy on the ferry to Catalina, so crossing an ocean on a compass and a prayer is a hard no from me. So Portugal's winning. Where's Spain?

Marcus: Just finished kicking the Moors out of Granada, 1492, same year, and suddenly the crown has money and attention for a Genoese sailor with a wild pitch.

Maya: Columbus. Who was wrong about basically everything.

Marcus: The big thing, yes. He figured Asia sat a few thousand miles west of the Canaries. The real distance was more like ten thousand. And no —

Maya: He wasn't trying to prove the earth was round? I definitely learned that in third grade.

Marcus: Everyone learns it. Nobody educated in 1492 thought the earth was flat. Columbus was wrong about the size, not the shape. He lands October 12, 1492, in the Bahamas, calls the people he finds Indians, and dies insisting he'd reached Asia.

Maya: Never even saw North America.

Marcus: Four voyages, never set foot on it. Now Spain's holding new islands and Portugal is furious. They'd spent decades mapping the African route. So the pope steps in.

Maya: The pope just divides up the planet?

Marcus: In 1494, Spain and Portugal sign the Treaty of Tordesillas. An imaginary line, 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde islands. Everything west goes to Spain, everything east to Portugal.

Maya: Did France and England just agree to this?

Marcus: They ignored it completely. But Spain and Portugal mostly honored it, which is why Brazil speaks Portuguese today. It bulged east of the line.

Maya: One down, two to go.

Marcus: After 1492, plants, animals, people, and diseases start crossing the Atlantic in both directions. In 1972, a historian named Alfred Crosby gave it a name: the Columbian Exchange.

Maya: So potatoes go east, horses go west?

Marcus: Pretty much. Potatoes, maize, and tomatoes sail east and help feed a population boom in Europe. Wheat, sugarcane, and rice sail west.

Maya: That's a lot of groceries crossing an ocean.

Marcus: And livestock too. Horses, cattle, and pigs sail west, and the horse completely remakes life on the Plains.

Maya: Wait, potatoes are American? Idaho potatoes? My whole personality is a lie.

Marcus: All American. Now the dark side. Disease mostly moves one way: smallpox, measles, influenza. The Americas had never seen them, so there was no immunity. In the hardest-hit places, estimates run as high as eight or nine out of ten dead.

Maya: That's the kind of cause-and-effect the test asks about, isn't it? Like what actually let a few hundred Spaniards topple an empire —

Marcus: It is. Steel and horses get the headlines, but disease cleared the ground first.

Maya: Anything go back east, disease-wise?

Marcus: There's a debate about syphilis. Nobody's fully sure.

Maya: Two down.

Marcus: Last box: what Spain built on that cleared ground. The conquest pattern first. A few hundred Spaniards bring down the Aztec capital by 1521.

Maya: A few hundred? Against an empire?

Marcus: Guns and horses help, and smallpox helps more. But the part people skip: tens of thousands of Native allies marching with them, peoples the Aztecs had spent years bullying. Same story in Peru with the Inca a decade later.

Maya: And then Spain just owns everyone?

Marcus: They build the encomienda system. A colonist gets a grant: the labor and tribute of the Native people in an area. In return he's supposed to protect them and convert them to Christianity.

Maya: Supposed to.

Marcus: Supposed to. In practice: forced labor, brutal and deadly. But one of them breaks ranks: a friar named Bartolomé de las Casas, who watches it happen, gives up his own grant, and spends decades lobbying the crown. In 1542 the crown passes the New Laws: no more enslaving Natives, the encomienda phases out.

Maya: Did it work?

Marcus: Barely. The colonists nearly revolted, the crown watered the laws down, and the system limped on. But Las Casas got the argument on the record: are Native people subjects to convert, or labor to use?

Maya: And that's all three.

Maya: That covers the voyage out. Three boxes, let's land them. One: Tordesillas.

Marcus: 1494. Line in the Atlantic, 370 leagues west of Cape Verde. West to Spain, east to Portugal, which is why Brazil speaks Portuguese.

Maya: Two: the Columbian Exchange. Stuff goes both ways, except the bad stuff mostly goes one way?

Marcus: Mostly one way, yes. Disease sailed west; the crops went both directions. Potatoes, maize, and tomatoes east. Wheat, sugar, horses, and cattle west. Smallpox goes west and kills most people in the worst-hit places.

Maya: Three: encomienda.

Marcus: Spanish grant of Native labor and tribute, supposedly in exchange for Christianity. Brutal in practice. Las Casas blows the whistle, New Laws in 1542, barely enforced.

Maya: Two questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. One: name one thing Europe got from the Columbian Exchange and one thing the Americas got.

[3-second pause]

Maya: Europe: potatoes, maize, or tomatoes. Americas: horses, wheat, or sugarcane. One each.

Maya: Two: what was the encomienda, in one sentence?

[3-second pause]

Maya: A Spanish grant giving a colonist the labor and tribute of Native people, supposedly in exchange for converting them. Brutal in practice, challenged by Las Casas.

Maya: Check your three boxes. Episode two in the books. Next time: Spain plants its flag, and England starts watching.

Marcus: Two worlds, one ocean —

Maya: No going back.
