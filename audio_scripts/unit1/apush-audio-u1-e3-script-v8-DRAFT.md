Maya: Last time: the three Gs, the toolkit, and the pope's line. This time the ships start moving, and they carry more than sailors. Two worlds, separated for ten thousand years, suddenly sharing everything: crops, animals, people, germs. Three boxes on your sheet: the Exchange, the disease front, and who won and who paid. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Eight minutes, and you'll check all three off.

Marcus: Start with the inventory. Everything else in this episode hangs off that list. Westbound, Europe to the Americas: wheat, sugarcane, and the animals. Horses, cattle, pigs. The Americas had never seen any of them.

Maya: Wait. No horses? Cowboy movies are lying to me?

Marcus: For about ten thousand years, no horses on this continent. The native herds died out in the Ice Age. Every horse in every western you've ever seen is descended from Spanish imports.

Maya: My whole childhood was a lie. Eastbound?

Marcus: Eastbound, Americas to Europe: maize, potatoes, tomatoes, tobacco. The potato sailed east and fed a population boom across Europe. It also set a trap nobody saw: centuries later, Ireland would depend on one potato variety so completely that a blight became a famine.

Maya: So the potato is a hero and a time bomb.

Marcus: It was calories first, and history later. And tomatoes — think about that one for a second.

Maya: You're telling me marinara sauce is an American import.

Marcus: Every red sauce in Italy is riding on a fruit that crossed the Atlantic after 1492. Nobody in Rome tasted a tomato before Columbus.

Maya: I'm going to ruin dinner conversation with that one.

Marcus: Please do. And the animals went feral fast. Pigs escaped and overran the Southeast. Cattle multiplied into the herds that made ranching possible. The land itself changed shape.

Maya: Hold on, though. You're making it sound like the Americas only lost. Did anybody over there gain anything?

Marcus: One thing, and it was enormous. The horse. On the Great Plains, peoples like the Comanche took the Spanish horse and rebuilt their whole way of life around it: hunting bison at speed, covering ground no human legs could cover. The horse cultures everyone pictures when they picture the Plains? Those are post-Exchange. The Exchange built them.

Maya: So the most traditional Plains life is actually new?

Marcus: A few centuries old at most. Tradition with a start date.

Maya: Okay, that's box-worthy and it's not even one of the boxes.

Marcus: Fold it under box one. The inventory giveth, too. Now, the traffic had a name. In 1972 a historian named Alfred Crosby looked at the whole two-way transfer, crops and animals and people and disease, and gave it a label: the Columbian Exchange.

Maya: Named in 1972? The thing is almost five hundred years old and got its name in my grandparents' lifetime?

Marcus: History names things late. But here's the part that matters more than the label. The trade was the smallest part of it. Nobody signed anything. Nobody planned it. The biggest cargo crossed without a ticket.

Maya: The diseases.

Marcus: Smallpox, measles, influenza, sailing west with the colonists. The Native peoples had been separated from the rest of humanity's germs for thousands of years, so they had no immunity. When the epidemics hit, they hit like nothing Europe had ever seen. In the hardest-hit places, eight or nine out of ten people died.

Maya: Eight or nine out of ten. Not a war. An erasure, by smallpox and measles.

Maya: Why that bad, though? Europe had plagues too.

Marcus: Europe's plagues came from living cheek by jowl with domesticated animals for thousands of years. The diseases jumped from herds to humans, and the survivors' descendants carried some resistance. The Americas had far fewer herd animals to share diseases with. So when smallpox arrived, it met a population with no defenses at all. No shared diseases, no childhood exposure, no resistance. The historians call it virgin soil.

Marcus: Crosby's argument, and it's the one to know: disease was the real conquistador. Steel and horses get the paintings. The germs did the conquering.

Maya: Do we have a voice from inside it? Somebody who lived through it?

Marcus: We do, and it's devastating. The Florentine Codex preserves Nahua accounts of smallpox hitting Tenochtitlan. The sick, they wrote, lay in their houses and sleeping places, no longer able to move or stir. No one could tend them. The healthy couldn't bury the dead fast enough.

Maya: God. They just... lay there.

Marcus: Whole neighborhoods at once. And the dying went almost entirely one direction. West. The one disease that might have sailed east was syphilis, and historians still argue about that one.

Maya: Box two's checked, and I hate how quiet that box is.

Marcus: Sit with that a minute. Now box three: who won, and who paid. Europe won the calories. Maize and potatoes fed more mouths, and more mouths meant more workers, more soldiers, more everything. Europe also won the wealth — the silver mountain gets its own episode.

Maya: And the Americas?

Marcus: Paid in people. The collapse emptied villages and broke food systems. It made conquest possible on a scale steel alone never could. One book puts the Native population down roughly ninety percent by 1570, less than eighty years after contact.

Maya: Ninety percent. In a single lifetime, basically.

Marcus: And here's the turn the episode has been building toward: the dying created a labor crisis. Spain needed workers. The Native towns kept emptying. So Spain started looking across a second ocean.

Maya: Africa.

Marcus: The Exchange pulled Africa into the machine. To replace dying Native labor, Europeans shipped enslaved Africans across the Atlantic in the millions. The same ships that carried sugar west carried people in chains. Sugarcane was the hungriest crop of all, and the plantations it fed never stopped demanding hands. So when you rank the Exchange's effects, remember the order: germs empty the towns, empty towns mean no workers, and the labor crisis means a new forced migration.

Maya: So Europe got rich and everyone else got sick. Clean story.

Marcus: Cleaner than it was. Maize sailed east too, and it didn't stop in Europe. It crossed to Africa and became a staple crop there, feeding population growth on that continent as well. The calories moved in more than one direction.

Maya: But the dying didn't.

Marcus: The dying went one way. That's the asymmetry to hold.

Maya: So the Exchange doesn't end with crops. It ends with the Middle Passage.

Marcus: It doesn't end at all. The Exchange kept moving. Historians argue about whether the whole thing was a net wealth transfer, the Americas' riches flowing east to build Europe's power. Some say the calorie gain was shared. Others say the ledger only balances if you don't count the dead.

Maya: I know which side I'm on.

Marcus: Then argue it with the evidence, not the feeling. The job stands.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: the Exchange. Crosby named it in 1972. Westbound: wheat, sugarcane, horses, cattle, pigs. Eastbound: maize, potatoes, tomatoes, tobacco. The potato fed Europe and set the trap for Ireland.

Marcus: Nobody planned it. Nobody signed it. The trade was the smallest part.

Maya: Two: the disease front. Smallpox, measles, influenza sailed west. Eight or nine out of ten died in the hardest-hit places. Memory check: the Nahua account said the sick lay in their houses, unable to move or stir. And Crosby's line was... disease did the conquering?

Marcus: Disease was the real conquistador. Steel got the paintings.

Maya: Three: who won and who paid. Europe got calories and wealth. The Americas paid in people. And Africa got pulled into the machine, because empty towns meant a labor crisis, and the crisis meant enslaved labor across the Atlantic, for the plantations.

Marcus: Germs, then silver, then chains. That's the order the Exchange built.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer out loud before I do. One: a 1590s Nahua account describes smallpox victims lying in their houses, unable to move, with no one left to tend them. What's the bigger pattern?

[2-second pause]

Maya: Virgin-soil epidemics. Old World germs meeting populations with no immunity, no childhood exposure, no resistance. The dying cleared the way for conquest.

Maya: Two: a historian argues the potato did more to change Europe than any treaty of the 1500s. Defend or refute, in one breath.

[2-second pause]

Maya: Defend it. The Exchange moved calories, and calories move history. More food meant more people, and more people meant more power.

Maya: Three: the dying emptied the towns and broke the labor supply. What did Spain do next?

[2-second pause]

Maya: Looked across the Atlantic. Enslaved African labor replaced the dying, by the millions.

Maya: Check your three boxes. Next time: England stops raiding and starts planting. Jamestown, 1607, the colony that almost didn't make it.

Marcus: One ocean made one world —

Maya: and the bill came due in people, not treaties.
