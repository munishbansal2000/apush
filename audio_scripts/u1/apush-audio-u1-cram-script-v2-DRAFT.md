Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: the Pueblo Revolt, knotted cords, August 1680, and twelve years of Pueblo independence. This time: no new material. Eight episodes, ten questions, about thirteen minutes. The dare: say every answer out loud before Jay does. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now.

Jay: [sheepish] Ten questions. Last quiz I blanked on three of twelve.

Maya: [energetic] Then this is your redemption round. Question one: three Native regions, and how the land shaped each one.

[2-second pause]

Jay: [casual] Southwest: the Pueblo peoples, desert farmers. Adobe villages, irrigation. Plains: bison hunters on foot, tipis, following the herds. Northeast: the Iroquois, farming plus hunting and fishing, longhouses. Clans through the mother's line, matrilineal, and the clan mothers chose the council leaders.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] The engine underneath all three?

Jay: [casual] Maize. Native farmers built it from a wild grass, teosinte, over thousands of years, and it traveled north. No maize, no Cahokia.

Maya: [conversational] Built, not found. Cahokia: ten to twenty thousand at its peak, a hundred-foot mound at the center, raised basketful by basketful. Empty by the 1300s; nobody's fully sure why.

Jay: [casual] The Iroquois Confederacy: six nations, one council, everybody agreeing.

Maya: [firm] Five at the founding; a sixth joined later. The rule was consensus, not a vote. The myth to drop: untouched wilderness.

Jay: [casual] Controlled burns, cleared fields, fish traps across rivers. A managed landscape. In a lot of places, disease emptied the villages before Europeans ever saw them. Question one, landed.

Maya: [energetic] Question two: what sent Europeans across the Atlantic? Motives first, machines second.

[2-second pause]

Jay: [casual] Gold, God, and glory, ranked. Gold: the spice hunger, the Ottoman squeeze after 1453, and a bullion shortage. Not enough coin for a growing economy.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Historians point to the shortage, and gold did the heavy lifting. Now God: how did religion push the ships?

Jay: [casual] The Reconquista. Centuries of holy war on the Iberian peninsula, ending at Granada in January of 1492. Columbus landed that October. Same year.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Glory's the third. What did it actually do?

Jay: [casual] Crowns racing each other. Portugal getting rich down the African coast while Spain watched from the shore.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] The toolkit. Why 1492 and not 1392?

Jay: [casual] The caravel, small and fast. Lateen sails to claw into the wind. The astrolabe, reading latitude off the stars. Compass and portolan charts, hand-drawn coastal maps. And Henry the Navigator's money.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And his famous navigation school?

Jay: [casual] Mostly legend. He funded the voyages but never sailed them himself. The money was real; the academy wasn't.

Maya: [conversational] The money was the true part. Dias rounded Africa in 1488, da Gama reached India ten years later.

Jay: [casual] Then Columbus sells Spain a shortcut west with terrible math: Asia a few thousand miles off, really closer to ten thousand, with a continent in the way. He names his landfall San Salvador, makes four voyages, and dies still insisting he'd found Asia.

Maya: [conversational] Then the pope draws a line, a Spanish pope handing Spain the ocean.

Jay: [casual] Portugal forced the redo. Tordesillas, 1494: the line moved to three hundred and seventy leagues west of Cape Verde. Spain took the east, Portugal took the west —

Maya: [firm] Flip them. Spain took the west, Portugal the east: the line that made Brazil Portuguese-speaking.

Jay: [casual] West for Spain. Locked. And France and England never signed. The line only bound the two crowns that drew it.

Maya: [energetic] Question three: the Exchange. Each direction, and the deadliest cargo.

[2-second pause]

Jay: [casual] Sailing west: wheat, sugarcane, horses, cattle, pigs. Sailing east: maize, potatoes, tomatoes, tobacco. The deadliest cargo was invisible: smallpox, measles, influenza, sailing west into populations with no immunity.

Maya: [conversational] The deadliest cargo was invisible, you said. Give me the numbers.

Jay: [casual] In the hardest-hit towns, eight or nine out of ten dead. Between 1500 and 1650, between half and nine in ten of the Native peoples of the Americas died. A range, never a pin.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] The phrase some historians push back on?

Jay: [casual] "Virgin soil." It makes it sound like weather. Disease led, but war and the work helped.

Maya: [firm] Both sides, or the grader marks it half-done. Crosby gave the whole swap its label in 1972. Any Native winners in the swap?

Jay: [casual] The Plains. The horse. The Comanche rebuilt their whole life around it. The horse cultures everyone pictures are post-Exchange, newer than the movies say.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Centuries, not millennia. The Americas got wheat and sugar too, but Europe won the calories and the wealth. Anything deadly sailing east?

Jay: [casual] Syphilis, maybe: some say it crossed from the Americas, others say it was already in Europe. Nobody's settled it. And Europeans thought tomatoes were poisonous. Grew them as decoration.

Maya: [conversational] Nightshade family. Fair suspicion, honestly.

Maya: [energetic] Question four: Jamestown. Why did it almost die?

[2-second pause]

Jay: [casual] The king sent a hundred and five men on three ships in 1607 —

Maya: [conversational] The king chartered it; London merchants paid for it. The Virginia Company, 1606. Private money, private risk.

Jay: [casual] Gentlemen hunting gold in a swamp: iron pyrite, fool's gold. John Smith's rule held them together. "He that will not work, shall not eat."

Maya: [playful] Word for word. His gunpowder bag sent him home in the fall of 1609, and then the starving winter: about five hundred went in, about sixty came out. I get cranky when my dining hall runs out of fries. [chuckle] These people ate shoe leather.

Jay: [casual] Spring of 1610, the survivors quit, and sailed straight into De La Warr with three ships and about a hundred and fifty colonists. He turned them around.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] What saved it for good?

Jay: [casual] Tobacco. Rolfe's Caribbean strain, around 1612, sweeter than the native leaf. Then the headright, fifty acres to whoever paid the passage.

Maya: [conversational] The planter who paid, not the servant who sailed.

Jay: [casual] Indentured servants worked it off, four to seven years. 1619: the House of Burgesses meets in the summer; the White Lion lands "20 and odd" Africans at Point Comfort in late August.

Maya: [conversational] The assembly and the landing, same year. 1622: Opechancanough's attack, 347 dead, about a quarter of the colony. The company lost its charter in 1624; Virginia became a royal colony.

Maya: [energetic] Question five: Potosi. Why was one mountain the engine of the Spanish Empire?

[2-second pause]

Jay: [casual] The 1545 strike. Huallpa: a gust of wind knocks him flat, and the dirt in his hands is full of silver ore. Or he was chasing a llama. Both tellings exist.

Maya: [conversational] The wind's the one he told himself. Thirteen thousand feet up, the mint at the mountain's foot. The crown's fifth, the quinto, and a town of something like 160,000 by the early 1600s. London-sized, in the clouds.

Jay: [curious, inquisitive tone] Wait — how does a mountain of silver make an empire broke?

Maya: [conversational] Borrowing faster than the mines could produce. Four bankruptcies under Philip the Second; the silver kept coming. The spending outran it. Now the human cost.

Jay: [casual] The mita: Toledo's 1570s rebuild, about one in seven adult men, a rotating draft for the mines. Forced, but not slavery; they served their turn and went home. Mercury for the poorer ore pulled the silver out and poisoned the men.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Who speaks from inside?

Jay: [casual] Guaman Poma. Around 1615, twelve hundred pages, hundreds of his own drawings, a letter to the king. The king never saw it.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And the century of rising prices? The mechanism?

Jay: [casual] Bodin, 1568. American silver, more money chasing the same goods. Malestroit blamed watered-down coins. Bodin blamed the silver flood.

Maya: [firm] Malestroit's the trap answer. The silver that skipped Spain?

Jay: [casual] The Manila galleons: silver west, silk and porcelain east. Urdaneta cracked the return in 1565, north past Japan to catch the westerlies home. After the 1580s a Ming tax reform let Chinese families pay in silver, and the hunger never stopped. The merchants kept most of the profit, by most accounts.

Maya: [conversational] The crown's cut came from the Atlantic side. Five down, five to go.

Maya: [energetic] Question six: the labor chain. What drove each shift?

[2-second pause]

Jay: [casual] The encomienda: the crown granted a colonist a town's labor and tribute. Not the land. The people.

Maya: [conversational] Then disease emptied the towns, and the machine started eating itself.

Jay: [casual] The New Laws of 1542 tried to kill the inheritance: the grant dies with the holder. The encomenderos revolted, and the viceroy died at Anaquito in January of 1546. The crown backed down.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So the New Laws ended the encomienda, right?

Jay: [casual] Wrong. The encomenderos ended the reform. The crown dropped the ban, and the grant survived.

Maya: [warm tone] Good catch. What replaced it?

Jay: [casual] The repartimiento. The crown took the assigning power and ran a rotating draft: short stints, wages on paper, brutal anyway. By 1550 the old system was done. Peru's version was the mita.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Same idea, two names. The towns kept emptying. Then what?

Jay: [casual] Across the ocean. The Portuguese machine was already running on the Atlantic sugar islands; Sao Tome was the model. The Middle Passage: thousands at first, then millions. About one in eight died crossing.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] A one-in-eight death rate, and the ships kept sailing. Now Las Casas: where does he fit?

Jay: [casual] In 1516 he proposed African labor instead of working Natives to death. He didn't start the trade; it was already sailing. He lent it respectability, then took it back as every bit as unjust.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] The ladder the labor built?

Jay: [casual] The casta system. Peninsulares, criollos, mestizos, mulatos, Indigenous peoples, Africans. Peninsulares born in Spain, criollos born in the Americas. Part custom, part law. Behind it, the hacienda: peons technically free, bound by debt. The ledger never balanced, so the workers never left.

Maya: [conversational] The goal never changed. Only the system did.

Maya: [energetic] Question seven: Valladolid, 1550. The actual disagreement?

[2-second pause]

Jay: [casual] Whether the conquest was just. Sepulveda: Aristotle's natural slaves, four just causes. Las Casas: the Native peoples are rational souls, fully human, with rights.

Maya: [conversational] Lay out the four causes.

Jay: [casual] Natural slavery. Idolatry and sacrifice offending natural law. Rescuing the innocents. Carrying the faith. All from his library. He never crossed the Atlantic.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Aristotle and Oviedo's chronicles. Las Casas's evidence?

Jay: [casual] He'd been there. Gave back his encomienda in 1514. The Short Account: written 1542, printed 1552. But his Hispaniola numbers were wildly inflated.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] The cruelty was real; the arithmetic wasn't. His blind spot?

Jay: [casual] The 1516 African labor proposal. His own plan, his worst page. He took it back decades later.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So Las Casas won the debate?

Jay: [casual] Nobody won. The junta met in August 1550, again in April 1551, and scattered without a verdict. Both sides claimed victory.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] No verdict. So what happened to the book?

Jay: [casual] The Short Account became the Black Legend in Dutch and English hands: 1578, 1583. He wrote it to save the Indians; their printers turned it into propaganda.

Maya: [conversational] No ruling, but the question survived the empire that asked it.

Maya: [energetic] Question eight: the Pueblo Revolt. The causes, and what twelve years of freedom bought.

[2-second pause]

Jay: [casual] Eighty years of missions: kivas, the underground ceremonial rooms, banned, masks burned, Pueblo labor running the mission farms. Then drought, and Apache raids the Spanish couldn't stop. 1675: Trevino arrests forty-seven medicine men. Three hanged, one killed himself, the rest whipped.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] The 1675 crackdown was the spark. What did Pope do with it?

Jay: [casual] A Tewa holy man, not a king, walked out of that jail with a plan. Five years from Taos. Knotted cords of maguey fiber, a tough desert plant, one knot per day. August 10th, 1680.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] How do we know about the cords?

Jay: [casual] Through Spanish pens. A Pueblo man named Naranjo described them under interrogation. Nearly four hundred Spaniards dead, twenty-one of them priests. Santa Fe besieged, the water cut, Otermin's retreat to El Paso, August 21st.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Twelve years free. Why didn't it hold?

Jay: [conversational] The drought kept killing crops, old feuds came back, and Pope was deposed. Re-elected in 1688, dead around 1692. He didn't lead the whole twelve years.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Then Vargas. Talk or fight?

Jay: [conversational] Talk, in 1692. Pardons, respect for Pueblo lands. Bloodless, by the Spanish accounts. Then 1693, back with colonists, and it was force.

Maya: [conversational] The reconquest bargain: land under Spanish law, a public defender, ceremonies tolerated. Fear wrote those rules. The empire had watched a whole province walk out.

Maya: [energetic] Question nine: the New Laws and Valladolid both tried to check the conquest. Why did so little change on the ground?

[2-second pause]

Jay: [conversational] Distance and defiance. The crown was an ocean away with no way to enforce anything. The colonists answered the New Laws with rebellion and a dead viceroy.

Maya: [conversational] The debate gave the conquest new language; the ground reality stayed much the same.

Jay: [conversational] The machine needed workers. Every serious fix threatened the labor supply, so the crown caved and the machine kept running.

Maya: [energetic] Last one. Question ten: in one sentence, why does 1491 to 1607 shape everything that follows?

[2-second pause]

Jay: [conversational] Because the terms were set here — the exchange, the silver, the labor system — and almost everything after runs on them.

Maya: [confident tone] The period thesis. The biology: the Exchange. The economy: Potosi's silver. The labor: encomienda to hacienda.

Maya: Two predictions I'd bet on. One: evaluate the extent to which the Columbian Exchange transformed the Americas. Your turn.

[8-second pause]

Maya: Transformed, almost completely. The biology: eight or nine out of ten dead in the hardest-hit towns. The economy: maize and potatoes feeding Europe, silver flooding the world. The labor machine the dying forced into being. The horse rebuilt Plains life, and the Pueblos held a province for twelve years. Pushback belongs in the answer.

Maya: Two: evaluate the extent to which Spanish colonial rule changed between 1542 and 1700. Your turn.

[8-second pause]

Maya: 1542: the New Laws try and fail. 1550: Valladolid argues and rules nothing. The repartimiento and the mita replace the encomienda. The names on paper changed; the hunger for workers kept driving everything. 1680: the Pueblos rise. After 1692, a frightened empire grants land, a public defender, tolerated ceremonies. The thesis: the systems kept changing; the goal didn't. Then a province walked out, and the rules changed with it.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Ten questions, eight episodes, one unit —

Jay: [professional broadcast tone] and they're staying in my head this time.
