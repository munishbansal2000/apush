# APUSH Unit 6, Lesson 2: WWI — Neutrality to War
## Script v2 DRAFT — Maya + Marcus Interview

**Format:** Interview | **Words:** 1626 spoken | **Experienced runtime:** ~12 min (speech at ~150 WPM + 61 s scripted pauses) | **CED:** 7.4 (Period 7)

# Pronunciation: Zimmermann (TSIM-mer-mahn), Lusitania (loo-sih-TAY-nee-uh)

# Read note: pause tags used — [15-second pause], [8-second pause]. Production strips the header, this note, and converts every pause tag to real silence.

---

Maya: Last time: Europe explodes, America tries to sit it out, and it doesn't work. Today, the story of how sitting it out failed. August 1914, President Wilson begs the country to stay neutral — and by April 1917, he's asking Congress for war. Three boxes: neutrality and why we chose it, the slide toward war, and the break: submarines, a secret telegram, and Wilson's case for fighting. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Twelve minutes, let's go.

Marcus: August 1914. Europe is swallowing itself whole, and Wilson sends a message to the Senate. He says the United States must be neutral "in fact, as well as in name," impartial in thought as well as in action. Not just our ships and our money. Our heads. He's asking a country of immigrants not to take sides in a war their relatives are fighting.

Maya: And did anyone actually manage that?

Marcus: Not really. But the country did stay out for almost three years, and that took work. Start with tradition: George Washington's farewell warning about tangling with Europe's wars was practically a civic religion by 1914. Then distance: an ocean between American farms and European trenches. Most people figured this was Europe's fight, not ours.

Maya: Plus we were fighting each other about it at home. My high school was half Irish kids, and they still boo England at soccer games. German Americans, Irish Americans, everybody with cousins in the trenches. How do you declare war without splitting the country in half?

Marcus: You don't, and Wilson knew it. But Maya, America was never as neutral as it sounded. Britain's navy blockaded Germany, so American trade with Britain and France roughly quadrupled while trade with Germany shriveled to almost nothing. American banks loaned the Allies billions, something like a hundred times what they loaned Germany. If the Allies lost, that money died with them. The economy picked a side long before the government did.

Maya: I always figured we were just completely neutral until 1917. Neutral-neutral.

Marcus: Not quite. Neutral in name, Allied in business.

Maya: Okay. Checking that one. Neutrality: tradition, division, distance, and a bank account that quietly voted Allied.

Marcus: Your turn. If American banks have billions riding on an Allied victory, what happens to American neutrality the moment Germany starts winning? Think it through.
[8-second pause]
Marcus: It turns neutrality into a fiction. America can't afford to lose that money, which means it can't afford for the Allies to lose. From that point on, every German U-boat success is an attack on American investments too.

Maya: May seventh, 1915. The second box, right? The Lusitania.

Marcus: May 7, 1915. A German U-boat puts a single torpedo into the Lusitania off the Irish coast, and eighteen minutes later she's gone. Nearly twelve hundred people die, including 128 Americans. The ship listed so fast most lifeboats never made it into the water.

Maya: Eighteen minutes. I've never been right in deep water — my cousin threw me off a dock when I was nine and I've been negotiating with lakes ever since. I can't even picture that.

Marcus: Germany's defense was that the Lusitania carried munitions for Britain. And that part was true. Small-arms ammunition in the hold. They'd even run a newspaper ad in New York the morning she sailed, warning Americans not to travel on British ships. None of it mattered to the outrage. Wilson sent angry diplomatic notes. And three days later, in Philadelphia, he said there was "such a thing as a man being too proud to fight."

Maya: Notes instead of warships. One hundred twenty-eight Americans dead, and we sent letters?

Marcus: Wilson in 1915, in a sentence. But he did squeeze Germany into a promise. The Arabic pledge, August 1915: a U-boat sank the Arabic, killing two Americans, and Berlin agreed: no more sinking passenger ships without warning. Then March 1916, the Sussex, a French ferry, torpedoed with Americans hurt. Wilson threatened to break off relations entirely, and Germany signed the Sussex pledge: no more sinkings without warning, merchant ships included this time.

Maya: Did Berlin actually keep those promises?

Marcus: About a year. The pledges worked as long as Germany was scared of exactly what eventually happened, America entering the war. And Wilson's timing mattered. He ran for reelection in 1916 on "He kept us out of war" and won by a whisker. He went to bed election night thinking he'd lost. California decided it by fewer than four thousand votes, and his opponent had managed to insult California's governor on the campaign trail. The governor's people never forgave him.

Maya: So the guy who won on "kept us out of war" is the guy who takes us in. Awkward.

Marcus: The slide, in one line. And one correction before you check the box: students write that the Lusitania is what pulled America into the war. Don't write that. She sank in 1915. The war vote came in 1917. The Lusitania moved opinion; it didn't move Congress.

Maya: Box two, checked. The slide: Lusitania in 1915, the Arabic pledge, the Sussex pledge in 1916, and Wilson winning reelection on staying out. And the pledges only held while Germany feared us joining.

Marcus: Germany's admirals spent 1916 running the math, and by January 1917 they'd convinced themselves. Starve Britain and France into surrender before America could train and ship an army — that was the bet. On January 31, Berlin announced unrestricted submarine warfare: sink anything, warn nobody. Wilson broke off diplomatic relations three days later, and in February and March, American ships started going down.

Maya: Hundreds of them, right?

Marcus: Not quite. That's the legend. A handful of American-flagged ships in those weeks, but every sinking was proof the pledge era was over. And then the telegram. Germany's foreign minister, Arthur Zimmermann, sends a secret message to Mexico in January 1917: if America enters the war, join Germany, and afterward we'll help you win back Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona. British codebreakers cracked it and passed it to Washington. American papers printed it March 1, and two days later Zimmermann himself admitted it was genuine.

Maya: My history teacher showed us that telegram. "Want Texas back?" I thought it was a prank. It reads like someone dared him to send it.

Marcus: It reads like a prank, and it changed the conversation. The U-boats were already killing Americans at sea, and every one of those sinkings was about ships and money. The telegram was different. It made Germany feel like an active enemy of the United States itself, not just of Britain and France. The sinkings pushed America toward war; the telegram made the threat personal. Last straw, not the cause.

Maya: Your turn. The sinkings alone almost got us there. What did the telegram add that dead sailors hadn't? Talk it out.
[8-second pause]
Marcus: The telegram put American soil on the offer sheet. Dead sailors had already angered the country, but Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona turned a European war into a plot against home.

Maya: So April 1917, Wilson goes to Congress. What's his case?

Marcus: April 2, 1917. Wilson asks for a declaration of war, and his case is bigger than ships or territory. Germany's autocracy — its submarines, its secret deals — threatened self-government itself. His line: "The world must be made safe for democracy." The Progressive who'd spent four years using government to fix the country now thought a war could fix the world. Congress declared war on April 6.

Maya: Box three, checked. The break: unrestricted submarine warfare, the Zimmermann telegram, and Wilson's case, democracy itself on the line. And the peace he wanted after it, that's its own story.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: neutrality, nineteen fourteen to sixteen. Wilson's "impartial in thought as well as in action," held up by tradition, division, and distance — and quietly undercut by trade and bank loans. Two: the slide. Lusitania in 1915, then the Sussex pledge, then Arabic —

Marcus: Arabic in nineteen fifteen, Sussex in nineteen sixteen. You flipped them.

Maya: I did flip them. Lusitania, Arabic, Sussex. Plus Wilson winning reelection on "He kept us out of war." Three: the break. Germany resumes unrestricted submarine warfare, the telegram goes public March first, and Wilson's case for war is democracy itself.

Marcus: Clean, once you unflipped it. If you can explain why neutrality was always shaky and what finally broke it, you're set.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. First, stimulus-style. You're handed Wilson's August 1914 message, where he calls America "the one great nation at peace," ready to play a part of impartial mediation. What's the purpose of this source — what is Wilson trying to get Americans to do, and why?
[15-second pause]
Maya: He's not just announcing a policy. He's trying to stop Americans from taking sides in their heads. A country of immigrants with relatives on both sides could tear itself apart, and he needs the country united enough to act as an honest broker.

Maya: Second. Germany signed the Arabic and Sussex pledges promising to warn before sinking ships. Why would Germany — the country with the U-boats — agree to tie its own hands?
[15-second pause]
Maya: Because its biggest fear was America entering the war. The pledges bought time. As long as Berlin believed Wilson might actually fight, the U-boats stayed leashed.

Maya: Third. Was the Zimmermann telegram the cause of America's entry into the war? Say yes or no, then defend it.
[15-second pause]
Maya: No. The telegram didn't cause it. Unrestricted submarine warfare did: U-boats sinking American ships, strangling American trade and loans. What the telegram did was make it feel like an attack on America itself, and that's what finally pushed public opinion past the point of no return.

Maya: Next time: the home front. The draft, the agencies, and a government deciding what you're allowed to say.

Marcus: The war to make the world safe for democracy starts by testing it at home.
