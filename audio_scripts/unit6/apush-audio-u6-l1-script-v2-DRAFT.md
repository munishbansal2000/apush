# APUSH Unit 6, Lesson 1: Progressivism

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: Reconstruction died, and Jim Crow took over the South. Now the country turns north and west, to the factories, the trusts, the bursting cities. Four boxes: the muckrakers who exposed the rot, the states that rewrote democracy, Roosevelt's Square Deal, and the verdict, who progressivism actually served. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Twelve minutes, let's go.

Marcus: [dramatic] Nineteen-oh-six. Upton Sinclair's novel The Jungle lands on America's breakfast table, literally: a Lithuanian immigrant family ground up by Chicago's meatpacking plants. Rats in the meat. Workers falling into rendering vats. The story goes that Theodore Roosevelt threw his breakfast sausages out the window.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Did he?

Marcus: [deadpan] The sausages are legend. [chuckle] The laws were real: within months, Congress passed the Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act, with federal inspectors in the packing plants. A novel moved Congress. That's the Progressive Era in one image.

Maya: [playful] I read ingredient labels like crime reports, ever since eighth grade, when my mom made me read the sausage chapter. So what was broken?

Marcus: [measured] The Gilded Age hangover: by 1900, America was industrial, urban, and corrupt in ways the old rules couldn't touch. Trusts controlled oil, steel, and the railroads. City machines traded jobs for votes. Twelve-hour days were normal, kids as young as ten worked factory floors, and Washington was small and mostly hands-off. Progressives believed government and experts could fix what the market wouldn't.

Maya: [conversational] And the muckrakers pointed cameras at all of it.

Marcus: [conversational] Pointing pens, mostly. A muckraker was an investigative journalist who dug up dirt, named for a Pilgrim's Progress character who raked filth instead of looking up. Roosevelt meant it as an insult, and they wore it like a badge. Magazines like McClure's fed a middle-class hunger for exposés. Ida Tarbell spent years on Standard Oil, installment after installment, then a 1904 book: the secret railroad rebates, the spies in rival firms, the strong-arm buyouts.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Cities got the same treatment?

Marcus: [conversational] Lincoln Steffens gave it to them in The Shame of the Cities. And Jacob Riis photographed New York's tenements for How the Other Half Lives, 1890, and let the pictures argue.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Expose it, outrage people, force Washington to move. But who were these reformers? Not the tenement kids.

Marcus: [warm tone] Mostly not: the movement was urban and middle-class, and women were everywhere in it, which gave them a public role when most states still wouldn't let them vote. Some came through the churches: the Social Gospel, Protestants who said faith meant fixing slums, not just saving souls. And some just moved in. Jane Addams opened Hull House in a Chicago immigrant neighborhood in 1889, where reformers lived among the poor and ran kindergartens and health clinics. By the 1910s, more than four hundred settlement houses worked the same model.

Maya: [confident tone] Checking the first box: the muckrakers, and the reform impulse behind them.

Marcus: [firm] Don't write that The Jungle converted America to socialism. Readers ignored the socialist message; the food-safety uproar was the effect. Name the mechanism: documented exposure, public outrage, political pressure. But outrage needs somewhere to go, and the Progressives' other big idea was to rebuild democracy itself, starting in the states.

Maya: [sheepish] I keep mixing up initiative and referendum. Which one's which?

Marcus: [speaking slowly] Initiative: citizens proposing a new law by petition. Referendum: citizens voting directly on a law the legislature wrote. Recall: voters firing an official before the term ends. Three tools, one purpose: route around the machines.

Maya: [conversational] Plus the direct primary: voters picking candidates, not bosses in a back room.

Marcus: [measured] Minnesota went first in 1899, most states followed by 1916. Same logic as the secret ballot, which Massachusetts adopted in 1888; by 1910, curtained booths were the norm.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And the Wisconsin Idea, universities running the state?

Marcus: [conversational] Almost. Governor Robert La Follette, "Fighting Bob," brought University of Wisconsin professors into the government to draft the laws: railroad regulation, worker protections, tax reform. Other states copied the homework.

Maya: [thoughtful tone] So the states were the laboratory and Washington took notes.

Marcus: [confident tone] The biggest note was the Seventeenth Amendment, 1913: direct election of senators. Before that, legislatures chose them, which meant corporate money usually chose them.

Maya: Your turn: one Progressive trusts experts, the other trusts voters. The machine just rigged another election. Which reform does each reach for? [8-second pause]

Marcus: The expert-truster wants the commission form: trained managers instead of bosses. The voter-truster wants initiative, referendum, recall. Same enemy, opposite weapons.

Maya: [confident tone] Checking the second box: the states lead, and experts versus voters underneath.

Marcus: [dramatic] Now the White House. Nineteen-oh-one: McKinley's assassinated, and Theodore Roosevelt, forty-two, the youngest president ever, takes over. He called the presidency his "bully pulpit," a first-rate preaching platform, and preached the Square Deal: a fair shake, in three C's.

Maya: [playful] Wait, "bully" as in excellent? My grandpa says "bully for you" when my brother scores.

Marcus: [measured] Exactly. First C: control of corporations. His opening move was the 1902 coal strike: winter coming, mines shut, and Roosevelt threatens to send in the Army to run the mines if the owners won't bargain. They caved, a ten percent raise, no union recognition. A president had never intervened in a strike on labor's side before. He also took on the railroads: the Hepburn Act of 1906 gave Washington real power to set their rates.

Maya: [confident tone] So Roosevelt broke up Standard Oil, the big one.

Marcus: [firm] Common mix-up, and it's the legend-versus-fact thing. Roosevelt was the loud trust-buster, and Northern Securities was his headline case, broken up with the Supreme Court's backing in 1904. But the quiet record belongs to his successor Taft: ninety antitrust suits, including the one that broke up Standard Oil in 1911. Roosevelt made trust-busting famous. Taft did more of it.

Maya: [conversational] Second C: consumer protection, the Jungle payoff: the Meat Inspection and Pure Food and Drug Acts, both 1906.

Marcus: [measured] Inspectors in the packing plants, labels forced to tell the truth. Third C: conservation, over two hundred million acres set aside, five new national parks, eighteen national monuments, a hundred and fifty national forests. The idea was new: the government holds land for the public instead of selling every acre.

Maya: [playful] So it's the Lorax versus the lumber company?

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] Close, and the split was real. His forestry chief Gifford Pinchot said use the forests wisely so they last; John Muir and the Sierra Club said leave the wild places alone. They went to war over Hetch Hetchy Valley in Yosemite, and the dam won in 1913.

Maya: Your turn: a railroad trust has crushed every competitor in its region. Roosevelt's in the White House. Smash it, or tame it? [8-second pause]

Marcus: Smash it. Roosevelt went after "bad trusts," the ones that killed competition, not the biggest ones. A giant company that played fair could live. Northern Securities didn't.

Maya: [conversational] Then Roosevelt hands Taft the White House, and 1912 blows the party apart.

Marcus: [speaking slowly] Roosevelt walks out of the Republican convention, starts the Progressive Bull Moose Party, and runs against his own successor. The split hands the election to Democrat Woodrow Wilson: forty-one percent of the popular vote to Roosevelt's twenty-seven, Taft's twenty-three, and the Socialist Eugene Debs at six.

Maya: [conversational] And Wilson had his own program, the New Freedom.

Marcus: [measured] Where Roosevelt wanted to regulate bigness, Wilson wanted to break it up and restore competition. The Clayton Antitrust Act of 1914 named the specific tricks, price discrimination and exclusive deals, and said unions weren't illegal conspiracies. The Sherman Act had been used against strikers. Clayton took labor off the target list.

Maya: [thoughtful tone] So the same law that handcuffed the trusts un-handcuffed the unions.

Marcus: [conversational] Then the Federal Trade Commission, also 1914, a watchdog over unfair business practices, and the Federal Reserve Act of 1913, twelve regional banks, after the Panic of 1907 showed the whole system resting on one private banker, J.P. Morgan.

Maya: [confident tone] Checking the third box: the Square Deal, the legend and the fact on trust-busting, and Wilson's New Freedom. Exam tip: comparing Roosevelt and Wilson on trusts, lead with the principle, then the evidence. And the Hepburn Act was railroads, not meat.

Marcus: [serious tone] So here's the verdict box. Progressivism was mostly a middle-class movement, reformers helping the working class while looking at it with paternalism and suspicion. And it had a blind spot you could drive a train through: race.

Maya: [incredulous] The same era as Jim Crow. They just didn't see it?

Marcus: [serious tone] Most didn't look, and some endorsed it. The White House made it worse: Wilson ordered the segregation of federal offices, post offices included, and praised the racist film Birth of a Nation, which glorified the Ku Klux Klan.

Maya: [confident tone] But Black Americans weren't waiting on white progressives.

Marcus: [measured] Not for a second. W.E.B. Du Bois demanded full political equality, no patience, no compromise: the Niagara Movement, 1905, the NAACP, 1909. Booker T. Washington said economic progress first. Marcus Garvey said look back to Africa. Three answers, one community, which is how real movements work.

Maya: [serious tone] And women? Doing the reforming, still couldn't vote in most states.

Marcus: [measured] Until 1920: the National American Woman Suffrage Association had two million members by 1917. Alice Paul's National Woman's Party, founded 1916, went more radical: parades, hunger strikes, civil disobedience. Wilson came around in 1918, and the Nineteenth Amendment finished it in 1920.

Maya: [conversational] And sixteen, seventeen, eighteen: four amendments in seven years.

Marcus: [speaking slowly] Sixteenth, 1913: the income tax, revenue independent of tariffs. Seventeenth: direct election of senators, covered. Eighteenth, 1919: Prohibition, pushed over the top by wartime anti-German feeling, since a lot of brewers had German names. Production, sale, and transportation of alcohol, banned as of 1920.

Maya: [dry] So the same impulse that cleaned up the meat also told you what to drink. Help and control, same package.

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] That's the honest version. Reform for some, control for others, and for Black Americans, mostly neither. The trap answer is that progressivism helped everyone. It didn't.

Maya: [confident tone] Checking the fourth box: the verdict. Four boxes, let's land them. First: the muckrakers, Sinclair, Tarbell, Riis, Steffens, plus the Social Gospel and Hull House. Exposure, outrage, legislation.

Marcus: [conversational] The engine of the whole era.

Maya: [conversational] Second: the states. Initiative, referendum, recall, direct primaries, and the Wisconsin... the Wisconsin Idea. Universities helping run the state. Right?

Marcus: [conversational] Right, La Follette's professors drafting the laws.

Maya: [conversational] Third: the Square Deal, three C's. Roosevelt famous, Taft effective on trusts. Wilson's New Freedom answering back.

Marcus: [confident tone] Regulate bigness versus restore competition.

Maya: [conversational] Fourth: the verdict. Four amendments, bigger government, still a color line through it all.

Marcus: [conversational] Then let's see if it stuck.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. First, stimulus-style: a 1904 magazine series documents Standard Oil's secret rebates and spies in rival firms. A student claims this proves trusts won by being more efficient. What's wrong with that claim? [15-second pause]

Maya: The source shows the opposite of efficiency. It shows cheating: rebates, spies, forced buyouts. Standard Oil won by rigging the game, not by playing it better. The progressive case for antitrust, in one source.

Maya: Second. Roosevelt filed suit after suit, yet historians call Taft the more aggressive trust-buster. How can both be true? [18-second pause]

Maya: Fame isn't volume: Roosevelt made trust-busting a crusade and took the famous case, Northern Securities in 1904. Taft quietly filed ninety suits, including the one that broke up Standard Oil in 1911.

Maya: Third. A student writes: "Progressivism made American government more democratic." Agree and disagree in one breath, using Wilson as your evidence. [20-second pause]

Maya: Agree: the Seventeenth Amendment put senators' elections in voters' hands, and states added the initiative, referendum, and recall. Disagree: Wilson segregated federal offices and cheered a film glorifying the Klan. Democracy expanded, mostly for white Americans.

Maya: One more, fast: initiative or referendum, which lets voters put a brand-new idea on the ballot? [5-second pause] Initiative. The referendum votes on somebody else's law.

Marcus: Bonus banked.

Maya: [intrigued] Next time: Europe explodes, America tries to sit it out, and it doesn't work.

Marcus: [professional broadcast tone] Progressivism gave the government the power to fix the country —
Maya: [professional broadcast tone] and the next hundred years would argue about who "the country" meant.
