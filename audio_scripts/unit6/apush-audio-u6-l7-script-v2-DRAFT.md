# APUSH Unit 6, Lesson 7: The New Deal
## Script v2 DRAFT — Maya + Marcus Interview

**Format:** Interview | **Target:** ~2,010 spoken words, ~12 min experienced (speech at ≤180 WPM + 61s scripted pauses)

**Read note:** In the self-test, leave a full [15-second pause] after each question before Maya gives the model answer. Two prediction beats carry an [8-second pause] for students to answer first. Production: strip this header, the read note, and every pause tag; convert pause tags to real silence.

**Pronunciation:** Coughlin (COFF-lin), Schechter (SHEK-ter), Wagner (WAG-ner), Townsend (TOWN-zend)

# Production pause tags: [15-second pause], [8-second pause]

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] Last time: the Bonus Army marched on Washington. MacArthur cleared them out, and Herbert Hoover lost the country. The crash pulled the trigger, but the twenties loaded the gun. And the tease was: nineteen thirty-two. Hoover's out, Roosevelt's in. And the biggest answer the federal government ever gave: the New Deal.

Three boxes. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now.

March nineteen thirty-three. Banks closing in state after state, a quarter of the country out of work, and a new president saying the only thing we have to fear is fear itself. About twelve minutes. Let's go.

Marcus: [measured] Four hundred seventy-two electoral votes out of five twenty-nine. Fifty-seven percent of the popular vote. And here's the thing about that landslide: Roosevelt ran on a vibe. The campaign promise was two words, a New Deal.

Maya: [dry] Not a plan. A mood.

Marcus: [measured] It was a mood. And in March nineteen thirty-three, the mood met a banking system in ruins. On March fifth, Roosevelt shut every bank in the country for four days, the Bank Holiday, so the government could inspect them. Most reopened by the fifteenth.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And then he got on the radio.

Marcus: [warm tone] Within the week: the first fireside chat. Just the president, plain English, explaining the banks, telling people their money was safe. Over sixty million Americans listened. The next week, deposits beat withdrawals. The banks were healthy again.

Maya: [incredulous] Wait, the government inspected every bank in four days?

Marcus: [conversational] The Treasury took over the shaky ones and licensed the sound ones. That license was the seal of approval.

Maya: [sheepish] I tried listening to one of those recordings once, scratchy old audio on some archive site. Almost fell asleep and felt guilty about it. Sixty million people hung on every word, and I couldn't make it through four minutes.

Marcus: [playful] It worked, whatever you think of the sound quality. Then came the flurry, the Hundred Days. A special session of Congress, and between March and June, law after law.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Alphabet soup time. Which ones actually mattered?

Marcus: [measured] The CCC put young men to work: conservation, trails, flood control. About two and a half million of them over the decade. The AAA paid farmers to grow less, to kill the surplus crushing crop prices. The NRA set wages and prices industry by industry. The TVA built dams and power lines where private companies wouldn't bother.

Maya: [incredulous] Hold on. The government built its own power company? In Tennessee?

Marcus: [warm tone] Dams, electricity, fertilizer, the works. For a lot of those families it was the first time their homes ever had electric light. Then the PWA built the big stuff: highways, bridges, schools. FERA sent relief money to the states. The FDIC, born in the Banking Act of nineteen thirty-three, insured deposits, so an ordinary bank run couldn't wipe out your savings the way it had before.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And the SEC, the Wall Street cops, that was the Hundred Days too?

Marcus: [firm] Not quite. A year later, nineteen thirty-four. It licensed brokers, limited buying on margin, banned insider trading.

Maya: [conversational] Common mix-up bait. Noted.

Marcus: [confident tone] The shape of the whole thing: relief for the desperate, recovery for the economy, reform so it never happens again. Relief, recovery, reform, the three Rs.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] Give me one of each.

Marcus: [conversational] Relief: the CCC paycheck. Recovery: the NRA trying to get industry moving again. Reform: the FDIC, so a panic could never do this twice.

Maya: Your turn, then. The AAA money goes to the landowners, not the people working the soil. Eight seconds: what happens to the sharecroppers?

[8-second pause]

Maya: They get pushed off. The landowner takes the federal check and cuts the tenants loose. Fewer acres planted, fewer tenants needed.

Marcus: [serious tone] Exactly what happened. The AAA lifted crop prices and hurt the poorest farmers in the South, Black and white. That was the New Deal's blind spot, and it was a big one.

Maya: [confident tone] First box, checked: the Hundred Days, the alphabet soup, FERA, the three Rs, plus the AAA's sharecropper catch.

Marcus: [serious tone] But by nineteen thirty-five, unemployment still sat near twenty percent. Down from a quarter, but more than ten million people still out of work. Roosevelt was getting squeezed from both sides.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] The left first. Who was yelling "do more"?

Marcus: [dramatic] Huey Long. Louisiana, "Every Man a King." His Share Our Wealth clubs said break up the big fortunes and hand them out. He was talking about running against Roosevelt in thirty-six. Then he was assassinated in thirty-five.

Maya: [impressed] Every Man a King. That's a slogan.

Marcus: [conversational] And a movement behind it. A huge following.

Maya: [conversational] And the radio priest.

Marcus: [serious tone] Father Charles Coughlin. A hugely popular national radio show. He'd backed Roosevelt in thirty-two, then turned hard, calling him a Communist, a dictator. And Dr. Townsend: two hundred dollars a month, federal money, for every American over sixty.

Maya: [incredulous] Two hundred a month? In nineteen thirty-five?

Marcus: [dry] Fantasy money. But the Townsend clubs were a massive movement, and Roosevelt felt the pressure. Upton Sinclair, the guy who wrote The Jungle, ran for governor of California on a plan literally called End Poverty in California.

Maya: [surprised] The Jungle guy? Meatpacking?

Marcus: [conversational] Same one. Sweeping, practically socialist solutions. That's how hungry the country was for bigger answers. Then the other squeeze, the Court. Nineteen thirty-five: the Schechter case, a poultry company. The Court killed the NRA. Congress can't hand its lawmaking power to the president.

Maya: Your turn. The Court just said Congress can't hand its lawmaking power to the president. The AAA is sitting right there. Eight seconds: what's coming?

[8-second pause]

Maya: The Court comes for the AAA.

Marcus: [measured] A year later, United States v. Butler, nineteen thirty-six. Same Court, same result. Exam tip: Schechter and Butler travel as a pair. If a stimulus hands you one, reach for the other. Schechter killed the NRA on delegation, Butler killed the AAA on federal overreach.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So the two biggest programs, just dead. What does he do?

Marcus: [measured] He goes back to Congress with a second wave. The WPA put millions to work: construction crews, theater troupes, artists painting post-office murals, writers cranking out a guidebook for every state. At its peak, over three million WPA workers. Eight million total before it shut down in forty-three.

Maya: [conversational] And Social Security, nineteen thirty-five. Old-age pensions, unemployment insurance, funded by payroll taxes on workers and employers.

Marcus: [impressed] Steered through by Frances Perkins, the Labor Secretary, the first woman ever to hold a Cabinet seat. And don't file it under the Hundred Days. Common mistake. Nineteen thirty-five, Second New Deal.

Maya: [conversational] And the Wagner Act, the unions one. The right to organize, and a labor board, the NLRB, to enforce it.

Marcus: [measured] The Wagner Act gave that organizing wave legal cover — part of why the sit-down strikes could work. Flint, Michigan, the winter of thirty-six into thirty-seven. Auto workers occupied the General Motors plants instead of picketing outside. Union membership went from three million in thirty-three to ten and a half million by forty-one. The organizing wave split the labor movement too: the old craft-union AFL against the new industrial CIO, organizing the assembly lines the AFL had always ignored.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] And that labor board's still around?

Marcus: [conversational] The NLRB? Still overseeing union elections, still arbitrating fights between workers and owners.

Maya: [playful] Okay, I actually know something about this decade. Monopoly. Nineteen thirty-five, Charles Darrow sells it to Parker Brothers, and a broke country spends its evenings pretending to be landlords. [chuckle]

Marcus: [dry] The timing tells you everything. People standing in breadlines, going home to fantasize about Park Place.

Maya: [serious tone] One catch the books flag on Social Security: at first, farm workers and domestic workers weren't covered. In the South, that left out a lot of Black workers.

Marcus: [serious tone] Another blind spot. Same decade, same pattern.

Maya: [thoughtful tone] So the second wave happened because the first one wasn't enough. Unemployment still near twenty percent. The left was demanding more, Long, Coughlin, Townsend. And the Court was killing the big programs. Roosevelt answered everybody at once.

Marcus: [dramatic] And then he snapped. February, nineteen thirty-seven: the court-packing plan. For every justice over seventy, he'd get to add one more, up to six extra justices. A Court of fifteen.

Maya: [dry] So he just adds judges until he wins.

Marcus: [conversational] Congress thought so too. Not even his own party would touch it. But then the Court started upholding New Deal laws anyway, the famous "switch in time that saved nine." Justice Owen Roberts flipped his vote in a minimum-wage case, and suddenly the New Deal had a friendlier Court.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So the threat worked?

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] Nobody's fully settled that. Some historians say the threat scared the justices straight. Others say the votes were already shifting. Either way, the plan died in Congress. Roosevelt got his friendly Court anyway.

Maya: [firm] Common mistake: students write that the court-packing plan passed. It didn't. Congress killed it.

Marcus: [serious tone] And then nineteen thirty-seven brought the spending cut. Roosevelt tried to balance the budget, the economy fell back into a ditch, the Roosevelt recession. He was rejecting the economist John Maynard Keynes, who'd argued the year before that deficit spending was fine, even good.

Maya: [curious, inquisitive tone] So did the New Deal work?

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] Depends what you mean by work. The Depression didn't end until wartime spending arrived. The real recovery came in thirty-nine, when America started making arms for a world going to war.

Maya: [incredulous] So the honest answer is it failed?

Marcus: [firm] At ending the Depression, yes. But Washington never went back to its old size.

Maya: [conversational] Because the soup left stuff standing.

Marcus: [confident tone] Buildings and laws that never went away. Social Security stayed hugely popular for generations. The FDIC, the SEC, the Wagner Act, the TVA. And it built a new Democratic coalition, city workers, the South, unions, Black voters, that ran American politics for a generation. Americans stopped expecting Washington to sit out a crisis.

Maya: [thoughtful tone] So every big-government argument since is basically this argument.

Marcus: [thoughtful tone] Pretty much. The debates of the thirties never really ended.

Maya: [conversational] Three boxes, let's land them. First: the First New Deal. Roosevelt takes office in March thirty-three, banks closed everywhere. Bank Holiday, the first fireside chat, then the Hundred Days: CCC, AAA, NRA, TVA, PWA, FERA, FDIC — and the AAA's sharecropper catch. Relief, recovery, reform. Checking that one.

Marcus: [playful] And the SEC?

Maya: [playful] Nineteen thirty-four. Not the Hundred Days. Got you that time.

Marcus: [warm tone] You did.

Maya: [conversational] Second: the Second New Deal, thirty-five. The WPA, Social Security, the Wagner Act. It happened because unemployment was still near twenty percent. The left kept demanding more: Long, Coughlin, Townsend, Sinclair. And the Court killed the NRA in Schechter, thirty-six. And the AAA in Butler, thirty-five. Wait.

Marcus: [firm] Not quite, flip them. Schechter was thirty-five, the NRA. Butler was thirty-six, the AAA.

Maya: [sheepish] Right, checking that one off.

Maya: [conversational] Third: the fight and the verdict. The court-packing plan fails. The Court switches anyway, the switch in time that saved nine. Then the thirty-seven recession. And the Depression doesn't end until the war. But Washington never shrinks back. Three boxes, all landed.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give mine. First, stimulus. It's nineteen thirty-six. You find a photograph: a worried mother with her children in a pea-pickers' camp in California, taken by a government photographer. What's the point of this source?

[15-second pause]

Maya: You're looking at Dorothea Lange's Migrant Mother. It puts a human face on the Depression's suffering: a mother and her kids, destitute. And the camera was the government's own, documenting the need its relief programs were supposed to meet. The whole New Deal argument for relief, in one frame.

Maya: Second: why did Roosevelt need a Second New Deal in nineteen thirty-five?

[15-second pause]

Maya: The first wave wasn't enough, unemployment still near twenty percent. The left was pushing for more: Long, Coughlin, Townsend. And the Court had killed the NRA and was coming for the AAA. The second wave answered the pressure and the wreckage at the same time.

Maya: Third: the court-packing plan died in Congress. Why does a failed plan still matter?

[15-second pause]

Maya: Because the Court started upholding New Deal laws anyway, the switch in time that saved nine. And because the fight proved even a popular president can't bend the rules without paying for it. The limits held.

Maya: [confident tone] Check your boxes.

Maya: [intrigued] Next time: the world's on fire, and America argues about whether to pick up the extinguisher. Isolationism, Lend-Lease, and a Sunday morning in December.

Marcus: [professional broadcast tone] The New Deal didn't end the Depression.

Maya: [professional broadcast tone] It changed Washington's job description.
