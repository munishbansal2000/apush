# APUSH Unit 6, Lesson 3: WWI — The Home Front
## Script v2 DRAFT — Maya + Marcus Interview

**Format:** Interview | **Words:** 1852 spoken | **Runtime:** ~12 min experienced (10.3 min speech @ 180 WPM + 61 s scripted pauses) | **CED:** 7.4
# Read note: Pause tags used: `[8-second pause]` (prediction beats), `[15-second pause]` (self-test CER). Strip all tags before TTS.
**Pronunciation:** Baruch (buh-ROOK), Creel (kreel), Schenck (shenk)

---

Maya: Last time: America went to war to make the world safe for democracy. Now that war comes home. The draft, the agencies, and a government deciding what you're allowed to say. Three boxes: mobilizing for war, the Great Migration and women's new roles, and the crackdown on civil liberties. Circle the ones you couldn't explain right now. Twelve minutes.

Marcus: July 1917. The first draft lottery was held, and the numbers it drew started deciding who went to France. Congress had passed the Selective Service Act that May, and by the end of the war about twenty-four million men had registered. Around two point eight million were drafted.

Maya: A lottery for your life. But at least it was one system for everybody. No three-hundred-dollar buyout like the Civil War draft.

Marcus: That was the point. In theory, rich and poor drew from the same pool. The reality had its inequities, since local boards had plenty of discretion, but the design was a national lottery, and the public mostly accepted it. Now comes the harder part: an army of millions needs food, uniforms, rifles, ships. Washington had never run an economy at anything like this scale.

Maya: So what, the government just took over the factories?

Marcus: Less than you'd think. Bernard Baruch ran the War Industries Board, which set priorities, nudged prices, and rationed raw materials like steel and copper. One history text calls its success "mixed": a big bureaucracy, slow and inefficient. Contrast that with Herbert Hoover's Food Administration. No ration books, no food police. Hoover ran on voluntary pledges. "Food Will Win the War." Meatless Mondays, wheatless Wednesdays, families signing pledge cards in their windows.

Maya: Wait, so one agency ordered and the other one just asked nicely?

Marcus: Pretty much. And that's a useful exam distinction. The War Industries Board directed production; the Food Administration persuaded it. Students tend to write both as a government takeover. They weren't the same thing, and the mixed results show it.

Maya: Okay, but somebody had to pay for all of this. The bonds, right? Four Liberty Loan drives, twenty billion dollars?

Marcus: Not quite. The four wartime drives raised about seventeen billion. Taxes covered another eight-plus. So the honest split is roughly two-thirds bonds, one-third taxes. Still an astonishing number, but don't round it up to twenty.

Maya: Box one, checked. Mobilization. If your essay says bonds paid for the whole war, cross it out. Two-thirds bonds, one-third taxes.

Marcus: Those bond drives were part of something bigger. The CPI's campaigns were among the first national ad campaigns in American history: celebrity pitches, quota races between cities. Madison Avenue borrowed the playbook.

Maya: So Washington mobilized bodies, factories, and wallets. Who did the factories actually pull in?

Marcus: That's box two, and it's the biggest social shift of the war. With millions of men overseas and European immigration sharply reduced, Northern factories were desperate for workers. During the war years, more than half a million Black Southerners left the South for the big industrial cities: New York, Chicago, St. Louis, Detroit. Historians call it the Great Migration.

Maya: The push was Jim Crow. The poverty, the violence, the humiliation. The pull was a factory job and wages you couldn't touch in Mississippi.

Marcus: And the wage gap was real. The migrants poured into Chicago, Detroit, Pittsburgh, New York. But the "promised land" story skips the ugly part. The migrants found packed tenements, brutal job competition, and white mobs. East St. Louis exploded in July 1917. The official count says thirty-nine Black people and nine white people were killed, though some historians think the real number was much higher. Families fled. Nobody's fully sure.

Maya: Your turn. Suppose European immigration to the North hadn't slowed, and the army hadn't pulled millions of men overseas. Would the Great Migration still have happened on this scale? Talk it out.
[8-second pause]
Marcus: Almost certainly not. The pull depended on the labor shortage. Northern factories didn't suddenly discover a conscience. They ran out of workers and turned to Black Southerners because they had to. No shortage, no mass pull. The push factors were always there. The war created the pull.

Maya: And women were getting pulled in too.

Marcus: They were. Factories, streetcars, offices. The work was visible and patriotic in a way it hadn't been before, and that visibility became leverage for the vote. But don't flatten this. Alice Paul had founded the National Woman's Party in 1916 and was picketing the White House before the war touched American soil. Carrie Chapman Catt's suffrage association had over two million members by 1917. Decades of organizing.

Maya: So the war didn't hand them the vote. It gave them a better argument.

Marcus: Exactly. Wilson finally backed a federal amendment in 1918, and his words to the Senate that September were pure war logic: "we have made partners of the women in this war... Shall we admit them only to a partnership of suffering and of sacrifice and toil and not to a partnership of privilege and right?" It still failed, two votes short. Congress passed the amendment in 1919, the states ratified it in 1920. The war cracked the door. The movement kicked it open.

Maya: Box two, checked. The Great Migration and women's new roles. Common mistake: don't write that the war gave women the vote. The organizing took decades. The war was leverage, not the cause.

Marcus: Box two leads straight into box three, where the war to make the world safe for democracy starts testing democracy at home. Journalist George Creel ran the Committee on Public Information, created in 1917 to sell the war. His Four-Minute Men, about seventy-five thousand volunteers, gave short patriotic speeches in movie theaters during reel changes, in churches, in schools. Posters everywhere. Some of the ugliest ones turned German soldiers into cartoon monsters, "the Huns."

Maya: My grandpa has one of those posters in his garage. The pointing Uncle Sam one. I thought the guy was just yelling at me my whole childhood.

Marcus: Flagg's "I Want You." And the gorilla poster, the German brute in a spiked helmet grabbing a woman, "Destroy This Mad Brute: Enlist," is all over APUSH review TikToks now. The propaganda worked, but it had a cost. German Americans caught the fear in their daily lives, and the country decided that winning the war required one unquestioning public.

Maya: One unquestioning public. That doesn't sound like the First Amendment.

Marcus: It wasn't. The Espionage Act of 1917 made it a crime to interfere with the draft or the bond drives, or to speak "disloyally" about the war. The Sedition Act of 1918 extended it. Now it covered speaking against the government, the flag, or the military itself. The years get swapped constantly, so nail them: Espionage, seventeen. Sedition, eighteen, the tougher add-on.

Maya: Your turn. A Socialist organizer mails leaflets urging young men to resist the draft. Protected speech, or can the government punish him? Where would you draw the line? Talk it out.
[8-second pause]
Marcus: The Supreme Court drew the line in 1919, in Schenck v. United States. Charles Schenck had printed and mailed exactly those leaflets, and even argued the draft was unconstitutional, a form of involuntary servitude banned by the Thirteenth Amendment. Justice Holmes said no: free speech doesn't protect a man "falsely shouting fire in a theatre." Speech creating a "clear and present danger," like obstructing the draft in wartime, could be punished. Schenck went to prison for the leaflets.

Maya: For leaflets. And Debs, the Socialist leader, he got ten years for a speech, right? Then ran for president from prison.

Marcus: He did. And here's the number students always get wrong: in 1920, from a federal prison cell, Debs won roughly nine hundred thirteen thousand votes. More actual votes than he'd ever gotten, at about three and a half percent.

Maya: Wait, more votes at a lower percentage? Because the whole electorate was bigger.

Marcus: The electorate was bigger, so the percentage shrank while the raw count grew. Wilson refused to pardon him. Harding commuted the sentence, and Debs walked out of prison on Christmas Day, 1921.

Maya: Box three, checked. The crackdown.

Marcus: One exam habit before the recap. When a prompt asks about civil liberties in wartime, don't treat 1917 as a one-off. It's a pattern: the Alien and Sedition Acts in the 1790s, Lincoln and habeas corpus, the Espionage and Sedition Acts here, Japanese American internment in World War II. Name one before and one after, and your answer has a spine.

Maya: Three boxes, let's land them. One: mobilization. The draft lottery, twenty-four million registered, Baruch's War Industries Board, Hoover's food pledges, meatless Mondays, wheatless Wednesdays. Liberty Bonds: four drives, about seventeen billion, two-thirds of war costs with taxes covering the rest. Two: the Great Migration. Over half a million Black Southerners move north during the war, pushed by Jim Crow, pulled by factory jobs. The Northern cities swell with new arrivals, but the welcome includes East St. Louis. Women take visible wartime jobs, and the suffrage movement turns that into leverage: Wilson's 1918 Senate speech, the amendment through Congress in nineteen nineteen, ratified in nineteen twenty. Three: the crackdown. Creel's Committee on Public Information, seventy-five thousand Four-Minute Men, anti-Hun posters. Espionage Act, nineteen seventeen —

Marcus: — Sedition Act, nineteen eighteen. You had it.

Maya: Espionage seventeen, Sedition eighteen. Schenck's leaflets lose under "clear and present danger." Debs gets ten years, runs from prison, wins roughly nine hundred thirteen thousand votes. The war expanded what the government could do, and shrank what Americans could say.

Marcus: Hold both halves. The mobilization worked, and it cost something. If you can explain that tension, you're set.

Maya: Three questions, AP-shaped. Say your answer before I give it. First, stimulus-style. You're shown an Army enlistment poster: a giant gorilla in a German helmet clutching a terrified woman, captioned "Destroy This Mad Brute: Enlist." What's the purpose of this source, and what does it reveal about the home front?
[15-second pause]
Maya: Its purpose is to drive enlistment by turning the enemy into a monster. Fear as a recruiting tool. And it reveals how the home front ran on managed emotion: the same government mobilizing factories was mobilizing hatred, which helps explain why German Americans had such a brutal war at home.

Maya: Second. How did the war change the relationship between the federal government and the economy?
[15-second pause]
Maya: Washington steered production through the War Industries Board's priorities and rationing, steered food through Hoover's voluntary pledges, and financed the war mostly through mass bond sales rather than taxes alone. It was a level of federal economic power with no real peacetime precedent, and the advertising tricks those drives road-tested never went away.

Maya: Third. A student writes that the Sedition Act of 1917 punished Eugene Debs. What's wrong, and what's the better answer?
[15-second pause]
Maya: Two things wrong. The Sedition Act was 1918, not 1917. The 1917 law was the Espionage Act. And Debs was convicted under the Espionage Act for his 1918 speech, then ran for president from prison in 1920. The years are the whole trick: Espionage seventeen, Sedition eighteen.

Maya: Next time: Versailles and the League fight. Wilson sails to Paris with fourteen points and comes home to a Senate that says no.

Maya: Check your boxes.

Marcus: Peace was supposed to be the easy part.
