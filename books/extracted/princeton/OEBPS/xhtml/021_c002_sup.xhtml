OVERVIEW

The short-answer part of Section I involves answering three short-answer questions: the first two will be required, and the last two will offer you a choice between two possible prompts. Each question always has three parts to it (an A, B, and a C), and they will always proceed in the same order:

Question 1: Secondary Source Analysis

You will be given either a single secondary source or a set of two secondary sources, generally written by a professional historian. You will be required to read and understand the historical claims in the passage and then come up with some kind of outside evidence to support or refute the claims.

Question 2: Primary Source Analysis

You will be given a primary source, which can be textual or visual. For this part, you will have to answer a series of questions analyzing the source, usually that involve putting the source in context, analyzing its audience, discussing the purpose, or making a claim about the point of view of the source.

Questions 3 and 4: No Stimulus

For questions 3 and 4, you choose between them (it does not matter which one you choose, and they will be equivalent in difficulty). Each question will focus on a different time period, but the question stems will likely look similar. You will likely need to use one of the historical reasoning processes (comparison, causation, or change/continuity over time) to answer the question.

TIME CRUNCH

Probably the biggest challenge you will have in answering the short-answer questions is the time allotment. You have a total of 40 minutes to answer three different 3-part prompts, which gives you about 13 minutes total for each question (the question without the stimulus will probably take you less time since there is no source you need to analyze for it). Plan on taking about 3 minutes to brainstorm and 10 minutes to answer each one. The name “Short-Answer Question” is well-chosen on this section because your answer should be exactly that: Short. This is not a time to develop a thesis-driven essay. Each part of the Short-Answer Question should be answerable in one to two sentences.

Timer on the Digital Exam!

On the digital exam, there will a timer at the top of your screen to remind you of how much time you have left on each part of the exam.

Here are the steps you should follow for each short-answer essay:

  1. Choose: It does not matter what order you answer these short-answer questions in. Remember, the first two are required, but you are given all of the questions at once. It’s best to preview the questions and see which set is easiest for you to answer first. All of them are worth the same number of points, so if you answer the stuff you know first, you will maximize your points.

  2. Analyze: You need to spend some time analyzing the question to come up with a response. This will be more time-consuming for the questions that have a stimulus like a reading or image attached to them, but even the ones without a stimulus still require some kind of analysis to answer them correctly. Note in particular what time period the questions specify. If they ask you for a piece of evidence within a certain time period and yours is even one year outside of what was assigned, you won’t be given credit for it.

  3. Write: Unlike the essay sections, there is very little pre-writing to be done here. Use the question stem to formulate your response, and then include the specific evidence you need to answer the question. The College Board prefers that you label each part of your answer with an A, B, and C to make it clear which part of the question you’re referring to. It is not advised to type your response in paragraph form. However, the question always specifies that you must type using complete sentences; bullet points will not be sufficient.

Choose, think, write: your short-answer strategy!

STRATEGY FOR THE SHORT-ANSWER QUESTIONS

Every Short-Answer Question is worth a total of 3 points, and you are required to answer 3 of them, totaling to 9 points between all the questions. Even though there are 4 possible questions, you do not get extra points for answering an extra question (and you likely wouldn’t have space to do this anyway).

The scoring guide determined by the College Board for each short-answer question is 0 to 3 points:

  • 3 Points: Response accomplishes all three tasks set by the question

  • 2 Points: Response accomplishes two of the tasks set by the question

  • 1 Point: Response accomplishes one of the tasks set by the question

  • 0 Points: Response accomplishes none of the tasks set by the question

It is important to note that for each part of the question (a), (b), and (c), there is no partial credit. You either earn the point for the task or you don’t.

Many of these questions will resemble the example shown below.

“Convinced that the Revolution was the work of a full few miscreants who had rallied an armed rabble to their cause, they expected that the revolutionaries would be intimidated…. Then the vast majority of Americans, who were loyal but cowed by the terroristic tactics…would rise up, kick out the rebels, and restore loyal government in each colony.”

Jeremy Black, Crisis of Empire: Britain and America in the Eighteenth Century (2008)

Respond to parts A, B, and C using complete sentences.

  1. Briefly explain one point of view about the American Revolution that the author describes above.

  2. Briefly explain one piece of evidence not directly mentioned in the excerpt that could be used to support the point of view described from 1763 to 1800.

  3. Briefly explain one piece of evidence not directly mentioned in the excerpt that could be used to refute the point of view described from 1763 to 1800.

Apply the three-step strategy to answer this question.

Here’s How to Crack It

  1. Choose: If you remember all those Revolutionary War notes from the beginning of the school year, then this one should be a slam dunk. If not, maybe look for an SAQ that asks about a different time period that you remember more clearly. If you need a bit of help, here’s a reminder: the time period given is 1763 to 1800. 1763 is when the Seven Years’ War ended, and when the British started passing a series of taxes including the Stamp Act (1765), the Townshend Acts (1767), the Sugar Act (1764), and the Tea Act (1773). Formal independence was declared in 1776, and the war concluded in 1783. Even though the time period given goes through 1800, you will likely want to confine your evidence to the time periods above.

  2. Analyze: Make sure you understand what the question is asking. In this case, Part (a) is asking you to explain the “point of view” about the Revolution that the author describes. Does that mean that it’s asking you for the author’s point of view? Not necessarily. In this case, this excerpt doesn’t really tell you about how the author feels; it’s telling you how the British felt at the time. So, to answer this, we would need to make clear that he is saying that the British felt that the majority of colonists were loyal. When we answer Part (a), we will want to make sure to put this in our own words rather than quoting from the document. Parts (b) and (c) ask us for outside evidence. The operating word here is “Outside Evidence”; they do not want us repeating what the source is saying, and the evidence we give has to fit in the time period they assigned us. For the first part, we need evidence that supports the idea of the Americans mostly being loyal and pushing back on Revolution. That could include the Olive Branch Petition, Charles Inglis’s pamphlets against Revolution, and Patriot seizures of Loyalist land. For the second part, we need evidence of the Americans being more for independence, which could include Thomas Paine’s Common Sense, the shots at Lexington and Concord, the Second Continental Congress, and the Declaration of Independence itself.

  3. Write: Here is a sample response using some of the ideas outlined above, formatted in the way that the College Board would prefer you to answer:

    1. One point of view about the American Revolution that the author describes above is that of the British, who believed that colonists during the Revolutionary Era were, for the most part, loyal to the Crown and uninterested in joining a rebellion.

    2. One piece of evidence from 1763 to 1800 that could be used to support the British point of view is the Olive Branch Petition. The fact that the colonists exhausted all diplomatic means and pursued peaceful settlement even when war was beginning shows how reluctant they were to break free from British rule.

    3. One piece of evidence from 1763 to 1800 that could be used to refute the British point of view would be Thomas Paine’s “Common Sense.” Pamphlets like his were enormously popular and made the revolutionary cause one that average Americans could easily understand, showing how the cause for independence was not simply being pushed by radicals like the Sons of Liberty.

Freedom of Choice

One beneficial aspect of the short-answer section in Part I is that you are allowed to choose between Questions 3 and 4. This allows you to play up your strengths, while avoiding topics that are less familiar. Questions 3 and 4 will always draw from different time periods (Question 3 always from an earlier time period than Question 4) and subjects, but the historical reasoning processes they expect you to use will mostly be identical. It’s best to choose the time period or subject you know the most about.

Consider the following two prompts. Which one would be most suitable for you based upon your own knowledge?

Directions: Answer either Question 3 or Question 4.

  1. Respond to parts A, B, and C. Confine your response to the period from 1600 to 1800.

    1. Briefly describe one specific historical difference between settlers in the New England colonies and settlers in the southern colonies.

    2. Briefly describe one specific historical similarity between settlers in the New England colonies and settlers in the southern colonies.

    3. Briefly describe one specific historical effect of the differences between the types of people who settled and colonized New England and those who settled the southern colonies.

  2. Respond to parts A, B, and C.

    1. Briefly describe one specific historical difference between the immigration policies of the United States in the period 1920–1965 and the immigration policies in the period 1965 to the present.

    2. Briefly describe one specific historical similarity between the immigration policies of the United States in the period 1920–1965 and the immigration policies in the period 1965 to the present.

    3. Briefly explain one specific historical effect of the immigration policies from either time period.

3-4 Mark for Review

Choose a question to answer before you start writing.

  1. Answer Question 3

  2. Answer Question 4

Here’s How to Crack It

  1. Choose: Perhaps you’re a little foggy about immigration policies throughout the 20th century. Sure, they changed, but how is 1965 significant? If you have no idea, then Question 3 is safer. The differences between the New England colonies and the southern colonies were fairly well-defined, and the “South” could even include the Chesapeake region, if you so choose. And there had to be similarities too. The most obvious one might be that most colonists in both the North and South were from the British Isles. One important thing to note is that this prompt limits you to the years 1600 to 1800. So, the “historical effect” you choose for part (c) should be relevant to this time period.

  2. Analyze: No matter which prompt you choose, keep in mind that the historical reasoning processes are the same. The first part asks for a similarity, the second part asks for a difference, and the third part asks for an effect, so we can see each of these SAQs is asking about the historical reasoning processes of Comparison and Causation.

    Let’s brainstorm a response to Question 3:

    • Similarity: mostly Protestant, with the exception of Maryland; Difference: Puritans in New England

    • New England: religious freedom; South: cash crops, leading to widespread use of indentured servants and enslaved people

    • Both the North and South maintained cultural separation from Native Americans.

    • New England had more early conflicts with and made more of an attempt to convert Native Americans to Christianity.

    • Focus on town-meeting-style democracy in New England

    • Mercantilism

    • Joint-stock companies in the South

    • Similarity: strong British identity

    • Effects: more New England independence from the Crown, fewer economic strings; dependence on slavery in the South; differences in views on French and Indian War and Northwest Ordinance

  3. Write: Here is a sample response using some of the ideas we outlined above:

    1. One specific difference between settlers of the New England colonies and settlers of the southern colonies includes the motives for settlement of the regions. The New England colonies were primarily founded by Puritans seeking to practice their religion without influence of the Church of England, but the southern colonies were mainly founded by Joint-Stock Companies seeking to make their fortunes in the New World.

    2. One specific similarity between settlers of the New England colonies and settlers of the southern colonies includes their strong English identity. Both the Puritan pilgrims of the North and plantation owners of the South were strongly tied to their English identity and brought English common law traditions with them in establishing their own local governments.

    3. One specific historical effect of the differences in motivations between the New England and southern settlers includes how slavery developed in each region. Because the South became more organized around cash crops and plantations, slavery came to play a much more prominent role there than in New England, where it largely died out by 1800 after the Revolution.
