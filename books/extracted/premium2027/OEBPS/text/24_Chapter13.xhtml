The events following the French and Indian War led many British colonists to question their allegiance to Great Britain; that process accelerated during the American Revolution, as many colonists began to think of themselves as Americans. At the same time, a significant minority of colonists remained loyal to the crown and persisted in thinking of themselves as British subjects.

* The Civil War greatly altered the way a significant number of Americans thought of themselves. First, more and more Americans thought of themselves as participants in a consolidated nation rather than in a loose collection of states; they thought of the “United States” as a singular noun rather than a plural one. In addition, many Americans perceived that they were part of an experiment to fulfill the promise of the Declaration of Independence to see all men as “created equal.”

Contextualization (1 point)

Students will earn one point if they describe a broader historical context relevant to the prompt. Contextualizing the French and Indian War could include a discussion of the long struggle among the different European powers and the different American Indian groups to establish control of North America. Contextualizing the war could also include a discussion of the impact of the war on British policy in North America, focusing on the substantial war debt that Great Britain had accrued and its desire to exercise tighter control over a greatly expanded New World Empire. These changes in British policies also provide context for the American Revolution, which began just over a decade after the end of the French and Indian War and grew out of grievances against Britain’s new approach to maintaining its empire. Contextualizing the American Revolution could also include a discussion of the spread of Enlightenment ideas in the colonies and the ongoing intellectual exchange between the Old and New Worlds. For example, the ideas of John Locke—particularly ideas related to natural rights, the social contract, and the right of the people to rebel against illegitimate authority—greatly influenced the Declaration of Independence. In regard to establishing the context of the Civil War, a student could discuss westward expansion—the idea, widely held at the time, that America was fulfilling its “manifest destiny.” The acquisition of additional territories contributed to sectional tensions, as northern and southern political leaders argued over whether these new territories should allow slavery or not. This conflict was evident as early as the Missouri crisis (1820) and intensified in the decade leading up to the Civil War. Contextualizing the Civil War could also focus on intellectual debates in the decades before the war. Some Americans embraced the abolitionist movement and saw slavery as a moral sin that needed to be ended. Other Americans came to defend slavery as a “positive good” that benefited the country. These intellectual debates drove sectional tensions, which ultimately resulted in the Civil War.

Evidence (2 points)

Students will earn one point for providing specific examples of at least two pieces of evidence relevant to the topic of the prompt—how participation in military conflicts influenced American and national identity. They will earn a second point if they use this evidence to support their argument. The specific evidence the student chooses will vary depending on the approach of the essay. An essay focusing on how the French and Indian War and the American Revolution influenced American identity could focus on the resistance to British policies between those two wars—the Sons of Liberty, the Committees of Correspondence, the Boston Tea Party—and how participation in these movements influenced colonial identity. The essay could also focus on how the American Revolution fostered the idea of republican virtue. In addition, in the aftermath of political independence from Great Britain, many Americans sought to affirm cultural independence from European traditions. This can be seen in Noah Webster’s school books for American children and early histories of the new republic, such as books by Mercy Otis Warren and Mason Weems. In regard to evidence related to the impact of the Civil War on American identity, an essay could discuss President Abraham Lincoln’s Gettysburg Address and its impact on many Americans. His understanding of the war as a test of funding principles—liberty and equality—resonated with many Americans. They, too, saw the war as “a new birth of freedom.” The war pushed Americans to ratify the Thirteenth, Fourteenth, and Fifteenth Amendments to the Constitution, redefining what it meant to be an American.

Analysis and Reasoning (2 points)

Students must develop their argument by expanding upon the thesis. Students can earn up to two points in this section. One point will be granted if the student successfully uses historical reasoning (in this case, causation) to frame or structure an argument that addresses the prompt. Students can earn a second point if they can demonstrate a complex understanding of the historical development that is the focus of the prompt through sophisticated argumentation and/or effective use of evidence. For the first point, an essay could explain and evaluate several different ways that the French and Indian War and the American Revolution influenced colonial and American Identity, or that the Civil War influenced American identity. After the American Revolution, many Americans had different perceptions of what it meant to be a citizen in the new republic. These differences are evident in the development of the first political parties—the Democratic-Republicans and the Federalists. Likewise, northerners and southerners had decidedly different views about what it meant to be American in the post-Civil War world. The point for historical complexity could be earned in several ways, including looking at causes of the military conflicts under discussion as well as their effects on American identity. Or, students could qualify or modify their argument by considering diverse or alternative views or evidence, such as the perspective of American Indians, African Americans, or women in regard to identity. For example, in the decades after the American Revolution, American Indians resisted encroachments on their territory in the Great Lakes region. Leaders such as Tecumseh and his brother, Tenskwatawa, known as “the Prophet,” encouraged American Indian groups to recognize their commonalities and unite in the face of ongoing conflicts with the U.S. military. In regard to the Civil War, the self-perceptions of freedmen and women might differ from those of white Americans in regard to identity. For this final point, students can also incorporate additional evidence (at least four pieces) that supports a nuanced or complex argument relevant to the prompt.
