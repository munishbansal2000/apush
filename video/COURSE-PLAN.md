# APUSH Video Course Plan — "APUSH Explained" content series

**Workstream C deliverable. Repo:** munishbansal2000/apush, main. **Status:** plan; scripts/visuals/drills are workstreams B/D/E.
**Authority:** College Board APUSH CED Effective Fall 2026 (exam Friday, May 7, 2027, fully digital Bluebook); `build/cb-codes.json` (105 topic codes + RP1–RP3); `build/engagement-playbook.md` (series format); `build/video-series-bible.md`; the 9 period reviews in `build/content/period-reviews/`.

## Series format (binding on every video)

- **Length:** 6–8 min per video (~900–1,200 words of narration).
- **Skeleton:** hook → context frame → 3 beats (enumerated: first/second/third) → significance landing ("the point is…") → close (final content sentence → dual CTA: next video + drill set → signature sign-off). Never open with a question; declarations with energy.
- **Per-video requirements:** ≥1 callback to an earlier video; "remember this" flags reserved for genuine exam-movers; primary-source cold opens wherever a pre-1930 public-domain quote exists (doc_zoom); ≥3 motion primitives (timeline/map/kinetic-text/split/chart/doc-zoom/counter); no slides-only episodes.
- **Differentiation (what the transcript corpus never does):** "what historians still argue about" beats (feeds the DBQ complexity point — 0.15/1, the worst 2025 Chief Reader component); 30-second counterfactual cold opens; direct primary-source voices; present-tense immersion sequences; animated maps/timelines as the primary visual language.
- **Exam-aside rule:** implicit pressure only ("you need to know"); one explicit rubric mechanic per video max. Exam technique lives in the 53 grading-walkthrough videos, not here.
- **Language:** plain teacher voice, no sales-jargon; working titles and angles are ORIGINAL — never the transcript corpus's titles or angles (mechanics abstracted, content ours).

## Unit 1 — 1491–1607: Contact (5 videos)

### Chapter 1.1 — Three Worlds on the Eve of Contact

#### vid-u1-01 — "The World in 1491"
- **Topics:** 1.1 · **Length:** 7 min
- **Narrative question:** What did the Americas, Europe, and Africa each look like right before contact — and why did the encounter happen when it did?
- **Brief:** Opens the whole course: in 1491 the Americas held millions of people in societies from Mexica and Inca empires to Pueblo farmers and Pacific Northwest fishing peoples, while Iberian Europe was finishing the Reconquista and perfecting ocean sailing. The video frames Period 1 as the collision of three worlds with three different logics — tribute empires, crusading kingdoms, and kinship villages. Closes by naming the exchange and the catastrophe to come without front-loading them.
- **Visuals:** animated world map (three-region zoom); timeline 1491–1607; artifact images (pottery, caravel model).

#### vid-u1-02 — "Three Ways to Live in America"
- **Topics:** 1.2 · **Length:** 7 min
- **Narrative question:** Why were some Native societies huge and hierarchical while others stayed small and mobile?
- **Brief:** The organizing fact of Native America was maize: where it grew, dense populations and chiefdoms followed (Mississippian Cahokia, the Mexica, the Inca); where it didn't, life stayed smaller and more mobile. Walks through the major culture areas — Southwest Pueblos, Northeast woodlands, Great Plains, Pacific Northwest — each adapted to its environment. **Remember-this flag:** agriculture → population density → social complexity is the exam's favorite Period 1 logic chain.
- **Visuals:** culture-area map with animated shading; split_scene (maize vs. non-maize societies); artifact images.

### Chapter 1.2 — Europe Reaches America

#### vid-u1-03 — "Why Europe Sailed West"
- **Topics:** 1.3 · **Length:** 6 min
- **Narrative question:** Why did tiny Portugal and newly-unified Spain suddenly start crossing oceans in the 1400s?
- **Brief:** Columbus was hunting Asia, not America: Portuguese caravel technology, the spice trade, and Spain's crusading culture after Granada (1492) created the push. Covers the four voyages, the 1507 naming of "America," and why the Spanish model was conquest-first while the French (Cartier, fur trade) and English (failed Roanoke, 1587) came later and differently. Counterfactual cold open: what if Columbus had found the Asia he was looking for?
- **Visuals:** animated route map (Iberian coast → Caribbean); caravel diagram; timeline of voyages.

#### vid-u1-04 — "Conquest, Silver, and the Exchange"
- **Topics:** 1.4, 1.5 · **Length:** 8 min
- **Narrative question:** How did a few hundred Spaniards topple two empires — and what did the whole world get in return?
- **Brief:** Cortes (1519–21) and Pizarro (1532) won with disease and Native alliances more than guns; Spain then built an empire on forced labor (encomienda) and silver, above all Potosí (1545), whose output flooded European treasuries. The second half is the Columbian Exchange as genuinely two-way: maize, potatoes, and tomatoes to Europe; wheat, horses, sugar — and smallpox — to the Americas. Closes on the casta system and the fateful shift to enslaved African labor under the asiento.
- **Visuals:** animated conquest route map; counter_scene (silver output); exchange diagram (two-way arrows); Potosí engraving.

### Chapter 1.3 — The Argument Over Conquest

#### vid-u1-05 — "Who Had the Right?"
- **Topics:** 1.6, 1.7 · RP2 · **Length:** 7 min
- **Narrative question:** Did anyone at the time think conquest was wrong — and what did their argument change?
- **Brief:** Bartolomé de las Casas campaigned against Native enslavement, winning the New Laws of 1542 and debating Sepúlveda at Valladolid (1550–51) over whether Native peoples had natural rights — a primary-source-voices video built around the debaters' own words. Then the reasoning-process turn (RP2/Causation): weighing the causes of the demographic catastrophe — disease, warfare, labor systems — and why Spain replaced Native forced labor with enslaved Africans. This is the corpus's biggest Period 1 opening (zero transcript coverage of Valladolid).
- **Visuals:** doc_zoom on Las Casas/Sepúlveda excerpts (PD); debate split_scene; causation chain diagram.

## Unit 2 — 1607–1754: Colonization (7 videos)

### Chapter 2.1 — Three Models of Empire

#### vid-u2-01 — "Three Europes, One Coast"
- **Topics:** 2.1 · **Length:** 6 min
- **Narrative question:** Why did the Spanish conquer, the French trade, and the English settle?
- **Brief:** The context opener: by 1607 three European models were in play in North America — Spanish conquest built on silver and missions, French fur-trade alliances along the St. Lawrence, and English settler colonies founded by joint-stock companies. Each model produced a different relationship with Native peoples and a different economy. Sets up the period's driving question: which model would shape the future United States?
- **Visuals:** three-column animated map (Spanish/French/English zones); timeline 1588–1607.

#### vid-u2-02 — "Jamestown's Desperate Gamble"
- **Topics:** 2.2 · **Length:** 7 min
- **Narrative question:** Why did England's first permanent colony nearly starve — and what saved it?
- **Brief:** Jamestown (1607) was a business venture, not a community: gentlemen-adventurers hunting gold who nearly died in the "starving time" until tobacco made the colony pay. Contrasts with Plymouth (1620), a religious community that survived through a different social logic, and the later proprietary and royal colonies. Present-tense immersion opening: it's 1609, you're a Jamestown settler, and the ships aren't coming.
- **Visuals:** animated settlement map; split_scene (Jamestown vs. Plymouth); tobacco advertisement artifact.

### Chapter 2.2 — Thirteen Different Colonies

#### vid-u2-03 — "Three Colonial Americas"
- **Topics:** 2.3 · **Length:** 7 min
- **Narrative question:** Was there ever really one "colonial America," or three?
- **Brief:** New England (town meetings, mixed farming, fishing), the Middle colonies (breadbasket diversity, Philadelphia), and the Southern colonies (plantation staple crops) developed distinct economies, labor systems, and political cultures. The video's comparison engine runs throughout — every fact is paired with its regional contrast — because the exam tests regions against each other, not in isolation.
- **Visuals:** three-region animated map with economic icons; chart_scene (population/economic growth by region).

#### vid-u2-04 — "Sugar, Ships, and the Middle Passage"
- **Topics:** 2.4 · **Length:** 8 min
- **Narrative question:** What made the Atlantic economy run — and who paid for it?
- **Brief:** Mercantilism was the theory (colonies exist to enrich the mother country); the triangular trade was the practice — manufactured goods to Africa, enslaved people to the Americas, sugar and tobacco to Europe. The Middle Passage gets the primary-source-voices treatment (PD accounts), then the significance landing: this trade built colonial wealth and entrenched racial slavery as an economic foundation, not an accident.
- **Visuals:** animated triangle-trade map; ship diagram; doc_zoom on a PD Middle Passage account.

#### vid-u2-05 — "Allies, Enemies, and Everything Between"
- **Topics:** 2.5 · **Length:** 7 min
- **Narrative question:** Were Native peoples just victims of colonization — or players in it?
- **Brief:** Pushes past the victim narrative: Native nations traded, allied, converted, resisted, and played Europeans against each other — from the Powhatan Confederacy's shifting stance to the Pueblo Revolt (1680) and Metacom's (King Philip's) War (1675–76). Covers diplomacy, intermarriage, missionary efforts, and the environmental consequences of the fur trade. **Remember-this flag:** Native agency is the exam's Period 2 through-line.
- **Visuals:** alliance/conflict animated map; timeline of major conflicts; artifact images (wampum, trade goods).

### Chapter 2.3 — Slavery and Society

#### vid-u2-06 — "How Slavery Became Racial"
- **Topics:** 2.6 · **Length:** 8 min
- **Narrative question:** Slavery wasn't always racial in the colonies — so when and why did it become so?
- **Brief:** Early Chesapeake labor was mixed (indentured Europeans and enslaved Africans); Bacon's Rebellion (1676) scared planters into a system that divided poor whites from enslaved Blacks, and slave codes hardened into lifelong, hereditary, racial slavery. Covers the Stono Rebellion (1739) as resistance and the regional contrast: slavery's centrality in the South vs. its smaller-scale presence in the North. "What historians still argue about" beat: how much did racism cause slavery vs. slavery cause racism?
- **Visuals:** timeline (1619→1705 slave codes); doc_zoom on a Virginia slave code (PD); chart_scene (enslaved population growth).

#### vid-u2-07 — "Awakenings and Arguments"
- **Topics:** 2.7, 2.8 · RP1 · **Length:** 7 min
- **Narrative question:** What did the colonies argue about before they argued about Britain?
- **Brief:** The First Great Awakening (Edwards, Whitefield) challenged established churches and seeded democratic habits; Enlightenment ideas (Franklin, republican thought) offered a new political vocabulary; colonial assemblies practiced self-rule while social hierarchies (gentry, middling, enslaved) hardened. Then the RP1/Comparison wrap: the three colonial regions compared head-to-head, and the exam skill of structuring a comparison (similarities *and* differences, with a reason why).
- **Visuals:** revival-meeting engraving; split_scene (Awakening vs. Enlightenment); comparison table animation.

## Unit 3 — 1754–1800: Revolution and Republic (12 videos)

### Chapter 3.1 — The Break with Britain

#### vid-u3-01 — "The Colonies on the Brink"
- **Topics:** 3.1 · **Length:** 6 min
- **Narrative question:** How did the most loyal British subjects in the world become revolutionaries in twenty years?
- **Brief:** The context opener: in 1754 the colonies were prosperous, growing, and proudly British — with colonial assemblies, the Albany Plan's failed unity bid, and a French empire pressing from the north and west. Establishes the three threads the period will braid: imperial rivalry, colonial self-government, and the new ideas crossing the Atlantic. Ends where the war begins.
- **Visuals:** 1754 North America map (British/French/Spanish/Native zones); timeline 1754–1800.

#### vid-u3-02 — "The War That Made America Rebel"
- **Topics:** 3.2 · **Length:** 7 min
- **Narrative question:** How did winning a war together drive Britain and its colonies apart?
- **Brief:** The Seven Years' War (1754–63) removed France from North America but left Britain with a huge debt and a new attitude: the colonies should pay and obey (Proclamation of 1763, standing armies, tighter enforcement). Colonial contributions to the war — and exclusion from its spoils — taught the colonies both military confidence and resentment. Significance landing: the war didn't cause the Revolution, it created the conditions for it.
- **Visuals:** animated war map (territory changes to 1763); counter_scene (British war debt); timeline.

#### vid-u3-03 — "No Taxation Without Representation"
- **Topics:** 3.3 · **Length:** 8 min
- **Narrative question:** Why did a tax on paper provoke a revolution?
- **Brief:** Stamp Act (1765) → Townshend duties → Boston Massacre (1770) → Tea Act → Intolerable Acts (1774): the crisis escalates as Parliament asserts sovereignty and colonists answer with boycotts, committees of correspondence, and the First Continental Congress. Kinetic-text treatment of the slogan itself, with the crucial distinction the exam tests: colonists objected to *Parliament's authority to tax*, not the amount. Present-tense immersion: Boston, December 1773.
- **Visuals:** kinetic_text ("NO TAXATION WITHOUT REPRESENTATION"); escalation timeline; Boston Massacre engraving (PD).

#### vid-u3-04 — "The Ideas Behind the Guns"
- **Topics:** 3.4 · **Length:** 7 min
- **Narrative question:** Would the Revolution have happened without Enlightenment philosophy?
- **Brief:** Locke's natural rights, Montesquieu's separation of powers, and republican virtue gave colonists a language for resistance; Paine's *Common Sense* (1776) translated philosophy into a bestseller. Primary-source-voices cold open from the Declaration's preamble (doc_zoom), then the hard question: how much did ideas *cause* the Revolution versus justify it after the fact? **Remember-this flag:** the exam tests philosophical foundations as causes, not decoration.
- **Visuals:** doc_zoom (Declaration preamble, PD); thinker-portrait montage; ideas→events flow diagram.

#### vid-u3-05 — "How the Underdog Won"
- **Topics:** 3.5 · **Length:** 8 min
- **Narrative question:** How does a ragtag army beat the world's greatest empire?
- **Brief:** The war itself: Washington's survival strategy, Saratoga (1777) bringing France in, the Southern campaign, Yorktown (1781). Beats are organized as American advantages — home field, French money and navy, British overreach — not just battle narration. Counterfactual cold open: what if France had stayed out? Closes on the Treaty of Paris (1783) and the enormous new nation nobody knew how to govern.
- **Visuals:** animated campaign map; split_scene (British vs. American advantages); Yorktown painting (PD).

#### vid-u3-06 — "Who Got Freedom?"
- **Topics:** 3.6 · **Length:** 7 min
- **Narrative question:** The Revolution promised liberty — who actually received it?
- **Brief:** The uneven application of revolutionary ideals: northern states began gradual emancipation, religious liberty expanded (Virginia Statute, 1786), primogeniture and entail were abolished — while slavery deepened in the South and women gained little politically. Abigail Adams's "remember the ladies" letter (PD voice) opens the video. Significance landing: the Revolution created the *language* of equality that later movements would weaponize.
- **Visuals:** doc_zoom (Abigail Adams letter, PD); emancipation timeline by state; split_scene (North vs. South trajectories).

### Chapter 3.2 — Inventing the Republic

#### vid-u3-07 — "The Government That Couldn't"
- **Topics:** 3.7 · **Length:** 7 min
- **Narrative question:** Why did the government that won the war fail at governing?
- **Brief:** The Articles of Confederation: a confederation that couldn't tax, couldn't regulate commerce, and couldn't pay its debts — with Shays' Rebellion (1786–87) as the proof that the system was breaking. But the video is fair to the Articles' achievements (Northwest Ordinance, 1787) so the failure story is honest, not cartoonish. "What historians still argue about": was the Constitution a necessary fix or a counter-revolution by elites?
- **Visuals:** Articles structure diagram; Shays' Rebellion print (PD); Northwest Ordinance map.

#### vid-u3-08 — "The Fight to Ratify"
- **Topics:** 3.8 · **Length:** 7 min
- **Narrative question:** The Constitution was written in secret — how did anyone get the country to agree to it?
- **Brief:** The Philadelphia Convention (1787): the Virginia vs. New Jersey plans, the Great Compromise, the Three-Fifths Compromise — then the ratification battle, Federalists (Hamilton, Madison, Jay's *Federalist Papers*) vs. Anti-Federalists demanding a bill of rights. Primary-source voices from *Federalist No. 51* (PD). Closes with the Bill of Rights (1791) as the price of ratification.
- **Visuals:** convention scene painting (PD); doc_zoom (Federalist excerpt); ratification vote map.

#### vid-u3-09 — "The Constitution's Bargains"
- **Topics:** 3.9 · **Length:** 8 min
- **Narrative question:** What deals did the founders make to get a Constitution — and what did each deal cost?
- **Brief:** The Constitution as a bundle of compromises: federalism (divided sovereignty), separation of powers with checks and balances, the slavery compromises (Three-Fifths, fugitive slave clause, 1808 slave-trade clause). Each compromise gets its own beat with its long-term cost attached — because the exam tests the Constitution's slavery bargains as seeds of the Civil War. **Remember-this flag:** every compromise here reappears as a crisis later.
- **Visuals:** Constitution structure diagram (animated); compromise→consequence chain; parchment doc_zoom.

### Chapter 3.3 — The Republic Tested

#### vid-u3-10 — "Hamilton vs. Jefferson"
- **Topics:** 3.10 · **Length:** 8 min
- **Narrative question:** The founders agreed on independence — so why did they immediately start fighting each other?
- **Brief:** Washington's precedents (cabinet, farewell warning), Hamilton's financial plan (national bank, assumption, tariffs), the birth of the Federalists and Democratic-Republicans, and the first party fight over the French Revolution, the Whiskey Rebellion (1794), and Jay's Treaty. The Alien and Sedition Acts (1798) and the Virginia/Kentucky Resolutions close the video — the republic's first free-speech crisis and the first claim of nullification.
- **Visuals:** split_scene (Hamilton vs. Jefferson visions); timeline 1789–1800; Sedition Act broadside (PD).

#### vid-u3-11 — "Becoming American"
- **Topics:** 3.11, 3.12 · **Length:** 7 min
- **Narrative question:** What made people on the frontier feel "American" instead of Virginian or Pennsylvanian?
- **Brief:** Two threads: the development of a distinct American identity (Webster's dictionary, American art and architecture, the idea of republican motherhood) and westward movement (Northwest Ordinance's orderly settlement, migration over the Appalachians, early immigration). The video argues identity was *built* — by schools, print culture, and shared movement west — not inherited.
- **Visuals:** frontier migration map; Webster's speller artifact; early American art montage (PD).

#### vid-u3-12 — "Revolution: What Actually Changed?"
- **Topics:** 3.13 · RP3 · **Length:** 7 min
- **Narrative question:** After all the blood and philosophy, what was genuinely different in 1800 — and what wasn't?
- **Brief:** The RP3/Continuity-and-Change wrap: political transformation was real (republican government, expanded — for white men — political participation, disestablishment); social and economic continuities ran deep (slavery intact and growing in the South, women excluded, Native dispossession accelerating). Models the exam's continuity-and-change thesis skill explicitly: change *within* continuity, with a defensible claim and evidence on both sides.
- **Visuals:** change/continuity two-column animation; callbacks to vid-u3-01 and vid-u3-06.

## Unit 4 — 1800–1848: Growth and Division (12 videos)

### Chapter 4.1 — The Young Republic

#### vid-u4-01 — "The Republic Grows Up"
- **Topics:** 4.1 · **Length:** 6 min
- **Narrative question:** What kind of country was the United States in 1800 — and what was about to hit it?
- **Brief:** The context opener: a republic of ~5 million people, overwhelmingly rural, already stretching to the Mississippi, with the "Revolution of 1800" transferring power peacefully between parties. Establishes the period's twin engines — territorial/economic expansion and democratization — and the fault line under both: slavery. Continuity bridge from vid-u3-12.
- **Visuals:** 1800 U.S. map; population counter_scene; timeline 1800–1848.

#### vid-u4-02 — "Jefferson's America"
- **Topics:** 4.2 · **Length:** 7 min
- **Narrative question:** Could a president who distrusted government double the country?
- **Brief:** The election of 1800 and Jefferson's "revolution" (shrinking government, cutting taxes and the military), the Louisiana Purchase (1803) — a constitutional stretch by a strict constructionist — and Lewis and Clark's expedition. The video's tension: Jeffersonian ideals vs. Jeffersonian actions, with the Embargo Act (1807) as the case study in backfiring principle.
- **Visuals:** Louisiana Purchase map animation (territory shading); Lewis & Clark route; Jefferson portrait (PD).

#### vid-u4-03 — "The Court Builds a Nation"
- **Topics:** 4.3 · **Length:** 7 min
- **Narrative question:** How did an unelected court become the most powerful branch of government?
- **Brief:** John Marshall's Supreme Court nationalized power: *Marbury v. Madison* (1803, judicial review), *McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819, implied powers), *Gibbons v. Ogden* (1824, interstate commerce). Set against the Era of Good Feelings and rising sectionalism (Missouri Compromise, 1820), the video shows national vs. regional interests fighting through court cases. **Remember-this flag:** Marshall's cases are the exam's favorite Period 4 evidence.
- **Visuals:** court-decision flow diagrams; Missouri Compromise map (36°30′ line); Marshall portrait (PD).

#### vid-u4-04 — "America's Second War for Independence"
- **Topics:** 4.4 · **Length:** 7 min
- **Narrative question:** Why did Americans call a stalemate a victory — and what did the war actually win?
- **Brief:** The War of 1812: impressment and trade restrictions as causes, the invasion of Canada, the burning of Washington, Andrew Jackson at New Orleans — and the Treaty of Ghent changing almost nothing on paper. The real product was psychological: nationalism surged, the Federalist Party died at Hartford, and the Monroe Doctrine (1823) soon declared the hemisphere off-limits to Europe. Counterfactual cold open: what if Britain had pressed its advantage in 1814?
- **Visuals:** animated war map; "Star-Spangled Banner" manuscript (PD); Monroe Doctrine hemisphere map.

### Chapter 4.2 — The Market Revolution

#### vid-u4-05 — "The Machine Age Arrives"
- **Topics:** 4.5 · **Length:** 8 min
- **Narrative question:** How did canals and railroads remake where Americans lived and worked before the Civil War?
- **Brief:** The transportation revolution (Erie Canal 1825, railroads, steamboats) and communication revolution (telegraph) collapsed distance; the Lowell mills pioneered factory production with young women's labor; interchangeable parts pointed toward mass production. Counter_scene beats (canal freight costs, miles of track) make the scale visceral. Significance landing: a national market economy replaced local subsistence — and created winners and losers.
- **Visuals:** animated transport map (canals/railroads spreading); Lowell mill engraving (PD); cost/time split_scene (1800 vs. 1850 travel).

#### vid-u4-06 — "Who Won the Market Revolution?"
- **Topics:** 4.6 · **Length:** 7 min
- **Narrative question:** The economy boomed — so why were so many people angry?
- **Brief:** The social side: a new middle class of clerks and professionals, the separation of home and workplace (and the "cult of domesticity"), Irish and German immigration swelling cities, and the first labor unions and strikes. The video keeps score by group — merchants and manufacturers up, artisans and mill girls squeezed — because the exam tests the Market Revolution's *differential* effects, not just its existence.
- **Visuals:** class-structure diagram; immigration chart_scene; tenement/rowhouse images (PD).

### Chapter 4.3 — Jacksonian Democracy

#### vid-u4-07 — "Democracy for (White) Men"
- **Topics:** 4.7 · **Length:** 7 min
- **Narrative question:** Was Jacksonian democracy a triumph of the common man or a triumph for only some men?
- **Brief:** The "corrupt bargain" of 1824, the expansion of white male suffrage (property requirements dropped), the new mass party machinery (nominating conventions, campaign hoopla) — and the exclusions: women, free Blacks (disenfranchised in new state constitutions), and Native peoples. The nullification crisis (1832–33) tests how democratic the union really was when South Carolina defied federal tariffs.
- **Visuals:** suffrage-expansion chart by state; nullification timeline; campaign artifact images (PD).

#### vid-u4-08 — "King Andrew"
- **Topics:** 4.8 · **Length:** 8 min
- **Narrative question:** Was Andrew Jackson the people's champion or the presidency's first tyrant?
- **Brief:** The Bank War (vetoing the Second Bank's recharter, "pet banks"), the spoils system, and — the moral center — Indian Removal: the Trail of Tears (1838–39) carried out despite *Worcester v. Georgia* (1832). The video takes the playbook's stance rule seriously: it renders a verdict (expansion of presidential power, catastrophe for Native nations) while using the "historians argue" beat on Jackson's economic legacy. Callbacks to vid-u4-03's Marshall Court.
- **Visuals:** Trail of Tears animated route map; Bank War political cartoons (PD); veto message doc_zoom.

### Chapter 4.4 — Culture and Reform

#### vid-u4-09 — "Inventing American Culture"
- **Topics:** 4.9 · **Length:** 6 min
- **Narrative question:** When did Americans stop copying Europe and start sounding like themselves?
- **Brief:** The Hudson River School painted an American landscape, Irving and Cooper wrote American stories, transcendentalists (Emerson, Thoreau) preached self-reliance — all wrapped in the nationalism the War of 1812 had inflamed. Shorter by design: this is the corpus's most consistently covered topic, so the video moves fast and spends its energy on the significance landing (culture as nation-building).
- **Visuals:** Hudson River School paintings (PD); author portrait montage; transcendentalist quotes kinetic_text.

#### vid-u4-10 — "The Reform Generation"
- **Topics:** 4.10, 4.11 · **Length:** 8 min
- **Narrative question:** Why did a religious revival produce abolitionists, feminists, and teetotalers?
- **Brief:** The Second Great Awakening (Finney's revivals, the Burned-Over District) taught that individuals could perfect themselves — and society. That perfectionism powered the reform wave: temperance, public schools (Horace Mann), asylums (Dorothea Dix), abolition (Garrison's *Liberator*, 1831; Frederick Douglass's voice, PD), and women's rights (Seneca Falls, 1848). The video's causal chain — revival → moral urgency → organized reform — is the exam's Period 4 spine.
- **Visuals:** revival camp-meeting engraving (PD); reform-movement timeline; doc_zoom (Douglass excerpt, PD; Seneca Falls Declaration, PD).

#### vid-u4-11 — "Two Souths"
- **Topics:** 4.12, 4.13 · **Length:** 8 min
- **Narrative question:** What was life actually like in the South — for the enslaved majority and everyone else?
- **Brief:** The cotton South: the cotton gin's explosive effect, the "peculiar institution" defended as a "positive good," the planter aristocracy atop a society of yeomen, poor whites — and the enslaved, whose resistance ranged from work slowdowns to Nat Turner's rebellion (1831). Alongside it, free Black communities in North and South building churches, schools, and businesses under constant threat. **Remember-this flag:** the exam contrasts the *defense* of slavery with the *reality* of enslaved life.
- **Visuals:** cotton production counter_scene; plantation/slave-quarter split_scene; Douglass narrative doc_zoom (PD).

#### vid-u4-12 — "Why the Sections Split"
- **Topics:** 4.14 · RP2 · **Length:** 7 min
- **Narrative question:** Was the Civil War already inevitable by 1848?
- **Brief:** The RP2/Causation wrap: weighing the period's accumulating sectional pressures — the Market Revolution's divergent economies, the Missouri Compromise's fragile line, abolitionism's moral assault, and the West as the arena where slavery's expansion would be decided. Explicitly models multi-cause analysis (long-term vs. immediate causes) and pushes back on single-cause answers — the exam rewards weighing, not listing.
- **Visuals:** causation-weight diagram; callbacks to vid-u4-05, vid-u4-10, vid-u4-11.

## Unit 5 — 1844–1877: Civil War and Reconstruction (12 videos)

### Chapter 5.1 — The Road to War

#### vid-u5-01 — "A Nation Pulling Apart"
- **Topics:** 5.1 · **Length:** 6 min
