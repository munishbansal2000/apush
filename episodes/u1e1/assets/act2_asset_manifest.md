## Beat 1 — Cahokia site (urban scale, 10,000–20,000 people)

### Option A (RECOMMENDED): Herb Roe artist's reconstruction
- **URL:** https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/26/Cahokia_Aerial_HRoe_2015.jpg
- **File page:** https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cahokia_Aerial_HRoe_2015.jpg
- **Source:** Wikimedia Commons / Southern Illinois University SCRC Virtual Museum
- **Creator:** Herb Roe (2015)
- **License:** CC BY-SA 4.0 (attribution required: "Herb Roe")
- **Depicts:** Artist's conception of Cahokia at its height — Monks Mound at center, Grand Plaza to the south, palisade-enclosed central precinct, three surrounding plazas, Woodhenge cedar-post circle to the west
- **Period represented:** ~1050–1150 CE (Cahokia's peak)
- **Anachronism warning:** 2015 reconstruction, not a period artifact. Shows the city as archaeologists believe it looked — appropriate as a reconstruction, not evidence.
- **Fit:** Best single image for "10,000–20,000 people, urban center" — shows the full city layout and scale.

### Option B: NARA aerial photograph of Monks Mound
- **URL:** https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/67/Great_River_Road_-_An_Aerial_View_of_Monks_Mound_-_NARA_-_7718947.jpg
- **File page:** https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Great_River_Road_-_An_Aerial_View_of_Monks_Mound_-_NARA_-_7718947.jpg
- **Source:** U.S. National Archives and Records Administration (NARA), via Wikimedia Commons
- **License:** Public domain (U.S. federal government work)
- **Depicts:** Modern aerial photograph revealing the scale of Monks Mound on the landscape
- **Period represented:** Modern photograph of the surviving earthwork
- **Anachronism warning:** Modern landscape (highways, fields visible). Shows what survives, not the living city.
- **Fit:** Good for "astonishingly large" scale beat; pairs well with Option A (reconstruction + surviving reality).

## Beat 2 — Monk's Mound (earthen pyramid, ~100 ft tall)

### Option A (RECOMMENDED): Ground-level public-domain photo
- **URL:** https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/76/Cahokia_Monks_Mound.jpg
- **File page:** https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cahokia_Monks_Mound.jpg
- **Source:** Wikimedia Commons (Wikivoyage contributor Ethajek)
- **License:** Public domain
- **Depicts:** Monk's Mound from ground level, showing its stepped-terrace profile
- **Period represented:** Modern photograph of the surviving mound (built in 14 stages, ~900–1200 CE)
- **Anachronism warning:** Modern photo; the mound today is grass-covered and eroded. The 100-ft height and four terraces are still visible.
- **Fit:** Clean ground view showing the pyramid form. No attribution needed.

- **URL:** https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b4/Monk%27s_mound_panorama.jpg
- **File page:** https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Monk%27s_mound_panorama.jpg
- **Source:** Wikimedia Commons
- **License:** Public domain
- **Depicts:** Panoramic view of Monk's Mound, the pre-Columbian earthwork at Cahokia near Collinsville, Illinois
- **Period represented:** Modern photograph
- **Anachronism warning:** Same as Option A — modern landscape, grass-covered mound.
- **Fit:** Wider framing; useful if the scene needs landscape context.

### Option C: NARA aerial (shared with Beat 1)
- Same as Beat 1 Option B. The aerial best conveys "largest earthen structure north of Mexico" — 6 hectares, 4 terraces, 30m/100ft tall.

## Beat 3 — Turquoise trade (Southwest mining → continental networks)

### Option A (RECOMMENDED): Chacoan turquoise inlay pieces
- **URL:** https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/5a/Chacoan_turquoise_with_argillite.jpg
- **File page:** https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Chacoan_turquoise_with_argillite.jpg
- **Source:** U.S. National Park Service, Chaco Culture National Historical Park museum collection (via Wikimedia Commons)
- **License:** Public domain (PD-USGov-Interior-NPS)
- **Depicts:** Ancestral Puebloan (Anasazi) turquoise and argillite (orange) inlay pieces from Pueblo Alto, Chaco Canyon. Catalog CHCU 31076.
- **Period represented:** circa 1020–1140 CE — exactly contemporary with Cahokia's rise
- **Anachronism warning:** None. This is a period artifact from the right century and the right trade-network origin point (Chaco was the turquoise redistribution center).
- **Fit:** Perfect for "turquoise mined in the Southwest travels through networks" — genuine Chacoan turquoise from the peak era.

### Option B: Hohokam turquoise mosaics
- **URL:** https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/41/Hohokam_turquoise_mosaics.jpg
- **File page:** https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hohokam_turquoise_mosaics.jpg
- **Source:** National Park Service, found 1925 at Casa Grande Ruins National Monument, Arizona (via Wikimedia Commons)
- **License:** Public domain (NPS)
- **Depicts:** Pre-Columbian Hohokam culture turquoise mosaic work
- **Period represented:** Hohokam Classic period (~1150–1450 CE); the culture spans ~450–1450 CE
- **Anachronism warning:** Hohokam, not Ancestral Puebloan — different Southwestern culture, slightly later than Cahokia's peak. Still a legitimate pre-Columbian turquoise artifact showing the craft tradition.
- **Fit:** Good second option showing finished mosaic work; use when the narration is about turquoise craftsmanship rather than the Chaco network specifically.

## Beat 4 — Shell trade (Gulf Coast shell → Mississippian interior)

### Option A (RECOMMENDED): Fain's Island shell gorget (period engraving)
- **URL:** https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/06/Fain%27s_Island_shell_gorget.png
- **File page:** https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Fain%27s_Island_shell_gorget.png
- **Source:** Wikimedia Commons; engraving by Stephen D. Peet (1831–1914), 19th-century archaeological illustration
- **License:** Public domain (pre-1929 publication)
- **Depicts:** Mississippian culture engraved shell gorget from the Fain's Island Site (40JE1), Jefferson County, Tennessee — marine shell (Gulf Coast/Atlantic) engraved with Mississippian iconography, found hundreds of miles inland
- **Period represented:** Artifact is Mississippian (~1200–1400 CE); illustration is 19th century
- **Anachronism warning:** It is an engraving, not a photograph — the artifact rendering is accurate but stylized in 19th-century archaeological-illustration style. The artifact itself is genuine and the find-spot (Tennessee) proves the inland trade point.
- **Fit:** Excellent for "Gulf Coast shell moves inland" — the Tennessee find-spot IS the trade evidence.

### Option B: Met Museum shell gorget (photograph)
- **URL:** https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/99/Gorget_MET_DT258619.jpg
- **File page:** https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gorget_MET_DT258619.jpg
- **Source:** Metropolitan Museum of Art (Open Access), via Wikimedia Commons
- **License:** CC0 (Met Open Access — no restrictions)
- **Depicts:** Mississippian shell gorget/ornament, photographed as a museum object
- **Period represented:** Mississippian (~1000–1400 CE)
- **Anachronism warning:** None — genuine artifact, museum photograph.
- **Fit:** Clean object photo; use when the scene needs a photographic artifact rather than an engraving.

### Option C: Herb Roe gorget drawing (use with caution)
- **URL:** https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/40/Lick_Creek_style_gorget_Hairston_Site_HRoe_2012.jpg
- **Source:** Wikimedia Commons, illustration by Herb Roe (2012)
- **License:** CC BY-SA 3.0 (attribution required)
- **Depicts:** Mississippian Lick Creek style herpetomorphic shell gorget, Hairston Site, North Carolina
- **Period represented:** Early Saratown Phase, 1450–1600 CE
- **⚠️ Anachronism warning:** 1450–1600 CE — THREE CENTURIES after Cahokia's peak. Do not use for the ~1100 CE trade-network beat without noting the date. Included only as a stylistic option; prefer A or B.
- **Fit:** Same illustrator as the Cahokia reconstruction (visual consistency), but the date is wrong for Act 2. Not recommended unless the narration is about the broader Mississippian tradition.

## Beat 5 — Maize/teosinte (domestication over thousands of years)

### Option A (RECOMMENDED): Doebley teosinte–maize comparison
- **URL:** https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/cb/Maize-teosinte.jpg
- **File page:** https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Maize-teosinte.jpg
- **Source:** Wikimedia Commons; photograph by John Doebley (University of Wisconsin, maize geneticist)
- **License:** CC BY 3.0 (attribution required: "John Doebley")
- **Depicts:** Side-by-side comparison: teosinte (wild ancestor) → maize-teosinte hybrid → domesticated maize. Shows the dramatic morphological transformation from a grassy plant with tiny hard kernels to the large-eared crop.
- **Period represented:** Modern scientific photograph illustrating a ~9,000-year domestication process (teosinte domesticated in the Balsas Valley, Mexico, ~9000 BP; reached the Eastern Woodlands ~2000 BP)
- **Anachronism warning:** Modern lab/greenhouse photo, not a period artifact. It illustrates the biological process, not a historical moment.
- **Fit:** THE standard textbook image for "maize domesticated from grass over thousands of years." Instantly legible: grass → corn.

### Option B: USDA teosinte botanical illustration
- **URL:** https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/9d/Zea_mexicana_%28Schrad.%29_Kuntze_-_Mexican_teosinte_-_ZEME_-_Hitchcock%2C_A.S._%28rev._A._Chase%29._1950_-_USDA.tif
- **File page:** https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Zea_mexicana_(Schrad.)_Kuntze_-_Mexican_teosinte_-_ZEME_-_Hitchcock%2C_A.S._(rev._A._Chase)._1950_-_USDA.tif
- **Source:** U.S. Department of Agriculture (Hitchcock, rev. Chase, 1950), via Wikimedia Commons
- **License:** Public domain (U.S. federal government work)
- **Depicts:** Botanical illustration of Mexican teosinte (Zea mexicana), the wild ancestor of maize
- **Period represented:** 1950 scientific illustration of the wild species
- **Anachronism warning:** Mid-20th-century scientific plate; shows the wild ancestor only, not the domesticated form.
- **Fit:** Good as a "wild grass ancestor" close-up; pairs with a maize image for the before/after. Note: downloads as .tif — convert to PNG/JPG for the pipeline.
