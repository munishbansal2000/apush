# U3-L3 INTERVIEW SCRIPT (Fish Audio) — Maya (host) + Marcus (expert)
# Same verified facts. Interview dynamics. Marcus keeps it short and punchy.

Maya: (energetic) Okay, so here's a paradox I need you to explain. In 1763, Britain wins the biggest war of the century... and goes broke doing it. The national debt doubles, the empire's bigger than ever, and the treasury is empty. So what do they do?
Marcus: (dry) They send the bill to thirteen colonies who never got a vote.
Maya: (laughs) That's the whole American Revolution in one sentence, isn't it?
Marcus: Pretty much. Taxation without representation. That's the episode.

Maya: Alright, but before the taxes, things were actually fine for a while. What changed?
Marcus: Britain mostly left the colonies alone. Historians call it salutary neglect. London passed trade laws, then looked the other way.
Maya: (playful) The "I'm not mad, I just won't check" school of parenting.
Marcus: (chuckles) Exactly. Colonies ran their own affairs, taxed themselves. Everybody happy. Until 1763 — Britain draws a line down the Appalachians, says no settlement past it. War's over. The old arrangement? Finished.

Maya: Okay, here's the part I really want to understand. Parliament says "we can tax you." The colonists say "no you can't." What was Britain's actual argument?
Marcus: Virtual representation. Most people in Britain couldn't vote either. But Parliament spoke for the whole empire — so it could tax the whole empire.
Maya: Hmm. That's annoyingly reasonable-sounding?
Marcus: Until the colonists answer: only our own elected assemblies can tax us, because only they answer to us. Actual representation, or nothing.
Maya: (dramatic) And just like that, it's not about money anymore.
Marcus: It's about who rules. That's the whole ballgame.

Maya: So the taxes start coming. Walk me through the escalation.
Marcus: 1764: Sugar Act. First law actually meant to raise revenue, not just regulate trade. 1765: Stamp Act. Direct tax on nearly every piece of paper.
Maya: Including playing cards? I read that somewhere.
Marcus: (laughs) Playing cards and dice. Couldn't shuffle a deck without paying the king.
Maya: Okay, that's amazing. So people lose it, obviously.
Marcus: Sons of Liberty form. Nine colonies send delegates to the Stamp Act Congress. Parliament caves — repeals it in 1766.
Maya: Victory!
Marcus: Same day, they pass the Declaratory Act. Claims power over the colonies in all cases whatsoever.
Maya: (sarcastic) So they dropped the tax and kept the principle.
Marcus: Classic. It was never really about the money.

Maya: It doesn't stop though. Townshend Acts, 1767?
Marcus: Duties on glass, lead, paint, paper, tea. Plus customs boards and juryless courts to squeeze every penny.
Maya: And the boycotts come roaring back?
Marcus: Bigger. Daughters of Liberty spin homespun cloth so families can ditch British textiles. Parliament repeals most duties in 1770 — keeps the tea tax, just to prove the point.
Maya: Petty.
Marcus: Very. Same year, March 1770 — British soldiers fire into a Boston crowd. Five dead.
Maya: (serious) The Boston Massacre.
Marcus: Paul Revere's engraving turns it into propaganda overnight. In a war of ideas, that's a weapon.

Maya: Now the part I love. 1773, the Tea Act. And the twist is...
Marcus: The tea got cheaper.
Maya: (incredulous) Wait — cheaper? And they destroyed it anyway?
Marcus: (laughs) 342 chests, into Boston Harbor, December 16, 1773. Some of them dressed as Mohawks.
Maya: Because buying it meant accepting Parliament's right to tax them.
Marcus: Principle beat price. That's the line to remember.

Maya: Parliament must have been furious.
Marcus: Coercive Acts. Colonists called them the Intolerable Acts. Harbor closed till the tea's paid for. Massachusetts loses self-government.
Maya: The plan was to isolate the radicals, right?
Marcus: Did the opposite. Twelve colonies send delegates to the First Continental Congress — Philadelphia, September 1774.
Maya: So the chain is: war debt, taxes, resistance, harsher measures, wider resistance.
Marcus: Every single time. That spiral runs straight to Lexington and Concord.
Maya: (warm) And that's the pattern to carry into the Revolution. Thanks for this — genuinely one of my favorite stories in all of APUSH.
Marcus: (chuckles) Wait till the Revolution actually starts.
