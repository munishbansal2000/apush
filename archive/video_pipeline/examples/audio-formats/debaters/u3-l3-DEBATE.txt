# U3-L3 DEBATE SCRIPT (Fish Audio) — Maya moderates, Loyalist vs Patriot
# Same verified facts. Both sides at full strength.

Maya: (energetic) Welcome to the debate. Tonight's question: was Parliament justified in taxing the American colonies? Arguing for the Crown, our Loyalist. Arguing for the colonies, our Patriot. Keep it clean, keep it sharp. Loyalist, open.
Loyalist: (measured) Thank you. Let us begin with arithmetic. In 1763, Britain had just won the greatest war of the century — and the national debt had doubled. The empire was larger than ever. The treasury was empty. The thirteen colonies had contributed barely a fraction to their own defense. Asking them to help pay was not tyranny. It was fairness.
Patriot: (passionate) Fairness? We had no vote in the Parliament that taxed us! For decades, Britain left us alone — salutary neglect, they call it. We ran our own affairs, taxed ourselves. Then in 1763, London draws a line down the Appalachians, tells us where we can live, and starts reaching into our pockets. That's not fairness. That's a power grab.
Loyalist: (calm) The colonists speak of representation. Parliament's answer was virtual representation. Most subjects in Britain itself cannot vote — yet Parliament legislates for the entire empire. The colonists are represented in exactly the same way as a shopkeeper in Leeds.
Patriot: (fierce) A shopkeeper in Leeds lives under Parliament's laws every day! We are three thousand miles away, governed by assemblies we elect ourselves. Only our own assemblies can tax us, because only they answer to us. Actual representation — or nothing.
Maya: (intrigued) Strong clash. Let's get specific. The Sugar Act, 1764, then the Stamp Act, 1765. Loyalist?
Loyalist: (reasonable) The Sugar Act was the first law genuinely meant to raise colonial revenue — modest, necessary. The Stamp Act asked colonists to pay a small duty on paper — legal documents, newspapers. Even playing cards. And when the colonies protested, what did Parliament do? It listened. It repealed the Stamp Act in 1766.
Patriot: (sarcastic) Oh, it listened. It repealed the Stamp Act on the same day it passed the Declaratory Act — claiming power over us "in all cases whatsoever." They gave up the tax and kept the principle! And the Sons of Liberty didn't form for fun. Nine colonies sent delegates to the Stamp Act Congress because the principle was everything.
Loyalist: (firm) Principle cuts both ways. The Townshend Acts of 1767 placed duties on glass, lead, paint, paper, and tea — external taxes, the kind Parliament had always levied. The boycotts that followed were economic warfare against the mother country.
Patriot: (heated) Economic warfare? The Daughters of Liberty spun their own cloth so families wouldn't buy British textiles — that's peaceful resistance! And how did Britain answer colonial protest? March 1770. British soldiers fired into a Boston crowd. Five colonists dead.
Loyalist: (cool) A tragic clash, provoked by a mob. And Paul Revere's engraving turned it into propaganda — the famous "massacre" was street theater turned into a weapon.
Patriot: (triumphant) Propaganda wins wars of ideas! But here's my favorite part of your case, friend. 1773: the Tea Act. Parliament gives the East India Company a monopoly — and the tea gets cheaper. Cheaper! And we destroyed it anyway. 342 chests, into Boston Harbor, December 16th. Because buying it meant accepting Parliament's right to tax us. Principle beat price.
Loyalist: (stern) You destroyed private property over a tax cut. Parliament answered with the Coercive Acts — closing Boston's harbor until the tea was paid for, restoring order in Massachusetts. Firm, lawful, necessary.
Patriot: (fierce) The colonists called them the Intolerable Acts — and your "firm" response did the exact opposite of what London wanted. Twelve colonies sent delegates to the First Continental Congress in Philadelphia, September 1774. You tried to isolate Massachusetts. You united America instead.
Maya: (dramatic) Final word each. Thirty seconds.
Loyalist: (measured) War debt, then fair taxes, then ingratitude met with lawful order. Britain asked its colonies to behave like subjects of the same empire.
Patriot: (passionate) War debt, then taxes without consent, then resistance, then harsher measures, then wider resistance. Every crackdown made the rebellion bigger. That spiral ends at Lexington and Concord.
Maya: (warm) There it is — the same chain of events, two completely different stories. Who won? That's your call. And that's the pattern to carry into the Revolution.
